# Auto-Scaling in Cloud Computing: Technology Classification, Challenges, and Research Solutions

Auto-scaling is the automated adjustment of computing capacity in response to changes in workload, application performance, or resource demand. Depending on the platform, the actuator may change the number of replicas or virtual machines (horizontal scaling), change the resources assigned to an individual instance (vertical scaling), or change the capacity of the underlying node pool. For example, Kubernetes exposes Horizontal Pod Autoscaling, Vertical Pod Autoscaling, and node autoscaling, while Amazon EC2 Auto Scaling adjusts the capacity of Auto Scaling groups according to policies, schedules, health conditions, and demand. [1]

The most useful high-level classification is indeed reactive versus proactive, but the distinction should be understood as a classification of decision timing, rather than a mutually exclusive classification of algorithms. A reactive controller decides from the current or recently observed state; a proactive controller provisions resources on the basis of a schedule, forecast, or forward-looking policy before the anticipated shortage occurs. Machine learning, optimization, or control theory can therefore appear on either side depending on how they are used. Production platforms increasingly combine the two: AWS predictive scaling, for example, forecasts capacity increases but deliberately does not perform predictive scale-in; removing excess capacity is left to target-tracking or step-scaling policies operating on real-time measurements. [2]

## Auto-Scaling Technology Classification

### Reactive Methods

Reactive auto-scaling forms a closed loop of monitor → evaluate → scale → observe again. It is attractive because decisions are grounded in measurements rather than uncertain future forecasts, but that also means it normally cannot begin corrective action until a workload change has already become observable. The practical consequence is that monitoring intervals, detection delays, instance or pod initialization times, and stabilization mechanisms become part of the control problem. [3]

| Technical method | Core working principle | Main advantages | Main limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Threshold- and rule-based scaling | A monitored metric—CPU utilization, memory, queue length, request rate, latency, or a custom metric—is compared with one or more predefined thresholds. Crossing a threshold triggers a predetermined scale-out or scale-in action. AWS step scaling, for example, changes capacity in predefined increments after a CloudWatch alarm breaches a threshold. Kubernetes HPA follows the same general feedback idea and, in its basic form, computes desired replicas approximately as ceil(current replicas × current metric / desired metric). [4] | Simple to implement and explain; low decision-making overhead; deterministic behavior facilitates debugging and operational governance; already built into mainstream cloud and orchestration platforms. Different threshold ranges can also trigger different scaling magnitudes through step policies. [4] | The action begins after the metric has changed, so rapid load surges may create temporary overload before new capacity is ready. Selecting thresholds is workload-specific: a low threshold wastes resources, whereas an aggressive high threshold leaves less safety margin. Scale-out initialization delay and repeated threshold crossings can also produce lag or oscillatory behavior unless stabilization/warm-up mechanisms are used. Experiments in Graph-PHPA illustrate the resource-versus-overload trade-off produced by different Kubernetes CPU thresholds. [5] |
| Target-tracking and feedback-control scaling | Instead of defining discrete rules for every range, the controller continuously attempts to keep a metric near a desired set point—for example, average CPU utilization around 50%. Commercial target tracking is analogous to thermostat control. More sophisticated academic systems use PID-like, adaptive, fuzzy, or multi-input/multi-output control. The EuroSys AutoControl work, for example, combined an online model estimator with a MIMO resource controller to regulate multiple virtualized resources. [6] | Removes much manual rule construction, responds continuously to deviations, and naturally supports closed-loop correction when workload conditions change. Advanced MIMO controllers can explicitly represent interactions among several resources instead of treating CPU, memory, I/O, and application performance as unrelated quantities. [6] | Performance depends on controller tuning, measurement quality, and the dynamics of the application and actuator. Long provisioning delays, nonlinear resource/performance relationships, workload regime changes, or inaccurate online models can make a controller react too slowly or overshoot. Target metrics must also scale meaningfully with capacity; AWS explicitly recommends choosing metrics and targets that correspond appropriately to resource demand and leaving a buffer for unexpected traffic. [6] |
| Analytical- and queueing-model-based scaling | Current arrival rate, service rate, queue length, tier utilization, or response time is fed into an analytical performance model. The system estimates how many servers or replicas are required to satisfy a latency, throughput, or utilization objective and then changes capacity accordingly. Urgaonkar and colleagues' work on agile provisioning of multi-tier Internet applications is an early example built around queueing models; related VNF work models servers and requests explicitly as queues. [7] | More directly connects resource quantity to application-level performance than a raw CPU threshold. It can encode service-level objectives and, in multi-tier applications, reason about which tier is actually limiting performance. Analytical models can also evaluate candidate configurations without repeatedly experimenting on a live production system. [7] | The model is only as good as its assumptions and calibration. Real applications can violate assumptions about service-time distributions, independence, stationarity, bottlenecks, or network effects. Detailed models also become harder to construct and update as systems evolve from a few tiers to large microservice dependency graphs. DASA, for example, had to model setup time and legacy fixed capacity explicitly because conventional cloud models omitted important telecom-specific dynamics. [8] |

The three families are often combined. A practical controller might use a queueing model to estimate required capacity, target tracking for routine correction, and hard thresholds as emergency guardrails. This layering is valuable because reactive methods differ in what they optimize: threshold methods emphasize simplicity, feedback control emphasizes regulation and stability, and analytical methods emphasize an explicit relationship between load, capacity, and service-level performance. [9]

### Proactive Methods

Proactive auto-scaling attempts to answer a different question: What capacity will be needed when newly provisioned resources actually become usable? This is particularly valuable where initialization or state-transfer time is non-negligible. Roy, Dubey, and Gokhale's predictive autoscaling study explicitly identified VM acquisition, booting, application startup, and state changes as reasons to make resource decisions ahead of the workload rather than after it arrives. [10]

| Technical method | Core working principle | Main advantages | Main limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scheduled scaling | Capacity changes are programmed for known future times rather than triggered by observed utilization. A scheduled action may be one-time or recurring and may specify desired, minimum, and maximum capacity. AWS, for example, supports recurring or one-time scheduled Auto Scaling actions. [11] | Extremely simple and computationally inexpensive; no forecast model or training data is required. It works particularly well for known events such as daily business opening, scheduled batch processing, planned promotions, or regularly recurring traffic peaks, and capacity can be made ready before demand starts. [12] | It cannot adapt by itself to unexpected bursts, changing event times, or workload amplitudes that differ from the schedule. It therefore works best when timing and required capacity are known with relatively high confidence, rather than as a general solution to stochastic demand. [13] |
| Statistical and time-series forecasting | Historical measurements are transformed into a forecast using signal-processing or statistical models such as moving averages, exponential smoothing, autoregression, ARMA/ARIMA-like models, Fourier/periodicity analysis, or combinations of these. PRESS extracts fine-grained demand patterns using lightweight signal processing and statistical learning, while Roy et al. incorporated ARMA-style workload prediction into a predictive autoscaling controller. [14] | Generally lighter-weight and more interpretable than deep models; training and inference costs are modest; particularly effective for workloads with autocorrelation, seasonality, or repeating daily/weekly patterns. PRESS demonstrated that relatively lightweight online techniques can translate forecasting into lower over-provisioning and fewer SLO violations. [15] | Accuracy deteriorates when future demand is not represented by historical structure—for example, abrupt regime changes or unique events. This limitation is visible in commercial predictive autoscaling as well: AWS describes its mechanism as detecting recurring daily/weekly patterns, and recommends evaluating forecasts before allowing them to control capacity. [16] |
| Machine-learning and deep-learning forecasting | A supervised model learns a nonlinear mapping from historical workload and system measurements to future workload or resource demand. Models include regression, neural networks, LSTMs, transformers, and graph neural networks. Graph-PHPA, for example, uses an LSTM to forecast next-step microservice workload and a GCN to infer resource demand while accounting for dependencies among microservices, then translates predicted vCPU demand into pod counts before the next interval. [17] | Can capture nonlinear temporal relationships, interactions among multiple metrics, and—for graph-based models—dependencies among services that a univariate time series cannot represent. In Graph-PHPA's experiments, the predictive approach was able to provision pods in advance and showed resource savings relative to the Kubernetes HPA baseline. [17] | Requires representative training data, feature engineering/model selection, retraining as workload-resource relationships drift, and additional model-serving infrastructure. Prediction uncertainty remains consequential because an underestimate risks SLO violation while an overestimate creates cost. Individual models also have scope limitations: Graph-PHPA's authors note that their evaluated model concentrated on vCPU prediction using workload as the principal model attribute. [17] |
| Model Predictive Control and forecast-driven optimization | A workload forecast is combined with an application or cost model, and the controller searches over a finite future horizon for a sequence of resource configurations that minimizes an objective such as infrastructure cost + SLO penalties + scaling/reconfiguration cost. Only the immediate decision is applied; the optimization is repeated as new observations arrive. Roy et al.'s IEEE CLOUD work is a representative formulation, using a predictive look-ahead model and explicitly accounting for leasing and configuration-change costs as well as performance. [18] | More expressive than simply “forecast workload and map it to replicas.” The optimizer can explicitly incorporate boot delay, reconfiguration penalties, resource price, SLO penalties, capacity constraints, and a trade-off between present and future states. Receding-horizon recomputation also lets the policy correct itself when new observations differ from old forecasts. [10] | Requires both sufficiently accurate prediction and a sufficiently accurate performance/cost model. The planning horizon and objective weights affect the resulting policy, and larger configuration spaces make online optimization more computationally demanding. Incorrect forecasts can still cause the optimizer to reserve too much or too little capacity. [10] |
| Reinforcement-learning and learned-policy scaling | The autoscaler treats resource management as a sequential decision problem. A state may contain workload, utilization, queueing and SLO information; actions correspond to scaling or resource-limit decisions; and a reward penalizes resource cost and SLO violations. Through interaction or transferred experience, the agent learns a policy that maximizes long-run reward rather than obeying hand-written thresholds. AWARE is a production-oriented example using meta-learning and bootstrapping to adapt RL autoscaling agents to different workloads while making exploration safer. [19] | Can optimize long-term consequences and multiple competing objectives without requiring a manually derived analytical model. It is attractive where workload/resource relationships and interactions are too complex for simple rules. AWARE's experiments show that policy transfer and bootstrapping can materially accelerate adaptation while improving resource utilization during training. [19] | RL creates a particularly difficult production problem because bad exploratory actions can violate an SLO, training may require substantial interaction, and a policy trained on one workload may not generalize to another. The motivation for AWARE itself was the observed gap between RL autoscaling research and production deployment; its meta-learning and bootstrapping mechanisms specifically target workload adaptation and safe, robust exploration. [19] |

An important taxonomy caveat is that “machine-learning-based” does not automatically mean “proactive.” A classifier that observes the present state and chooses “scale up/down” is still reactive; an RL policy may likewise respond chiefly to its current state. These approaches become proactive when the decision incorporates predicted future demand, delayed effects, or long-horizon value. Conversely, simple scheduled scaling is proactive despite containing no machine learning. The robust production architecture is therefore usually hybrid: prediction handles expected demand early, while real-time reactive control handles forecast error and unexpected disturbances. AWS's predictive scaling design is an explicit industrial example: forecasts are allowed to add expected capacity, whereas scale-in is deferred until real-time policies confirm that actual demand has fallen. [2]

## Main Challenges and Solutions

### Energy Efficiency

**Objective.** Unused capacity can be removed or consolidated to save energy, but fewer active servers do not necessarily constitute a satisfactory energy policy. For a stateful service, the objective is lower energy use while preserving throughput, SLA guarantees, data consistency/reliability, and acceptable reconfiguration latency. Placement and replication therefore belong to the optimization problem, not just the server count. [20]

**Control actions.** Casalicchio, Lundberg, and Shirinbab's “Energy-aware auto-scaling algorithms for Cassandra virtual data centers,” Cluster Computing (2017), studies managed Cassandra virtual data centers. Opt jointly selects horizontal scaling, vertical scaling, and placement to seek a system-wide energy minimum. LocalOpt retains horizontal and vertical scaling through a lower-complexity local optimization; LocalOpt-H combines horizontal scaling with placement. The controller can enter the planning stage of a MAPE-K loop using measured throughput, CPU use, and memory use. [20]

**Constraints and reported limitations.** Consolidating replicas into a smaller failure domain can weaken fault tolerance. Nodes also need initialization and data/state redistribution during horizontal scale-out. The authors report that energy-efficient allocation can substantially reduce Cassandra's obtainable consistency/reliability level and that horizontal Cassandra scaling is too slow to comfortably handle sudden throughput surges. Vertical scaling can respond faster in some circumstances and complement horizontal scaling, but only where the infrastructure supports it. The study distinguishes exact optimization from lower-complexity heuristics; it does not make the energy objective a substitute for reliability or scaling-time constraints. [20]

**Applicable setting and implication.** For stateful services such as the studied Cassandra deployment, the short decision chain is: reduce energy → jointly adjust capacity and placement → retain reliability and reconfiguration-time constraints → apply within the service's stateful operating conditions. Resource utilization, replica placement, availability, state-movement cost, and the energy model must be considered together. This supports constrained optimization rather than a single “scale down whenever CPU is low” rule. [20]

### Dynamic Nature of Telecom Services

**Objective and setting.** Telecom traffic has daily peaks and low-utilization periods, potentially abrupt changes, and heterogeneous service classes. Provisioning physical equipment for the peak wastes capacity off-peak; too few virtual network functions risk latency and SLA violations. NFV elasticity must therefore balance VNF operating cost against user latency in a system that may combine costly, permanently available legacy equipment with dynamically created virtual capacity. Adding one VNF changes relative capacity differently across such deployments. [8]

**Control actions.** Ren, Phung-Duc, and Chen's “Design and Analysis of Dynamic Auto Scaling Algorithm (DASA) for virtual EPC (vEPC) in 5G Networks,” with a preliminary version presented at IEEE GLOBECOM 2016, models the service as a queueing system. Legacy equipment is a reserved block of always-on servers; VNF instances are powered on or off as outstanding jobs change. An analytical cost/performance model selects the active VNF count against an explicit latency constraint rather than a generic CPU threshold. [8]

**Constraints.** New VNF instances have non-zero startup time. Ren, Phung-Duc, and Chen identify the possibility that a requested VNF becomes available too late for the burst it was meant to serve. A controller must account for that delay and for both fixed legacy and elastic capacity when trading cost against latency; this is more involved than ordinary web-server autoscaling. [8]

**Reported result.** DASA includes VM/VNF setup behavior and a recursive computation for selecting capacity. Its reported simulations show that it can reduce operating cost subject to a latency upper bound. The analytical model lets operators examine cost/performance trade-offs without a full deployment. Thus the solution links cost reduction → queue-driven VNF activation/deactivation → setup and latency constraints → the modeled hybrid vEPC system. [8]

**Applicable setting and implication.** DASA illustrates telecom-aware analytical autoscaling: legacy capacity, VNF setup, queue dynamics, and the cost-latency objective are modeled together, rather than applying a conventional cloud threshold policy unchanged to NFV. More broadly, for 5G/NFV and later cloud-native network functions, relevant controller state includes session/request queues, per-function latency, instantiation time, service chains, fixed versus elastic network capacity, and operator cost policies—not CPU utilization alone. [8]

### Big Data Processing

Problem manifestation. Big-data analytics creates two coupled elasticity problems. First, required resources are difficult to estimate because interactive or exploratory users may issue very different ad-hoc queries in succession; a cluster size appropriate for one query may be inefficient for the next. Second, resizing the cluster itself can be expensive. Adding or removing workers can require data shuffling; pre-replicating data reduces future scaling latency but raises initial storage/setup cost; separating storage from compute can speed reconfiguration but can adversely affect query runtime or overall cost. [21]

These properties also make ordinary reactive control difficult. PerfEnforce's authors observed that query-time estimation errors could vary substantially—and in either direction—from one query to the next. Consequently, a feedback loop attempting to converge to one “correct” cluster size can be inappropriate because the correct resource configuration itself changes with each query. [21]

Published research example — Ortiz, Lee, Balazinska, and Hellerstein, “PerfEnforce: A Dynamic Scaling Engine for Analytics with Performance Guarantees” (2016). PerfEnforce was designed to select VM-cluster sizes that minimize monetary cost while probabilistically satisfying query-runtime SLAs. For the physical elasticity problem, the authors evaluate different data-organization strategies and conclude that careful data placement combined with partial replication provides a practical compromise between the reconfiguration delay of moving data and the cost of maintaining a fully replicated system. [21]

For the “when/how much to scale” problem, they compare feedback control, reinforcement learning, and perceptron learning. The perceptron continually updates a model of query runtime as queries execute, incorporates recent workload and system conditions, and selects a cluster size separately for each incoming query rather than waiting for a controller to converge to one persistent size. In their experiments, the perceptron approach provided better quality of service and cost effectiveness than the evaluated feedback-control and reinforcement-learning alternatives. [21]

Broader lesson. Autoscaling data-intensive applications cannot treat compute instances as stateless interchangeable units. The scaling policy must reason about data locality and redistribution cost together with compute capacity, and for highly heterogeneous analytics it may need to make fine-grained, query-specific decisions rather than maintain a single utilization target. [21]

### Cost-Effective Graph Processing

Problem manifestation. Large-scale graph analytics intensifies the big-data elasticity problem because many graph algorithms are iterative and repeatedly communicate along graph edges. Their performance therefore depends not only on aggregate CPU and memory but also on how vertices and edges are partitioned among workers. When worker capacity changes, a badly repartitioned graph can create load imbalance or expensive cross-machine communication, so a nominally beneficial scale-out operation can introduce enough redistribution and communication overhead to erode its performance benefit. Public clouds add a second dimension: instances have different CPU/memory capabilities and prices, and users pay for resources while the computation is running. Hence the goal is not simply the fastest graph execution, but the best performance–resource–monetary-cost combination under heterogeneous VM choices. Heidari and Buyya's IEEE study specifically addresses this coupling between graph scalability and pay-as-you-go cloud cost. [22]

Published research example — Safiollah Heidari and Rajkumar Buyya, “A Cost-Efficient Auto-Scaling Algorithm for Large-Scale Graph Processing in Cloud Environments with Heterogeneous Resources,” IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, 47(8), 1729–1741 (2021). Rather than assuming a homogeneous fixed cluster, the research makes resource heterogeneity part of the autoscaling decision. The proposed approach dynamically adjusts both the number and types of virtual machines according to the graph computation's requirements and combines resource adjustment with profiling and dynamic repartitioning so that graph partitions can be redistributed as the execution environment changes. [23]

The important conceptual contribution is the coupling of autoscaling with graph repartitioning. A generic cloud autoscaler sees “machines”; a graph-aware autoscaler must also consider which portions of the graph will execute on those machines and the communication/rebalancing consequences of changing the resource set. This transforms scaling from a scalar replica-count problem into a joint decision over VM quantity, VM type, and data/graph placement. [22]

Broader lesson. For graph analytics, “cost-effective autoscaling” should be evaluated against total job cost, not merely instantaneous hourly price. A cheaper configuration that greatly extends iterative execution time or causes repeated repartitioning may ultimately cost more. The Heidari–Buyya approach illustrates the corresponding solution direction: make heterogeneous resource selection and graph repartitioning part of one graph-aware elasticity mechanism. [22]

### Workload Prediction

Problem manifestation. Workload prediction is arguably the central technical obstacle separating reactive from genuinely proactive autoscaling. A reactive controller can wait for the workload to become measurable, but a predictive controller must estimate demand far enough into the future to cover the full actuation delay—resource request, VM/container startup, application initialization, registration, warm-up, and possibly state loading. If the prediction is too low, the system experiences the same SLO violations that proactive scaling was supposed to prevent; if it is too high, excess resources are provisioned and the economic benefit of elasticity is lost. Roy et al. explicitly describe this over-/under-provisioning dilemma and the interaction between prediction error, resource-acquisition delay, and scaling instability. [10]

Forecasting is difficult because cloud workloads combine several temporal behaviors: stable baselines, periodic daily or weekly patterns, short bursts, trends, regime changes, and sometimes one-off events. A single forecasting technique is therefore unlikely to dominate across all workloads. This limitation appears even in mature industrial implementations: AWS describes predictive scaling as particularly appropriate for recurring time-of-day or day-of-week patterns and provides a forecast-only mode so an operator can evaluate accuracy before allowing the forecast to alter capacity. [16]

Published research example — Gong, Gu, and Wilkes, “PRESS: PRedictive Elastic ReSource Scaling for cloud systems,” IEEE/IFIP CNSM 2010. PRESS attacks the problem using relatively lightweight signal-processing and statistical-learning techniques. Instead of relying on a fixed provisioned capacity, it continuously extracts fine-grained dynamic patterns from observed application resource demand, predicts upcoming requirements online, and adjusts allocated resources accordingly. [15]

The authors implemented PRESS on Xen and evaluated it with RUBiS and an application-load trace from Google. In those experiments, they reported less than 5% over-estimation error and near-zero under-estimation error, with the resulting elastic scaling reducing both resource waste and SLO violations. These figures should be interpreted as results for the evaluated workloads rather than universal accuracy guarantees, but they demonstrate the key principle: prediction becomes operationally useful when it is directly connected to an elasticity controller rather than treated as an isolated forecasting exercise. [15]

Subsequent work extends the same idea in two directions. Roy et al. combine workload forecasting with Model Predictive Control, allowing the autoscaler to optimize resource cost, SLO penalties, and reconfiguration cost over a look-ahead horizon; Graph-PHPA replaces comparatively simple statistical prediction with a two-stage LSTM + graph-neural-network model that first forecasts workload and then predicts microservice resource demand while accounting for inter-service dependencies. [24]

Industry practice suggests a particularly robust answer to imperfect prediction: do not make the entire scaling system depend on the forecast. AWS predictive scaling can provision extra capacity ahead of predicted growth but does not remove capacity merely because the forecast predicts a decline; scale-in is instead performed by a real-time target-tracking or step-scaling policy after actual demand has fallen. This asymmetric hybrid design limits the damage caused by an incorrect low forecast and illustrates a broader design principle emerging across the literature and industry: proactive prediction should provide anticipation, while reactive feedback supplies correction and safety. [2]

Taken together, the research indicates that the trajectory of auto-scaling is not a wholesale replacement of reactive control with prediction. Reactive approaches remain valuable because they operate on ground truth; proactive approaches are valuable because they compensate for provisioning and reconfiguration delay. The strongest architectures combine them: scheduled or learned forecasts create capacity ahead of expected demand; optimization decides how much capacity is economically justified; and reactive feedback corrects forecast errors, unexpected bursts, or model drift. Contemporary AWS predictive scaling already embodies this hybrid principle, while work such as PRESS, predictive MPC, Graph-PHPA, and AWARE shows how statistical learning, deep learning, optimization, and reinforcement learning can progressively enrich the decision layer. [25]
