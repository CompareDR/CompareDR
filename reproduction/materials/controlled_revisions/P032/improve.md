# International Organizations and Decolonization in the Twentieth Century

## Framing the relationship

The relationship between international organizations and decolonization is best understood not as a progression from a “bad” League of Nations to a “good” United Nations, nor as a story in which international institutions simply imposed decolonization on unwilling empires. International organizations were themselves built within a world dominated by empires. Their rules initially accommodated—and sometimes legitimized—colonial hierarchy. Yet by internationalizing questions that imperial governments had previously claimed as matters of domestic jurisdiction, those same organizations created procedures, forums, vocabularies, and audiences that anti-colonial actors could appropriate. Over time, the entry of newly independent states transformed not only what the organizations did but also who could speak authoritatively within them. Scholarship on both the League and the early UN consequently emphasizes an interactive process: international institutions reshaped colonial politics, while anti-colonial movements and postcolonial states reshaped international institutions. [1]

Three stages make that reciprocal relationship especially clear. In the interwar period, the League's Mandates System combined continued imperial administration with unprecedented international supervision, while activists organized alternative transnational anti-imperialisms such as the League Against Imperialism. After 1945, the UN initially embodied a compromise between colonial and anti-colonial positions, but the General Assembly, the Trusteeship System, Chapter XI reporting, and successive decolonization committees progressively gave anti-colonial claims greater institutional force. Finally, decolonization altered the composition of the UN itself: the proliferation of Asian, African, Caribbean, and other postcolonial states placed development, racial equality, sovereignty over natural resources, and reform of the international economy alongside—not simply in place of—the Cold War's East-West conflict. [2]

The analytical cutoff for interpretive scholarship in this report is early 2022, as requested. Later-dated institutional webpages are used only where they serve as repositories for historical primary documents such as the League Covenant, UN Charter, General Assembly resolutions, and historical membership records.

## From the League of Nations to the end of the Second World War

The Mandates System as a preservation and reorganization of empire. The League's colonial role was embedded in Article 22 of its Covenant. It applied not to colonies in general but specifically to colonies and territories taken from the defeated German and Ottoman empires after the First World War. Article 22 described their inhabitants in explicitly hierarchical language as peoples “not yet able to stand by themselves” and declared their welfare and development a “sacred trust of civilisation.” Their “tutelage” was to be entrusted to “advanced nations” acting as Mandatory powers. The system therefore did not dismantle colonial government. Britain, France, Belgium, South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, and Japan, among others, administered mandated territories, while the League supplied a new international legal and moral framework for that administration. [3]

The classification of mandates made the hierarchy especially visible. Former Ottoman territories were treated as the most politically “advanced”: Article 22 stated that their existence as independent nations could be provisionally recognized, although they would receive administrative “advice and assistance.” Other territories—especially in Africa—were subjected to more direct mandatory administration with restrictions relating to abuses such as the slave trade, arms and liquor traffic, while South-West Africa and certain Pacific islands could be administered under the Mandatory's laws as if they were integral parts of its territory. The legal form thus differentiated populations according to a civilizational scale rather than recognizing a uniform entitlement to immediate national independence. [4]

This was one reason the Mandates System could serve imperial interests. It redistributed former German and Ottoman possessions without challenging the legitimacy of British, French, Belgian, Dutch, Portuguese, or other colonial possessions as a whole. The Mandatory powers retained day-to-day coercive and administrative authority, and the Covenant established no universal timetable by which mandated peoples had to become sovereign states. As Susan Pedersen's major history of the system emphasizes, the League did not itself govern the mandates; it supervised Allied administration of territories seized from Germany and the Ottoman Empire. The innovation was therefore internationalized imperial trusteeship, not decolonization in the post-1945 sense. [5]

Yet describing mandates simply as disguised annexation misses what was institutionally novel. Article 22 required Mandatory governments to submit annual reports to the League Council, and it established a Permanent Mandates Commission to examine those reports and advise the Council. Colonial administration in these territories therefore became a recurrent subject of international documentation and scrutiny. Officials had to explain policies before an international body, and the mandates acquired a public legal status distinct from ordinary colonial possessions. [6]

International oversight and its unintended consequences. The Permanent Mandates Commission was not an anti-colonial parliament. Its members were presented as experts, and many came from colonial-administrative backgrounds. Its ability to compel Mandatory powers was limited, and its normal method was questioning, reporting, and recommendation rather than sanctions. Nevertheless, the very requirement that colonial governments account for their actions introduced an important change: imperial sovereignty in the mandated territories was no longer represented as wholly unanswerable to outsiders. Historians of the system have consequently stressed that it forced colonial authorities to justify practices to a transnational audience even when the resulting oversight fell far short of democratic accountability. [7]

Petitions pushed this internationalization further. The League developed procedures through which petitions concerning mandated territories could reach Geneva, and its archives preserve petitions concerning, among other places, Palestine and South-West Africa. The system was cumbersome: petitions normally passed through Mandatory authorities, petitioners had no general right to appear personally before the Commission, and a favorable hearing did not guarantee a remedy. Yet grievances over land, political representation, administration, and the conduct of mandatory governments could now enter an international archive and be discussed outside the imperial capital. [8]

The political significance lies in that contradiction. The League treated empire as a legitimate form of tutelage, but in doing so it made selected colonial governments objects of international comparison, reporting, petitioning, and criticism. Once an imperial power claimed that it governed not merely for itself but under a “sacred trust,” critics could demand evidence that the trust was being fulfilled. The Mandates System therefore helped normalize the proposition that treatment of dependent peoples was an international concern—even though the League neither proclaimed a general right to independence nor subjected all empires to the same oversight. Pedersen's formulation of the system's unintended consequences is particularly useful here: institutions created to manage the imperial settlement generated new arenas in which that settlement itself could be contested. [9]

This does not mean that the League “caused” the post-1945 collapse of empire. Nationalist mobilization inside colonies, two world wars, transformations in European power, Japanese expansion in Asia, economic change, anti-colonial intellectual networks, and later superpower politics were indispensable. Rather, the League contributed an institutional precedent: colonial administration could be supervised internationally, differentiated legal categories could be debated, subjects could petition an international body, and imperial powers could be expected to report on their conduct. Those practices would be inherited and substantially expanded by the United Nations. [10]

Anti-imperialist internationalism outside Geneva. At the same time, activists who rejected the League's accommodation with empire constructed rival international networks. Pan-African congresses connected Black intellectuals, labor activists, and political leaders across Africa, Europe, the Caribbean, and North America; anti-colonial students and expatriates formed organizations in European imperial capitals; and the Italian invasion of Ethiopia in 1935 stimulated organizations such as the International African Friends of Ethiopia, which was reorganized in Britain in 1937 into the International African Service Bureau associated with figures including George Padmore, C. L. R. James, and Jomo Kenyatta. Such networks linked colonial liberation to racial equality, labor politics, socialism, Pan-Africanism, and international solidarity rather than treating each colony as an isolated constitutional question. [11]

The most ambitious institutional counterpoint was the League Against Imperialism and for National Independence, commonly called the League Against Imperialism, or LAI. Its founding congress opened in Brussels on February 10, 1927. Historian Fredrik Petersson identifies German communist organizer Willi Münzenberg as its chief instigator and emphasizes Comintern support; the initiative had been under preparation since 1925. The congress brought together an unusually broad mixture of communist organizations, European leftists, trade unionists, and nationalist representatives from colonial and semi-colonial societies. Contemporary reconstructions count roughly 174 participants representing 134 organizations from more than thirty countries, colonies, or regions. [12]

Its importance lay partly in who met one another there. Participants included activists associated with the Indian National Congress, the African National Congress of South Africa, Perhimpunan Indonesia, Algerian nationalism, Chinese politics, and European socialist and communist movements. Jawaharlal Nehru, Mohammad Hatta, J. T. Gumede, and Messali Hadj were among the figures connected with the Brussels gathering. The political symbolism was deliberate: unlike the official League in Geneva, whose effective political subjects were sovereign states and empires, the Brussels congress sought to allow representatives of colonized peoples themselves to speak as international political actors. One contemporary participant, Gumede, relayed Münzenberg's characterization of the Brussels gathering as a “real league of nations,” an explicit claim that anti-imperialist internationalism offered a different conception of international representation. [13]

The LAI was not merely a conference. Berlin became its operational center from 1927 to 1933, where an international secretariat attempted to coordinate campaigns, publications, meetings, and solidarity work concerning colonial repression and struggles for national independence. A second major congress took place at Frankfurt in 1929. In aspiration, the organization was a permanent mechanism linking movements in colonized societies with anti-imperialist activists in imperial countries—a transnational “united front” rather than a diplomatic club of sovereign governments. [14]

Its achievement and weakness were inseparable from its communist connections. Comintern support supplied money, organizational capacity, contacts, and an international political framework at a time when colonial nationalists often had little access to formal international diplomacy. Yet that connection also limited the LAI's autonomy. Changes in Comintern strategy after 1928 encouraged sharper ideological divisions between communists and nationalist or social-democratic allies, undermining the broad coalition formed at Brussels. The seizure of power by the Nazis in Germany in 1933 then destroyed Berlin as its principal organizational center. The LAI lingered in diminished form into the later 1930s, but it never became the durable mass organization its founders envisaged. [15]

Its historical influence should therefore be framed carefully. It did not directly bring about decolonization and cannot simply be treated as the institutional ancestor of Bandung or the Non-Aligned Movement. Its membership was heterogeneous, its relations with the Comintern generated serious tensions, and its organizational life was short. But it demonstrated before the Second World War that activists from Asia, Africa, Europe, and the Americas could conceptualize colonialism as one international problem and create transcontinental political networks around it. Petersson notes that participants later remembered the LAI when postwar anti-colonial internationalism gathered momentum, while the careers of figures such as Nehru and Hatta illustrate personal continuities between interwar networks and the politics of postcolonial sovereignty. [14]

The contrast with the League of Nations is consequently more revealing than an opposition between “imperialists” and “anti-imperialists.” Geneva internationalized administration of empire through expertise, mandates, and reporting; Brussels tried to internationalize resistance to empire by connecting movements that the state-based diplomatic order often excluded. Both contributed, in very different ways, to the formation of an international arena in which colonial rule was no longer politically insulated within individual empires. [16]

## The United Nations and the internationalization of decolonization

The founding compromise of 1945. The United Nations was born in a dramatically different political environment, but it was not founded as an unequivocally anti-colonial organization. Britain and France remained major imperial states and became permanent members of the Security Council, while most colonial populations had no sovereign delegations at San Francisco. Negotiations exposed fundamental disagreements over whether all dependent territories should be subjected to international supervision, whether the objective should be “self-government” or explicit “independence,” and how powerful a trusteeship organization should be. Archival records from the San Francisco negotiations show that leading governments regarded these differences as sufficiently serious to threaten agreement on trusteeship itself. [17]

The resulting Charter contained both anti-colonial possibilities and imperial safeguards. Article 1 made respect for “equal rights and self-determination of peoples” one of the purposes of the UN, and the Charter's language of human rights and equality supplied concepts that anti-colonial representatives could deploy. But it contained no 1945 equivalent of the later demand for immediate abolition of colonial rule. Instead, dependent territories were divided principally between the stronger International Trusteeship System of Chapters XII and XIII and the looser regime for Non-Self-Governing Territories under Chapter XI. That distinction was a political compromise precisely because colonial powers had resisted putting all colonies under international trusteeship. [18]

From mandates to trusteeship. The UN Trusteeship System inherited the “sacred trust” idea of the League while changing its political endpoint. Trust territories included former League mandates, territories detached from enemy states after the Second World War, and any territories voluntarily placed under trusteeship by the states responsible for their administration. In practice, not all colonies entered the system: this was crucial, because the great majority of colonial dependencies remained outside full trusteeship supervision. [19]

The normative difference from Article 22 of the League Covenant is striking. Article 76 of the UN Charter expressly made “progressive development towards self-government or independence” an objective of trusteeship, taking account of the circumstances and the freely expressed wishes of the inhabitants. It also connected trusteeship with human rights and fundamental freedoms. The Trusteeship Council could consider reports, receive petitions, and arrange periodic visits to trust territories. The League's language of open-ended civilizational tutelage was therefore retained in institutional form but rewritten within a Charter that identified self-government or independence as the destination. [20]

The ultimate record illustrates both the system's significance and its limited coverage. Eleven territories were administered under UN trusteeship, and all eventually reached self-government or independence. This should not be confused with the whole history of decolonization, however: the independence of India, Algeria, the Belgian Congo, most British and French African colonies, and many other territories occurred outside the Trusteeship System itself. The UN's wider impact depended heavily on Chapter XI and on General Assembly politics. [21]

Chapter XI and the importance of information. Article 73 applied to members administering territories “whose peoples have not yet attained a full measure of self-government.” Those governments accepted the principle that inhabitants' interests were “paramount” and undertook to promote their political, economic, social, and educational advancement. Article 73(b) required them to develop self-government, take account of political aspirations, and assist the development of free political institutions. Article 73(e) required regular transmission to the UN Secretary-General of statistical and other information concerning economic, social, and educational conditions, subject to specified constitutional and security limitations. [22]

The wording mattered. Unlike Article 76 on trust territories, Article 73 did not initially make independence the explicit goal for every Non-Self-Governing Territory, and Article 73(e) specifically enumerated economic, social, and educational information rather than political data. Administering powers could therefore argue that Chapter XI was a declaration of broad obligations, not a license for the General Assembly to supervise internal colonial politics. Anti-colonial and non-administering states increasingly argued the opposite: once the Charter established obligations toward dependent peoples, the UN had to be capable of judging whether those obligations were actually being fulfilled. That contest over the meaning of a seemingly technical reporting requirement became one of the key routes by which international oversight expanded. [23]

In 1946, administering states identified territories for which they would transmit information under Article 73(e), creating the basis of the UN's list of Non-Self-Governing Territories. The General Assembly soon established machinery to examine that information. A succession of ad hoc and special bodies evolved into the Committee on Information from Non-Self-Governing Territories, which operated under that name from 1952 to 1963. Its work centered on information transmitted by administering powers, while the General Assembly's Fourth Committee dealt broadly with trusteeship and dependent-territory questions. [24]

At first glance this might appear bureaucratic rather than revolutionary: governments sent data and committees discussed education, health, labor, and economic conditions. In practice, however, the reporting process raised inherently political questions. Who could decide that a territory had become self-governing and that reporting could cease? Was the administering power alone competent to make that determination? Could representatives of the territory participate? Could the General Assembly examine political and constitutional development even though Article 73(e) did not explicitly list political information? These disputes gradually turned the ostensibly technical “information” machinery into what Jessica Lynne Pearson calls a de facto supervisory regime for dependent territories. [25]

Colonial governments recognized the stakes. Diplomatic correspondence from the early 1950s records British and other administering powers protesting that the Fourth Committee subjected colonial affairs to politicized criticism and that UN organs were expanding their interpretation of the Charter beyond what the colonial powers believed had been agreed at San Francisco. Such objections are revealing regardless of whether one accepts their legal interpretation: they show that, even before the great African independence wave of the 1960s, administering powers perceived UN scrutiny as a meaningful constraint on their ability to treat colonial questions as exclusively domestic affairs. [26]

Why the General Assembly became the central arena. Institutional design mattered enormously. In the Security Council, Britain and France possessed vetoes, and great-power strategic conflict often dominated deliberations. In the General Assembly, by contrast, every member state had a vote and colonial powers could be outnumbered as Asian, Middle Eastern, Latin American, and eventually African membership expanded. The Assembly could not ordinarily legislate binding rules for states in the manner of a national parliament, but it could place colonial conflicts on the international agenda, create investigative committees, hear arguments, define political standards, request information, recommend action, and repeatedly force governments to defend their conduct in a public diplomatic forum. [27]

This made decolonization partly a struggle over agenda control and legitimacy. Anti-colonial delegations did not need to defeat an imperial military force inside the General Assembly for the institution to matter. They could redefine a dispute that London, Paris, Brussels, Lisbon, Pretoria, or another government characterized as a domestic or constitutional matter as an international question of self-determination. Repeated resolutions, committee investigations, hearings, petitions, and publicity could delegitimize continued colonial rule, strengthen nationalist claims, and impose diplomatic costs on administering powers. Conversely, colonial powers and their allies continued to use legal argument, procedural challenges, claims of domestic jurisdiction, and developmental rhetoric to resist this expansion. [28]

The numerical balance shifted decisively during the 1950s and especially in 1960. The UN had begun with 51 members in 1945; by the end of 1960 it had 99. Seventeen African states were admitted during the “Year of Africa,” greatly enlarging the group of governments for which anti-colonialism was not an abstract ideological question but part of their recent political experience. New Asian and African states increasingly coordinated positions and could form majorities with other anti-colonial delegations. [29]

That transformation is why it would be misleading to attribute the UN's anti-colonial turn solely to its Charter. Most of the relevant provisions had existed since 1945. What changed was the membership capable of interpreting and operationalizing them. International law and institutional procedure supplied tools, but changing political representation altered how those tools were used. The UN thus became more strongly anti-colonial partly because decolonization was already happening and newly sovereign states carried the struggle into the organization. [30]

The Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples. The pivotal statement of the new balance was General Assembly Resolution 1514 (XV), adopted on December 14, 1960. A Soviet proposal had initially put a declaration on colonial independence before the Assembly, but the text that prevailed was an Afro-Asian draft introduced by Cambodia for 26 Asian and African countries and eventually sponsored by 43 delegations. It was adopted by 89 votes to none, with nine abstentions. [31]

Its content represented a substantial normative advance over both League mandates and the cautious language of the 1945 Charter settlement. The Declaration proclaimed that subjection of peoples to alien domination was contrary to fundamental human rights and the UN Charter; affirmed that all peoples have the right to self-determination and may freely determine their political status and pursue their economic, social, and cultural development; rejected lack of political, economic, social, or educational “preparedness” as a justification for delaying independence; called for an end to armed action and repressive measures against dependent peoples; and demanded immediate steps to transfer powers in Trust, Non-Self-Governing, and other territories according to the freely expressed wishes of their inhabitants. [32]

One clause also declared attempts to disrupt the national unity and territorial integrity of a country incompatible with the Charter. This provision illustrates an enduring tension in postcolonial self-determination. Resolution 1514 attacked imperial rule while simultaneously helping stabilize the territorial units that emerged from decolonization. In practice, the international order increasingly favored independence for established colonial territories while being much more cautious about an unrestricted right of secession for every distinct population within newly independent states. Decolonization therefore universalized state sovereignty without eliminating political conflicts over borders, minorities, and competing definitions of “the people.” [33]

A companion resolution adopted the following day, Resolution 1541 (XV), further clarified UN thinking about when a Non-Self-Governing Territory could be regarded as having achieved a full measure of self-government. It recognized three principal outcomes: emergence as a sovereign independent state, free association with an independent state, or integration with an independent state, subject to principles intended to protect free choice. This is important because UN decolonization was not formally synonymous in every case with creation of a separate sovereign state, even though independence became its most visible result. [34]

Resolution 1514's immediate institutional consequence was intensified monitoring. On November 27, 1961, Resolution 1654 created a Special Committee of seventeen to examine implementation of the Declaration; in December 1962 it was enlarged to twenty-four members, giving rise to the Special Committee commonly known as the Committee of 24. In 1963 the older Committee on Information from Non-Self-Governing Territories was dissolved and its functions transferred to the new anti-colonial committee. The institutional center of gravity had moved from asking primarily whether colonial powers were administering dependent territories properly to asking how and when colonial rule would end. [31]

Resolution 1514 should nevertheless not be treated as a magic legal instrument that automatically produced independence. Colonial wars continued; Portugal fought to retain its African empire into the 1970s; white-minority rule persisted in southern Africa; questions such as Western Sahara remained unresolved; and major powers sometimes subordinated self-determination to strategic concerns. General Assembly resolutions also lacked the coercive machinery necessary to implement every demand. The Declaration's importance was instead cumulative and normative: it established a clear anti-colonial standard against which governments would thereafter be judged and provided new states with a common vocabulary of self-determination, human rights, and immediate independence. [35]

The deeper postwar transformation can thus be summarized as a three-step process. The League had internationalized selected colonial administration; the 1945 UN Charter broadened that principle through trusteeship and reporting while retaining compromises with empire; the postcolonial General Assembly progressively converted supervision into an international presumption against colonial rule itself. The critical agent connecting those stages was not the institution alone but the changing political constituency operating inside it. [36]

## Decolonization remakes international organizations

The causal arrow now reverses. Decolonization was not merely a process acted upon by international organizations; it transformed those organizations from within. The increase from 51 UN members at the organization's founding to 99 by the end of 1960 was only the beginning of a much larger expansion. Because each sovereign state possessed one General Assembly vote irrespective of population, military power, or wealth, the admission of newly independent countries altered the Assembly much more dramatically than it altered institutions in which formal power remained concentrated among established great powers. [37]

The result was not a simple transfer of world power from Europe and North America to the former colonies. The five permanent members retained the Security Council veto; wealthy countries remained economically dominant; and new states differed widely in ideology, alliance, political system, strategic interest, and economic structure. But numerical power in universal or near-universal deliberative institutions gave postcolonial countries the capacity to set agendas, build voting coalitions, create subsidiary bodies, and redefine what counted as a legitimate international question. That mattered especially in the General Assembly, UNCTAD, UNESCO, and other one-state-one-vote settings. [38]

From East-West alone to East-West plus North-South. It is therefore better to say that decolonization complicated the Cold War than that it replaced an East-West confrontation with a North-South divide. The United States and Soviet Union remained enormously influential, and conflicts in Korea, Vietnam, the Congo, Angola, the Middle East, and elsewhere were entangled with superpower rivalry. New states themselves sometimes relied on one bloc or the other. But many postcolonial leaders rejected the assumption that international politics should be organized solely around Washington and Moscow. Colonialism, apartheid, racial hierarchy, economic development, commodity dependence, terms of trade, foreign control of natural resources, and unequal representation in international institutions became autonomous global issues. [39]

The term North-South captured this new fault line imperfectly but usefully. Whereas East-West language organized states primarily according to Cold War political and strategic blocs, North-South politics grouped many poorer, recently colonized states around shared concerns about structural economic inequality between industrialized and developing countries. The coalition was never homogeneous: oil exporters and oil importers, Latin American states and African least-developed countries, socialist and capitalist economies, and very large and very small states frequently had divergent interests. Nevertheless, international institutions made it possible for them to convert the common historical experience of peripheral position into negotiating coalitions. [40]

The Non-Aligned Movement. The first major political expression of this search for collective autonomy grew out of the Bandung Conference, held in Indonesia from April 18 to 24, 1955, and attended by delegations from 29 governments, overwhelmingly Asian and African. Bandung linked opposition to colonialism and racial domination with sovereignty, peaceful coexistence, economic cooperation, non-interference, and reform of international relations. Its principles included respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, equality, non-intervention, peaceful settlement of disputes, and adherence to the UN Charter. [41]

Building on Bandung, the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) was formally established at its first summit in Belgrade, Yugoslavia, on September 1–6, 1961. Its emergence is associated especially with leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru of India, Josip Broz Tito of Yugoslavia, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, Sukarno of Indonesia, and Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana, although non-alignment was always a coalition rather than the doctrine of a single founder. [42]

“Non-aligned” did not necessarily mean politically neutral, passive, or exactly equidistant between the superpowers. NAM governments often took strong positions on colonial wars, apartheid, Palestine, disarmament, military bases, and economic issues, and individual members sometimes developed close security relationships with one Cold War camp. The common principle was more fundamentally autonomy: newly independent states should not have to subordinate their foreign policy and development choices to membership in a great-power military bloc. This was an attempt to make sovereign equality meaningful in a system still organized by vast material inequalities. [43]

In this sense, NAM extended decolonization into diplomacy. Political independence would be incomplete if former colonies merely became clients in a bipolar hierarchy. The movement sought room for independent foreign policy while keeping anti-colonial questions on the international agenda. Its members were important advocates against the Portuguese colonial wars, apartheid and white-minority rule in southern Africa, and other remaining forms of colonial domination. At the same time, their different regimes and strategic relationships meant that non-aligned solidarity was always negotiated rather than automatic. [44]

The Group of 77 and the economic turn. The clearest institutional expression of North-South politics came in the economic sphere. On June 15, 1964, at the end of the first United Nations Conference on Trade and Development in Geneva, 77 developing countries issued a joint declaration and established what became known as the Group of 77 (G77). The organization's name endured even as its membership subsequently expanded. [45]

The founding declaration is revealing because it already framed development as a question of the structure of the world economy rather than merely domestic modernization. It called UNCTAD a significant step toward a “new and just world economic order,” sought an international division of labor conducive to accelerated industrialization in developing countries, complained of adverse terms of trade and inadequacies in commodity arrangements and export preferences, and insisted that developing countries had to maintain political unity in order to formulate joint programs of action. [46]

This represented a further transformation of decolonization. Nationalists had fought for the right to govern territories; postcolonial governments now asked what sovereign equality meant when their economies remained dependent on exports of a few primary commodities, foreign capital and technology, markets in industrial countries, and rules written when most of them had not been independent participants. The language moved from political decolonization toward what might be called economic decolonization—the idea that flag independence did not by itself remove inherited structures of unequal exchange and dependency. [47]

This agenda also generated new organizations. UNCTAD became a permanent UN body after its first 1964 conference and provided developing countries with a specialized forum for trade and development questions that they believed were inadequately addressed in existing economic institutions. The G77 progressively developed a standing diplomatic structure, beginning with its first ministerial process and the 1967 Charter of Algiers, and coordinated positions not only in New York but across parts of the broader UN system. [48]

The New International Economic Order. The most ambitious expression of this politics came in the early 1970s. The Fourth Summit of the Non-Aligned Movement and the G77's economic diplomacy converged around demands for a New International Economic Order (NIEO). At the UN General Assembly's Sixth Special Session, the Assembly adopted the Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order, Resolution 3201 (S-VI), and an accompanying Programme of Action, Resolution 3202 (S-VI), on May 1, 1974. [49]

The NIEO's premise was that formal sovereign equality coexisted with an international economic order shaped when much of Asia, Africa, and the Caribbean was still colonized or politically subordinate. Its advocates therefore sought structural changes rather than simply additional foreign aid. The agenda linked sovereign equality to permanent sovereignty over natural resources, stronger control over foreign investment and transnational corporations, reform of commodity trade, improved and more stable export earnings, preferential access for developing-country manufactures, expanded industrialization and technology transfer, more development finance, and greater participation by developing states in international economic decision-making. [50]

A closely related instrument, the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States, was adopted as Resolution 3281 (XXIX) on December 12, 1974, by 115 votes to six with ten abstentions. Its origins illustrate the connection between UNCTAD and the G77: Mexican President Luis Echeverría proposed such a charter at UNCTAD III in 1972; Ethiopia subsequently introduced a proposal on behalf of the G77; and in 1974 Mexico submitted a draft in the General Assembly's Second Committee on behalf of 90 G77 states. [51]

Natural resources were central because they lay at the junction of juridical and economic decolonization. Independence was of limited value, NIEO advocates argued, if states could not determine the exploitation of minerals, oil, agricultural resources, or other national wealth. This linked the economic program back to the UN's earlier doctrine of self-determination: political sovereignty was increasingly interpreted by developing countries as including a substantive right to control development strategy and economic resources. [52]

The NIEO was nevertheless a contested political program, not an achieved constitutional settlement for the world economy. Developed market economies disputed important proposals concerning nationalization, compensation, commodity policy, trade preferences, corporate regulation, and institutional redistribution. Developing countries themselves differed according to resource endowment and economic interest. By the later 1970s and 1980s, economic recession, debt crises, changes in commodity markets, divisions within the South, and the growing influence of market-oriented economic policies weakened the bargaining coalition behind the most ambitious version of the NIEO. Its importance lies less in whether every proposal was implemented than in the scale of the challenge it posed: postcolonial states argued that decolonizing political jurisdiction required reforming international economic rules as well. [53]

This also reveals the limits of the General Assembly's transformed voting balance. Postcolonial states could use their numbers to pass declarations, establish committees, and define new normative agendas. They could not, by General Assembly voting alone, redistribute capital, compel industrial states to accept new commodity regimes, or restructure every institution of global economic governance. Decolonization therefore widened the gap between formal sovereign equality—strongest in one-state-one-vote forums—and persistent inequalities of economic and military capability. [54]

New operational functions: decolonization and peacekeeping. Decolonization also changed what the UN was expected to do on the ground. Peacekeeping itself predated the great independence wave—the UN had already deployed observer operations in the late 1940s and the first large armed emergency force during the 1956 Suez Crisis—so it would be inaccurate to say that decolonization invented peacekeeping. But crises surrounding imperial withdrawal and fragile postcolonial statehood pushed peace operations into much more demanding territory. [55]

The most important early case was the United Nations Operation in the Congo (ONUC). The former Belgian Congo became independent on June 30, 1960. Within days, military disorder and Belgian intervention produced a major crisis, and the Congolese government requested UN assistance. ONUC was established in July 1960 initially to help secure Belgian withdrawal, assist the government in maintaining law and order, and provide technical assistance; the mission subsequently became entangled in the Katanga secession and the wider Congo crisis. [56]

The Congo demonstrated how decolonization generated problems that crossed the UN's traditional categories. A crisis beginning with colonial withdrawal rapidly became simultaneously a question of sovereignty, civil conflict, external intervention, superpower rivalry, territorial integrity, technical state capacity, humanitarian need, and international peace and security. The UN consequently found itself not just observing borders but deploying large military contingents and civilian expertise inside a newly independent state. That experience foreshadowed later peacekeeping and state-support functions in postcolonial conflicts. [57]

Here again the effect was contradictory. UN intervention could provide newly independent states with an alternative to direct intervention by former colonial powers or superpowers. Yet intervention also raised difficult questions about the limits of sovereign consent, the impartiality of international officials, the use of force, and the influence of powerful states over ostensibly international action. The postcolonial UN thus became more active precisely as newly independent governments insisted more strongly on sovereignty and non-interference—a tension that has remained central to peacekeeping ever since. [58]

The broader institutional change was therefore considerable. Decolonization expanded the UN's work from administering and supervising dependent territories toward development assistance, racial and anti-apartheid questions, economic reform, technical cooperation, peacekeeping, humanitarian activity, and support for newly independent states. At the same time, postcolonial states insisted that these expanded functions should not recreate paternalistic trusteeship under another name. The vocabulary of sovereignty that had enabled decolonization also became a constraint on international intervention. [59]

## The larger historical pattern

Across the century, institutional opportunities, their use by colonized and postcolonial actors, and their structural limits together explain a reciprocal process of appropriation and transformation—not an automatic march toward liberation.

**The League era: international accountability within empire.** The institutional opening consisted of annual reports, the Permanent Mandates Commission, petitions and publicity, which placed the governance of dependent peoples before procedures outside individual imperial states. The structural limit was equally important: the Mandates System legitimized foreign rule through tutelage and civilization, while leaving most colonial territories outside supervision. Empire gained international legitimacy by accepting only limited international accountability. [60]

**Interwar resistance: organizing beyond the official framework.** The League Against Imperialism and related Pan-African and anti-colonial networks exposed the exclusion built into that framework. Colonized peoples asserted their status as political actors entitled to organize internationally for emancipation, rather than merely as populations to be administered well. These networks created transnational relationships and a political language that survived into the age of Bandung. Their resources and durability, however, were far below those of governments, and ideological disputes constrained their effectiveness. Their opening was transnational organization, not equal access to governmental power. [61]

**The UN: supervisory opportunities used to change the agenda.** The institutional inheritance had two sides. Trusteeship retained a resemblance to League tutelage while explicitly aiming at self-government or independence; Chapter XI made other dependent territories an international concern. Administering powers initially retained considerable scope to resist intrusive scrutiny. Asian, African, Arab and other anti-colonial states then used their growing General Assembly majority to push committees and reporting beyond the modest supervisory role colonial powers had envisaged in 1945. The change depended on political participation, not simply on new wording. [62]

Resolution 1514 shows the resulting change in the standard those institutions applied. In 1919, international law had asked how “advanced” states should administer peoples considered unready for self-rule. By 1960, the General Assembly declared that alleged lack of preparedness could not justify delaying independence. The burden of political explanation increasingly fell on colonial rule rather than on the independence claim. This was the normative culmination of the shift, not a statement that institutional limits had vanished. [63]

**Postcolonial organization: extending sovereignty beyond the flag.** NAM pursued strategic autonomy, while the G77/NIEO campaign sought economic restructuring. Their premise was that juridical sovereignty was necessary but insufficient: without meaningful influence over trade, investment, technology, finance and natural resources, formal independence could coexist with inherited dependence. The North-South agenda therefore attempted to carry decolonization from territorial government into the organization of the international system. These were ambitions for restructuring, not a claim that economic sovereignty had already been secured. [64]

The institutional opportunity was strongest in the General Assembly, where one state had one vote and new states could transform representation. The structural constraint was the much more uneven distribution of military, financial and economic power elsewhere. Decolonization democratized international representation without eliminating hierarchy; genuine postcolonial agency coexisted with striking continuities in inequality. [65]

Nor did “from East-West to North-South” describe a chronological replacement. The Cold War continued, and solidarity did not erase differences among postcolonial states. Instead, superpower competition coexisted with collective agendas on colonial liberation, apartheid, development, commodity prices, economic sovereignty, international redistribution and racial equality. International politics acquired more organizing axes than capitalism versus communism alone could explain. [66]

The two directions of change can thus be read together: international organizations made colonial administration discussable, reviewable and eventually presumptively illegitimate; decolonization changed institutional membership, agendas, coalitions, legal vocabulary and operational responsibilities. Neither direction was automatic. Imperial governments used institutions to defend their interests, while anti-colonial actors encountered weakness, selectivity and paternalism. Because these organizations were contested arenas rather than instruments of one political purpose, actors excluded from their original design could gradually redirect them toward purposes their founders had not fully intended. [67]

## Source base and interpretive orientation

The core primary evidence for the interwar period is Article 22 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, supplemented by League archival material on mandate reporting and petition procedures. The principal scholarly guide is Susan Pedersen's The Guardians: The League of Nations and the Crisis of Empire and related pre-2022 scholarship on the Mandates System. For the League Against Imperialism, the report relies especially on Fredrik Petersson's work on the Brussels congress, Berlin secretariat, Comintern relationship, and organizational trajectory, together with scholarship on interwar Pan-African and anti-imperialist networks. [68]

For the UN period, the foundational primary sources are Chapters XI–XIII of the UN Charter, General Assembly Resolution 1514 (XV), the records concerning the Committee on Information and Special Committee on Decolonization, and contemporary diplomatic documentation from the San Francisco negotiations and early UN debates. Jessica Lynne Pearson's research is particularly useful in showing why the Chapter XI reporting system became politically more intrusive than colonial governments had expected, while Adom Getachew's Worldmaking after Empire helps situate postcolonial demands within the larger ambition to reconstruct international order rather than merely multiply nation-states. [69]

For the postcolonial transformation of international organizations, the central primary materials are the NAM record of Bandung and Belgrade, the G77's Joint Declaration of June 15, 1964, the 1974 UN resolutions establishing the NIEO, and the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States. Read together, these documents show a clear conceptual progression—from sovereign independence, to diplomatic autonomy, to collective bargaining over the economic structure of international society—while the Congo peacekeeping record demonstrates how decolonization simultaneously expanded demands for UN operational involvement. [70]

The resulting historiographical picture resists both institutional triumphalism and institutional cynicism. The League and UN cannot be separated from the unequal international orders in which they were created; nor can their rules be reduced entirely to the intentions of imperial powers. Procedures acquire political lives of their own when membership changes and excluded actors learn to use them. The history of decolonization is consequently also a history of who was entitled to participate in making “international” politics—and of how changing the participants changed the meaning and purposes of international organization itself. [71]
