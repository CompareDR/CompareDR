# Universal Basic Income in Developed Countries: Comparative Evidence and Long-Term Consequences

## TL;DR

- Across the best-designed developed-country pilots, unconditional cash reliably improves financial security, food security, and short-run subjective wellbeing, but produces **modest reductions in labor supply** (not mass withdrawal) and — in the most rigorous US RCT (OpenResearch, 2024) — **no lasting improvement in physical or mental health**; the evidence for large health and child-development gains is weaker and more contested than advocates claim.
- **No pilot has actually tested a universal, permanent UBI at scale.** Every study is a targeted, temporary transfer to a subset of a population, so pilots cannot capture the general-equilibrium, price, tax-financing and permanent-income effects that dominate the real macro debate — a point stressed by both skeptics (Hoynes & Rothstein 2019) and the pilots' own designers.
- The central unresolved question is **fiscal**: a genuine UBI generous enough to matter is extraordinarily expensive — Canada's Parliamentary Budget Officer puts a national guaranteed basic income at C$85 billion (2021–22) rising to C$93 billion (2025–26), [Pbo-dpb][ref-01] [CBC News][ref-02] and a $12,000/year US UBI would cost roughly $2.4 trillion annually (about one-eighth of GDP; [Third Way][ref-03] CBPP's Greenstein put a $10,000 version at $3 trillion/year, "$30 trillion to $40 trillion over ten years"). [Center on Budget an…][ref-04] Economists disagree sharply on whether it would reduce or increase poverty relative to targeted transfers, and whether general-equilibrium effects offset labor-supply disincentives.

## Key Findings

1. 1. **Labor supply falls modestly, mostly on the intensive margin, and does not collapse.** The OpenResearch/Sam Altman US RCT — the most rigorous test to date — found recipients of $1,000/month were 2 percentage points less likely to be employed and worked ~1.3–1.4 fewer hours/week [OpenResearch][ref-05] (the NBER working-paper abstract reports a larger 4.1-pp participation decline; see reconciliation below). Finland found no clean employment effect. Alaska's permanent dividend shows no aggregate employment reduction at all. Advocates' claim that cash "increases work" (Stockton) rests on weaker, non-experimental evidence.
2. 2. **Financial security and food security improve robustly** — the most consistent findings across all pilots.
3. 3. **Health effects are genuinely contested.** The strongest study (OpenResearch, Miller et al. 2024) found only short-lived (one-year) stress/mental-health gains and *precise null effects* on physical health, undercutting the strongest advocacy claims.
4. 4. **Child/brain-development evidence is suggestive but fragile** (Baby's First Years).
5. 5. **The fiscal and general-equilibrium questions — the ones that actually decide whether UBI is viable — remain essentially untested by pilots.**

## Details

### I. Why cross-pilot comparison is hard (read this first)

The single most important analytic point, emphasized by Hilary Hoynes and Jesse Rothstein ("Universal Basic Income in the United States and Advanced Countries," *Annual Review of Economics* 11:929–958, 2019; NBER WP 25538), is that **the pilots do not test UBI**. They argue that ongoing UBI pilot studies "will do little to resolve the major outstanding questions." [Annual Reviews][ref-06] Reasons:

- **Targeted, not universal**: Finland studied only unemployed benefit recipients; Stockton, SEED, Baby's First Years, Chelsea Eats, Denver, Compton and the OpenResearch study all targeted low-income or specific populations. Only MINCOME's Dauphin "saturation site" and the GiveDirectly Kenya villages approximate universality within a community.
- **Temporary, not permanent**: Pilots run 1–3 years (GiveDirectly's 12-year arm is the exception). A temporary windfall changes behavior differently from a lifelong entitlement (permanent-income/Ricardian logic).
- **Partial equilibrium only**: A pilot cannot capture what happens to wages, prices, rents, or aggregate labor markets when everyone receives the transfer and it must be financed by taxes. Jones & Marinescu's Alaska work is valued precisely because it is universal and permanent, capturing general-equilibrium effects pilots miss.
- **Interaction with existing welfare**: In Finland recipients forfeited some insurance-based benefits; in most US pilots, care was taken to protect existing benefits, so the "net" transfer varies.
- **Design heterogeneity**: benefit level, benefit-reduction (clawback) rates, sample size, self-selection, attrition, and control-group quality vary enormously. The Ontario pilot had no control group and was self-selected survey-based; SEED had only 125 treatment recipients.

### II. FINLAND — The Basic Income Experiment (2017–2018)

**Design**: Run by Kela with VATT evaluation; 2,000 unemployed people (randomly sampled from those receiving basic/labour-market unemployment benefit in November 2016) [Social and Health Mi…][ref-07] received **€560/month, tax-free, unconditional**, for two years (1 Jan 2017–31 Dec 2018). Control group: ~173,000 unemployed not selected. Recipients forfeited some insurance-based benefits, so the net transfer was often modest.

**Employment effects** (the definitive peer-reviewed source): **Verho, Hämäläinen & Kanninen, "Removing Welfare Traps: Employment Responses in the Finnish Basic Income Experiment," *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy* 14(1): 501–522 (2022)**. The experiment lowered participation tax rates by 23 percentage points for full-time work. Despite this, **first-year employment was statistically unchanged (point estimate +1.5 days, 95% CI −2.3 to 5.4)**. The **second year showed +6.6 days (95% CI 1.3 to 11.9), statistically significant** [Econstor][ref-08] — but this is confounded by Finland's 2018 "activation model" reform, which tightened benefit conditions asymmetrically. Chief Researcher Kari Hämäläinen (VATT): "The effects of the second year of the experiment cannot be separated from the effects of the activation model." [Finnish Government][ref-09] The widely-cited government "6 extra days of employment" figure refers to the pre-registered Nov 2017–Oct 2018 window (recipients worked 78 days on average) [Finland Toolbox][ref-10] and straddles both years, so it too is partly confounded. The paper rules out participation elasticities above 0.16. (Note: the "0.39 days" figure sometimes cited is a conflation — 0.39 is a participation *elasticity* elsewhere in this literature, not a days estimate.)

**Wellbeing** (Kela survey in Kangas, Jauhiainen, Simanainen & Ylikännö, eds., final report 2020; survey response rate only 23%): recipients reported **life satisfaction of 7.3 vs 6.8 (0–10)**; less mental strain, depression, sadness and loneliness; better perceived cognition; [Vatt][ref-11] more trust (in other people 6.3→6.8; in politicians 4.0→4.5; [Basicincome][ref-12] in courts/police 6.9→7.2); better perceived economic security. First-year self-reports: 55% of recipients vs 46% of control rated health good/very good; 17% vs 25% reported high stress. [Finnish Government][ref-13] **Key caveat** (Minna Ylikännö, Kela): "it is not possible to say with certainty that the better wellbeing of the test group was due specifically to receipt of a basic income" (low response rate; possible confounding).

A newer peer-reviewed study — **Hämäläinen, Simanainen & Verho, "Health effects of cash transfers: Evidence from the Finnish basic income experiment," *Journal of Public Economics* 250: 105480 (2025)** — reports the experiment produced a 9–11% increase in average annual income [ScienceDirect][ref-14] and reaffirms "only minor employment effects."

**Criticisms of design**: targeted at the unemployed (not universal); benefit-mimicking (net change small); confounded by the 2018 activation reform; short duration.

### III. CANADA

**Ontario Basic Income Pilot (2017–2019)**: Launched April 2017 in Hamilton/Brantford, Thunder Bay and Lindsay; up to ~C$17,000/year (individuals), C$24,000 (couples), +C$6,000 for disability, 50% clawback. [Pbo-dpb][ref-01] Planned for 4,000 people; **cancelled early by the Ford government (2018/2019)** before official evaluation. Evidence comes from a post-cancellation survey: **Ferdosi, McDowell, Lewchuk & Ross, "Southern Ontario's Basic Income Experience" (McMaster University / Hamilton Roundtable for Poverty Reduction, 2020)** and **McDowell & Ferdosi, *Basic Income Studies* 16(2): 209–256 (2021)**. Findings: **a small decline in labour-market participation but improvements in general wellbeing**; ~three-quarters of those working when the pilot began kept working; self-reported improvements in mental health, housing stability, social relationships, and reduced use of hospitals/physicians. **Methodological limits are severe**: self-selected survey, no control group, retrospective self-report, early cancellation — suggestive, not causal.

**MINCOME (Dauphin, Manitoba, 1974–1979)** — historical saturation baseline. Post-2015 reanalyses:

- **Evelyn Forget** ("The Town with No Poverty," *Canadian Public Policy*, 2011; later work): an **8.5% reduction in hospitalization** for participants vs matched controls (especially accidents/injuries and mental health), fewer physician contacts, and more adolescents completing grade 12; no increase in fertility or family dissolution.
- **David Calnitsky** ("'More Normal than Welfare,'" *Canadian Review of Sociology* 53(1):26–71, 2016): recipients viewed MINCOME as "more normal than welfare"; quasi-universality reduced stigma and shame.
- **Calnitsky & Latner** ("Basic Income in a Small Town," *Social Problems* 64(3):373–397, 2017): labour-market participation fell by a difference-in-differences estimate of ~**11.3 pp in Dauphin** (saturation) vs ~8.1 pp in the dispersed treatment — with a "social multiplier": the community effect exceeded the individual effect. Much of the reduction reflected mothers with infants and students staying in school.
- **A reanalysis in *Canadian Public Policy* (2021)** challenged Forget's hospitalization finding, arguing a pre-existing downward trend in Dauphin hospital use predated MINCOME, so the data "do not support claims that a guaranteed annual income reduces healthcare costs." [UTP Publishing][ref-15] Forget defends her results. **This is a genuine, unresolved dispute.**

**Newer Canadian efforts**: Quebec launched a **Basic Income Program in January 2023** (~C$1,211/month base) for people with severe/long-term employment limitations. Federal bills **S-233** (Sen. Kim Pate) and **C-223** (MP Leah Gazan) sought a "national framework for a guaranteed livable basic income"; C-223 was defeated at second reading Sept 25, 2024, and both died when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025. **PBO** costings (based on the Ontario model, report RP-2122-001-S): a GBI of ~C$16,989 (individuals)/C$24,027 (couples) with a 50% phase-out rate [Pbo-dpb][ref-01] would cost from **C$85 billion in 2021–22 rising to C$93 billion by 2025–26**, [Pbo-dpb][ref-01] and could cut poverty roughly in half.

### IV. UNITED STATES

**OpenResearch Unconditional Cash Study (2020–2023) — the most rigorous US RCT.** Funded largely by Sam Altman/OpenAI (plus NIH, NSF). 1,000 low-income individuals [umich][ref-16] [aeaweb][ref-17] (incomes <300% FPL) received **$1,000/month for 3 years**; [umich][ref-16] 2,000 controls received $50/month. Three 2024 NBER working papers:

- **Employment (Vivalt, Rhodes, Bartik, Broockman, Krause, Miller; NBER WP 32719)**: the transfer reduced individual income excluding transfers by ~$1,500–1,800/year and cut labor-force participation. **Note a data discrepancy**: the NBER WP 32719 abstract reports "a 4.1 percentage point decrease in labor market participation," while OpenResearch's own findings summary reports recipients were "about 2 percentage points less likely to be employed during years two and three" [OpenResearch][ref-18] and worked 1.3–1.4 fewer hours/week (these reflect different specifications/versions; treat the participation effect as roughly 2–4 pp). Partners reduced hours by a comparable amount. Recipients used freed time for leisure, had more agency in job choice, and showed modestly higher entrepreneurial intent.
- **Health (Miller, Rhodes, Bartik, Broockman, Krause, Vivalt; NBER WP 32711)**: large but **short-lived** improvements in stress and food security; increased hospital/ED use and ~$20/month more medical spending; **precise null effects on physical health** (biomarkers from blood draws) and **no mental-health improvement after year 1**. Sarah Miller: "Could cash transfers be the way to meaningfully and effectively reduce health disparities? It's hard for me to look at these results and say yes." [umich][ref-16] Children in treated households were more likely to be up to date on vaccinations.
- **Consumption (Bartik, Rhodes, Broockman, Krause, Miller, Vivalt; NBER WP 32784)**: recipients spent ~$300/month more on food, rent, transportation; gave more support to others; more residential mobility; improved ability to weather shocks. This study most cleanly contradicts the strongest advocacy narrative on health.

**Stockton SEED (2019–2021)**: 125 residents got **$500/month for 24 months**; 200 controls; RCT-style; evaluated by **Amy Castro (Baker) & Stacia West**. First-year results (*Journal of Urban Health*): recipients "obtained full-time employment at more than twice [Penn Today][ref-19] the rate" of controls (28%→40% vs a 5-pp rise); less than 1% of tracked spending on alcohol/tobacco (~37% food, 22% merchandise, 11% utilities, 10% auto); reduced income volatility; clinically significant improvements in anxiety/depression. **Caveats**: small sample; philanthropically funded; overlapped with COVID; employment result is suggestive.

**Baby's First Years (2018– )**: RCT, ~1,000 low-income mothers randomized at birth to **$333/month vs $20/month** for 40+ months. **Troller-Renfree et al., *PNAS* 119(5):e2115649119 (2022)**: infants in the high-cash group showed more high-frequency EEG power (associated with later development). **Important caveats**: pre-registered primary EEG endpoints were largely not individually significant; the headline came from secondary/exploratory bands, prompting a replication debate. Companion papers (e.g., *JAMA Network Open* 2023) found **no significant effects on maternal-reported child health, nutrition, or sleep**, and null effects on maternal substance use — more mixed than early coverage suggested.

**Chelsea Eats (2020–2021, Massachusetts)**: >2,000 residents got $200–$400/month for 9 months; lottery randomization; **Jeffrey Liebman et al. (Harvard Kennedy School Rappaport Institute)**. A 2024 *JAMA* paper examined health-care utilization; a January 2026 *Nature Food* paper found the transfer reduced caloric deficits and improved intake of nutrient-dense foods. No significant change in housing stability/rent burden.

**Denver Basic Income Project (2022– )**: RCT with 807 people experiencing homelessness; arms of $1,000/month, or $6,500 lump sum + $500/month, vs $50/month comparison. First-year results: **45% of participants secured housing**; ~$589,214 in reported public-service cost savings.

**Compton Pledge** (~695–800 households, $300–$600/month; Balakrishnan et al. 2024); **Magnolia Mother's Trust** (Jackson, MS; among the first US guaranteed-income programs for Black mothers); **BIG:LEAP** (Los Angeles; 3,200 parents below the poverty line).

**Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend — the best real-world quasi-UBI (universal + permanent since 1982).** **Jones & Marinescu, *AEJ: Economic Policy* 14(2):315–340 (2022)** (NBER WP 24312; IZA DP 11356): the dividend had **no effect on aggregate employment** and **increased part-time work by 1.8 pp (17%)**. Interpretation: general-equilibrium demand effects offset the individual income-effect disincentive, especially in non-tradable sectors — the strongest evidence that a *universal, permanent* transfer need not reduce aggregate employment. The dividend ranges from about $1,000 to a record **$3,284 in 2022** [Mike Dunleavy][ref-20] (including a $650 energy-relief supplement — the largest in the program's history). Related work links the PFD to reduced poverty among Indigenous Alaskans (Berman 2018) and to child-health outcomes.

**Expanded Child Tax Credit 2021 (quasi-experiment).** The 2021 CTC made the credit fully refundable and removed the earnings phase-in — the feature critics said would cut work.

- **Ananat, Glasner, Hamilton, Parolin & Pignatti, *Journal of Public Economics* 238 (2024)**: "very small, inconsistently signed, statistically insignificant impacts" on parental labor-force participation.
- **Enriquez, Jones & Tedeschi (*AEA Papers & Proceedings* 113:401–405, 2023)**: replicated the no-significant-response finding.
- **Corinth, Meyer, Stadnicki & Wu (NBER WP 29366, 2021)**: by contrast, *simulated* that a permanent expansion would lead ~1.5 million parents (2.6%) to exit; Bastian (2022) estimated ~350,000–413,000. The gap is driven by assumed labor-supply elasticities. **Real-world evidence shows little/no employment effect; the large negative predictions are model-based.** The expansion is credited with cutting child poverty and food insufficiency (Parolin et al. 2021).

**Guaranteed-income pilot wave and political backlash**: The Center for Guaranteed Income Research (Penn; Castro & West) and the Guaranteed Income Pilots Dashboard track a large wave of programs since 2018. Mayors for a Guaranteed Income (founded by former Stockton mayor Michael Tubbs) reported (June 2024) it had "supported 60+ pilots, delivering $300 million in direct, unconditional relief"; [Basicincome][ref-21] MGI director Sukhi Samra told Smart Cities Dive (2025) that "More than 150 cities have some type of guaranteed income program today." [Smart Cities Dive][ref-22] Backlash has arrived: **Arkansas (2023), then Iowa, Idaho and South Dakota (2024) passed laws barring state/local guaranteed-income payments**; Arizona, Wisconsin, Mississippi and others attempted bans; **Texas AG Ken Paxton sued Harris County** over its pilot.

### V. Developing-country benchmark (context only): GiveDirectly Kenya

**Banerjee, Faye, Krueger, Niehaus & Suri, "Universal Basic Income: Short-Term Results from a Long-Term Experiment in Kenya" (2023 working paper, not yet peer-reviewed)**: ~$0.75/adult/day across 295 villages (14,474 households), with long-term (12-year), short-term (2-year) and lump-sum arms. UBI villages saw economic expansion (more enterprises, higher revenues/net revenues); **labor supply did not fall overall but shifted from wage employment toward self-employment**. Lump sums had similar or larger effects than the small streams. Relevant to the developed-country debate mainly for showing (a) no "laziness" effect and (b) that a credible long-term/lump-sum commitment enables investment — but the poverty context is very different.

### VI. Long-term / macro considerations debated by economists (post-2015)

- **Fiscal cost & financing**: A meaningful UBI is enormously expensive. A $12,000/year UBI for all US adults would cost roughly **$2.4 trillion annually (about one-eighth of GDP)** — "nearly as large as the entire US safety net today" [Third Way][ref-03] (Third Way). CBPP's Robert Greenstein (2016) estimated a $10,000-per-person version at "more than $3 trillion a year — and $30 trillion to $40 trillion over ten years," [Center on Budget an…][ref-04] close to 100% of all federal tax revenue. Hoynes & Rothstein: a UBI large enough to raise transfers to low-income families "would be enormously expensive" [NBER][ref-23] and would direct much larger shares to childless, non-elderly, non-disabled and middle-income households. Financing options debated: VAT (Yang), income tax, carbon/land dividends.
- **General-equilibrium and inflation**: The **Roosevelt Institute (Nikiforos, Steinbaum & Zezza, 2017)**, using the Levy Institute model, projected a $1,000/month UBI could grow GDP by 12.56% and add ~4.6 million jobs — **but only if fully deficit-financed** (much smaller if tax-financed). The **Penn-Wharton Budget Model**, analyzing a deficit-funded $500/month UBI, projected GDP would *fall* 6.1% over a decade due to labor-supply and crowding-out effects. The divergence turns on assumptions about slack, elasticities and debt — illustrating how model-dependent macro claims are.
- **Poverty-reduction efficiency critique**: **Greenstein (CBPP, 2016)**: converting targeted transfers into universal payments "redistribut\[es\] income upward," so a politically feasible UBI "would likelier increase poverty than reduce it"; US means-tested programs have low administrative costs (1–9%). **Luke Martinelli (University of Bath)** modeled three UBI schemes and found all left significant numbers worse off — "an affordable UBI is inadequate, and an adequate UBI is unaffordable."
- **Substitute vs complement**: Whether UBI should replace or supplement the existing welfare state is central; most economists (Hoynes & Rothstein; Greenstein) favor targeted transfers or expanded EITC/CTC over a pure UBI on distributional grounds.
- **Automation/technological unemployment**: The Silicon Valley rationale (Altman, Yang) is that AI/automation will require UBI; Daron Acemoglu is skeptical UBI is the right response, favoring policies that steer technology toward labor. Advocates such as Guy Standing and Philippe Van Parijs argue universality builds durable political coalitions and reduces stigma (supported by Calnitsky's MINCOME work).
- **Political economy & durability**: The Ontario cancellation and the 2024 wave of state bans show programs are politically fragile.

## Headline findings by outcome domain

| Outcome | What the strongest evidence shows |
| --- | --- |
| **Labor supply** | Modest reductions on the intensive margin (OpenResearch: ~2–4 pp participation, −1.3–1.4 hrs/wk). Finland: no clean effect. Alaska (universal+permanent): no employment reduction, +1.8 pp part-time. MINCOME: −8 to −11 pp participation (saturation), mostly mothers/students. CTC 2021: no significant real-world effect. **No mass exit from work anywhere.** |
| **Physical health** | OpenResearch: precise null on biomarkers. Finland: better self-rated health. MINCOME: −8.5% hospitalizations (Forget), disputed. **Weak/contested.** |
| **Mental health/stress** | Short-run gains common (Finland, Stockton, OpenResearch year 1) but **fade** (OpenResearch: gone by year 2). |
| **Food security** | Consistent improvement (OpenResearch, Chelsea Eats, Stockton). **Robust.** |
| **Child/intergenerational** | Baby's First Years: suggestive EEG effects, primary endpoints largely null; MINCOME: more grade-12 completion. **Fragile.** |
| **Financial resilience/volatility** | Consistent large improvements (Stockton, OpenResearch, Chelsea). **Robust.** |
| **Temptation goods (alcohol/tobacco)** | No increase; Stockton <1%; OpenResearch found reduced problem drinking. **Robust — debunks a key critique.** |
| **Trust/stigma/social** | MINCOME: reduced stigma (Calnitsky); Finland: higher institutional trust. **Moderate.** |
| **Subjective wellbeing/agency** | Consistent improvement (Finland life satisfaction 7.3 vs 6.8; agency gains in OpenResearch). **Robust short-run.** |

## Recommendations

**For policymakers weighing UBI vs alternatives:**

1. 1. **Do not use pilots to justify a permanent, universal UBI.** They cannot answer the fiscal, general-equilibrium, or permanent-behavior questions. Treat pilot evidence as informative about *targeted, temporary cash transfers to low-income people* — which is what they actually test.
2. 2. **For poverty reduction, prioritize expanded targeted transfers** (a permanent expanded Child Tax Credit, EITC, or guaranteed income for specific vulnerable groups) over a pure UBI. The real-world 2021 CTC evidence (near-zero employment effect, large child-poverty reduction) is the strongest case that unconditional-ish cash to families works without destroying work incentives — and it is far cheaper than universal payments. This aligns with Hoynes & Rothstein and Greenstein.
3. 3. **If piloting, design for the questions that matter**: saturation sites (to capture community/GE effects), longer durations or credible permanence, adequate sample sizes, pre-registered primary endpoints, and administrative (not just self-reported) outcomes. Fund replication (per the Baby's First Years EEG debate).
4. 4. **Anticipate political fragility.** The Ontario cancellation and 2024 state bans show programs can be terminated before yielding evidence; build in legislative durability and independent evaluation contracts that survive government turnover.

**Benchmarks/thresholds that would change these recommendations:**

- If a **saturation, multi-year RCT** (or a natural experiment beyond Alaska) showed aggregate employment holding up *and* net fiscal cost being manageable under realistic tax financing, the case for scaling would strengthen materially.
- If **replicated** child-development RCTs confirmed durable cognitive/educational gains from early-childhood cash, the intergenerational ROI could justify child-focused universal payments even at high cost.
- If OpenResearch's forthcoming peer-reviewed longer-horizon results reversed the null health findings, the "cash as health policy" case would revive.

## Caveats and open questions

- **Working-paper status**: The OpenResearch (NBER 2024) and GiveDirectly Kenya (2023) headline results are working papers not yet peer-reviewed; treat effect sizes as provisional. Note the internal discrepancy in the OpenResearch participation estimate (2 pp in the summary vs 4.1 pp in the WP 32719 abstract).
- **Non-experimental/self-selected evidence** (Ontario; some Stockton framing) cannot support causal claims and is frequently over-interpreted by advocates.
- **The Forget vs. reanalysis dispute** on MINCOME hospitalization is unresolved.
- **Publication and advocacy bias**: many pilots are run/funded by pro-UBI organizations; the strongest independent RCTs (OpenResearch, Alaska, CTC studies) give more sober results than advocacy summaries.
- **Genuinely unanswered by all existing evidence**: (1) the general-equilibrium wage/price/rent effects of a truly universal transfer; (2) the labor-supply response to a *permanent* (vs temporary) UBI; (3) whether tax financing at the required scale would erode the gains; (4) long-run intergenerational effects; (5) whether UBI would strengthen or erode the existing welfare state politically and fiscally. Pilots, by design, cannot resolve these — the central limitation of the entire evidence base.
