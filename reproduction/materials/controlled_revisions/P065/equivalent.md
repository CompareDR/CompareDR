# Voting Age Limits in Democratic Elections: Minimum Ages, Maximum Ages, and Better Alternatives

## Executive Summary

Whether democracies should impose age limits on voting involves two analytically distinct questions. A **minimum voting age** asks when a person should first enter the electorate; a **maximum voting age** asks whether political rights should expire because of old age. The evidence and normative considerations point in different directions for those two questions.

The strongest general conclusion is that **a minimum age remains defensible, but age eighteen has no uniquely compelling scientific status**. Developmental research finds that capacities relevant to deliberate, reasoned decisions reach adult-like levels for many adolescents by roughly age sixteen, while psychosocial capacities connected to impulse control, susceptibility to peer influence, and emotionally charged risk-taking continue developing beyond eighteen. The distinction matters because voting is ordinarily closer to a deliberative, low-immediacy decision than to the emotionally aroused situations in which adolescent–adult differences are largest. A major multinational study of 5,227 people aged 10–30 in eleven countries found little further improvement in measured cognitive capacity after about sixteen, while psychosocial maturity continued developing beyond eighteen. It did not, however, test voting competence directly and therefore cannot by itself determine the correct franchise age. [[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6551607/)

Election evidence makes **sixteen a credible alternative to eighteen**. Austria, which has allowed voting from sixteen in national elections since 2007, provides the most studied case. Research there found that the quality of 16–17-year-olds' vote choices—measured by congruence between their political preferences and party choices—was similar to that of older voters, even though youth turnout remained below that of much of the older electorate. Other Austrian research finds that 16–17-year-olds may participate at rates comparable with or better than voters who first become eligible at eighteen, when residential mobility and life transitions are greater. [[2]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261379412000212) Comparative research likewise does not identify an abrupt competence discontinuity at eighteen. [[3]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-political-science-revue-canadienne-de-science-politique/article/lowering-the-voting-age-to-16-a-comparative-study-on-the-political-competence-and-engagement-of-underage-and-adult-youth/0E864E593EF252197F913616F97CE75D)

Evidence is weaker for extending the national franchise substantially **below sixteen**. Cognitive and media-discernment abilities continue developing strongly during early and middle adolescence; a 2025 study, for example, found increasing truth discernment from age eleven into adulthood. That finding does not establish electoral incapacity among younger adolescents, but it reinforces the greater uncertainty surrounding a threshold such as twelve or fourteen. [[4]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-90427-z) Accordingly, sixteen is presently the lowest threshold for which there is a meaningful body of real-world national-election experience and directly relevant empirical research.

By contrast, **there is no persuasive case for a maximum voting age**. Laboratory and everyday decision-making studies do show average age-related declines in some cognitively demanding tasks, especially those relying on working memory, fluid reasoning, or the application of complex decision rules. Yet these effects are heterogeneous: some decision abilities remain stable, knowledge and experience can compensate for declining fluid abilities, and longitudinal research finds small or absent declines on several dimensions of decision competence. Chronological age is therefore an unusually crude proxy for individual competence. [[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20545413/)

Nor does misinformation research justify an upper limit. In the 2016 U.S. Facebook study by Guess, Nagler and Tucker, users over sixty-five shared nearly seven times as many links from identified fake-news domains as the youngest group, although such sharing was uncommon overall. [[6]](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/less-than-you-think-prevalence-and-predictors-of-fake-news-dissem/) Other studies, however, find that older respondents can be **better** than younger adults at distinguishing true from false headlines; large German and British surveys found improved fake-news detection among older respondents, and recent reviews emphasize that the relationship between age and misinformation susceptibility depends substantially on the outcome measured—belief, discernment, confidence, exposure, or sharing. [[7]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-34402-6) Selecting one behavioral vulnerability as grounds for disenfranchisement would therefore be scientifically arbitrary as well as normatively dangerous.

International electoral practice reflects this asymmetry. Eighteen remains the predominant voting age, but national or supranational voting at sixteen now exists in Austria, Malta and several other settings, while Greece uses seventeen and Singapore remains an example of a twenty-one-year threshold. [[8]](https://www.oesterreich.gv.at/en/themen/transparenz_und_partizipation_in_der_demokratie/demokratie-und-wahlen/wahlen/1/Seite.320210) The comparative survey undertaken for this report found **no contemporary democratic national-election system with a general upper-age cutoff**. Brazil and Argentina illustrate something importantly different: people above seventy retain the vote but receive an exemption from otherwise compulsory voting obligations. [[9]](https://international.tse.jus.br/en/elections/voters-abroad)

International human-rights doctrine likewise treats inclusion as the baseline. The UN Human Rights Committee interprets Article 25 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights as allowing only objective and reasonable restrictions and identifies a minimum age as potentially reasonable, while rejecting literacy, educational and property requirements. [[10]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=qV9dR8wqMb8BPrsp39jKyXmKRz7aYuI2KzClF9P0NIH4Q6Ao30lXBDMMy1gIhWRIAfi%2BzKpGFNh8dUrIfgfVAPbJXDkb6q3Q9rBb7RDPtyw%3D) The Venice Commission states that universal suffrage may have a minimum age but that the right should be acquired no later than the age of majority; it simultaneously defines equal suffrage as each voter having, in principle, one vote. [[11]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e)

A related legal development strongly counsels against replacing chronological age limits with **political-knowledge or cognitive-competence tests**. Such tests create measurement problems, invite partisan manipulation and can reproduce educational, socioeconomic and disability inequalities. The UN Human Rights Committee rejects literacy and education requirements for voting, while the United States' Voting Rights Act permanently prohibits literacy, educational-achievement and knowledge tests as voting prerequisites—a response to their discriminatory history. [[12]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=qV9dR8wqMb8BPrsp39jKyXmKRz7aYuI2KzClF9P0NIH4Q6Ao30lXBDMMy1gIhWRIAfi%2BzKpGFNh8dUrIfgfVAPbJXDkb6q3Q9rBb7RDPtyw%3D) Disability law reinforces the point: although older standards such as the Venice Code and the Human Rights Committee's 1996 General Comment contemplated individualized deprivation for established mental incapacity, the later UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and its Committee have moved toward a much more inclusionary position. In *Bujdosó v. Hungary*, the CRPD Committee held that Article 29 provides no disability-group exception and rejected voting-capacity assessments, emphasizing accessible procedures and assistance instead. [[13]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=QFaC%2Fi9AOTk6l8Dh0NwBXr3tgTqon0X86ewcQ1E7Elcmt0UmKYVUHRqE5ZeuQ31eHq5kzfesRnSZQeX8SLmqMOlm2VZQOPaah56hfzzKodU%3D)

**Recommended general model:** absent jurisdiction-specific constitutional constraints, a democracy designing its franchise today should adopt **a minimum voting age of sixteen and no maximum voting age**, accompanied by strong civic and media education, preregistration or automatic registration, accessible voting, safeguards against coercion and an evaluation process after implementation. Retaining eighteen is still a defensible policy choice where reform lacks political or institutional support; what the evidence does *not* support is treating eighteen as a scientifically demonstrated threshold of political competence.

A maximum age should not be introduced. Competency tests, weighted votes and parental extra votes should likewise be rejected as general franchise rules. Intergenerational inequities are better addressed by adding younger citizens to the electorate where justified, reducing unequal turnout, and establishing future-generations or youth institutions than by giving unequal voting power to different age classes.

## Background and Democratic-Legal Framework

The starting point in modern democratic theory is not that citizens earn voting rights by demonstrating expertise. It is that citizens subject to collective decisions presumptively possess equal political status. Universal suffrage operationalizes that status by making exclusion exceptional; equal suffrage adds the principle that the included citizens participate on comparable terms. The Venice Commission describes Europe's electoral heritage in terms of universal, equal, free, secret and direct suffrage and states that, in principle, universal suffrage means that all human beings have the right to vote and stand for election. [[11]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e)

The ICCPR similarly guarantees political participation through universal and equal suffrage. The Human Rights Committee's General Comment No. 25 accepts that voting rights can be regulated, but conditions must be based on objective and reasonable criteria. It specifically treats a minimum voting age as potentially reasonable while saying that unreasonable requirements include literacy, educational and property qualifications. The Committee also stresses voter education and registration as ways of enabling effective participation rather than narrowing the electorate. [[10]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=qV9dR8wqMb8BPrsp39jKyXmKRz7aYuI2KzClF9P0NIH4Q6Ao30lXBDMMy1gIhWRIAfi%2BzKpGFNh8dUrIfgfVAPbJXDkb6q3Q9rBb7RDPtyw%3D)

This produces a central distinction between **capacity as a policy rationale** and **capacity as an individual precondition for citizenship**. A minimum age operates as a categorical proxy: society does not test every seven-, twelve- or sixteen-year-old separately. Such bright lines sacrifice individualized accuracy for predictability, administrability and protection against politically manipulated assessments. The question is therefore not whether every person immediately below a threshold is less capable than every person immediately above it—clearly that will never be true—but whether the boundary is a sufficiently reasonable approximation to independent political agency to justify excluding an entire class.

Age eighteen became widespread partly because voting-age reforms were historically tied to changes in the broader age of majority rather than because neuroscience identified eighteen as a biological transition point. The Venice Commission's contemporary standard reflects that institutional logic: there should be a minimum age, but the vote should be obtained **no later than** the age of civil majority. [[16]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e) The existence of countries voting at sixteen or seventeen further demonstrates that democracy does not require one universal number. [[17]](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/factsheets/en/sheet/21/the-european-parliament-electoral-procedures)

There are at least four democratic reasons for considering a lower threshold. First, laws governing education, taxation, public transport, digital regulation, climate policy and public debt affect teenagers directly and often for longer than they affect today's oldest voters. Second, political equality generally weighs against exclusion where the excluded group can form and communicate interests. Third, first voting experiences occurring while young citizens remain embedded in schools and households may make registration and civic socialization easier than first eligibility during the highly mobile 18–24 life stage. Austrian research and life-cycle turnout studies give some support to that mechanism. [[18]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4864896/) Fourth, in ageing societies the electorate can become increasingly weighted toward older cohorts even without deliberate discrimination; the OECD identifies population ageing and declining political weight of younger generations as an emerging intergenerational-governance issue. [[19]](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/youth-empowerment-and-intergenerational-justice.html)

The argument for retaining a threshold is nevertheless substantial. Very young children do not yet exercise independent political agency comparable to adults; family control is especially relevant where voters live under parental authority; factual comprehension and abstract reasoning develop through adolescence; and an election administrator needs a rule that can be verified without subjective screening. The difficult issue is where those considerations cease to outweigh political inclusion.

Maximum ages involve a fundamentally different burden of proof. Unlike childhood, old age does not place every member of an age category in a developmental stage preceding autonomous agency. Ageing produces highly variable trajectories. An eighty-five-year-old may be cognitively intact and politically knowledgeable, while a much younger adult may have serious cognitive impairments, profound political ignorance or susceptibility to manipulation. A maximum age therefore removes an existing fundamental political status based on a proxy that becomes less, rather than more, reliable at the individual level.

The disability-law evolution illustrates why this matters. The Venice Commission's older electoral code permits withdrawal of voting rights for mental incapacity only when provided by law, proportionate and expressly imposed by a court. [[11]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e) The CRPD Committee has taken a more demanding approach: in *Bujdosó v. Hungary*, it said Article 29 contains no reasonable restriction or exception for a group of persons with disabilities, and it emphasized accessible and understandable voting procedures and assistance requested by the voter rather than exclusion through capacity assessments. [[20]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=QFaC%2Fi9AOTk6l8Dh0NwBXr3tgTqon0X86ewcQ1E7Elcmt0UmKYVUHRqE5ZeuQ31eHq5kzfesRnSZQeX8SLmqMOlm2VZQOPaah56hfzzKodU%3D) Its later guidance characterizes exclusion from electoral processes connected with denial of legal capacity as a frequent form of disability discrimination and calls for reform of systematically exclusionary laws and accessible elections. [[21]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=9SDS8tooB0EDg%2BceXbuvCdyv%2FJCvJ1oajr2we13bTEG8wCq%2BEMy9c1nfj90tZW%2FNGPTiVltYL3Yz9xYVTlN5eQ%3D%3D)

Thus there is some tension between older electoral standards that tolerate narrowly individualized incapacity restrictions and the CRPD's newer disability-rights paradigm. A jurisdiction considering a maximum age would go considerably further than either: instead of a judicially assessed individual restriction, it would presume incapacity from date of birth alone.

## Literature Review: Development, Ageing, and Decision Competence

**Adolescent cognitive development.** Modern developmental science strongly cautions against treating maturity as one single trait. Icenogle and colleagues distinguish “cognitive capacity”—including reasoning and working-memory abilities relevant to deliberate decisions—from “psychosocial maturity,” including impulse control, future orientation, resistance to peer influence and behavior under emotionally arousing conditions. In their multinational sample, measured cognitive capacity largely reached adult levels around sixteen, whereas psychosocial maturity continued developing beyond eighteen. [[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6551607/)

This matters to voting-age analysis because casting a ballot is usually a **“cold” or deliberative decision**: citizens can consider alternatives for days or weeks, consult information and choose without an immediate physical reward, peer confrontation or split-second response. Developmental findings concerning adolescent driving, criminal behavior or high-arousal risk-taking therefore cannot automatically be transferred to voting. At the same time, campaigns can be emotional, partisan and socially influenced, so the distinction should not be overstated. The scientific conclusion is narrower: developmental neuroscience does not supply a crisp biological argument for eighteen rather than sixteen.

Direct electoral research provides a more appropriate test. Wagner, Johann and Kritzinger examined Austria after nationwide enfranchisement at sixteen. Their study found that although younger citizens' turnout was relatively low compared with the whole electorate, their lower participation could not simply be explained by deficient ability or motivation, and the quality of their vote choices was comparable with that of older voters. [[22]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261379412000212) A review of Austria's longer experience likewise describes the accumulated evidence as generally encouraging for voting at sixteen. [[23]](https://ucrisportal.univie.ac.at/en/publications/voting-at-16-in-practice-a-review-of-the-austrian-case/)

Mahéo and Bélanger's comparative study addresses political competence and engagement among underage and adult youth and reinforces the point that the boundary at eighteen does not correspond to an obvious empirical competence cliff. [[3]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-political-science-revue-canadienne-de-science-politique/article/lowering-the-voting-age-to-16-a-comparative-study-on-the-political-competence-and-engagement-of-underage-and-adult-youth/0E864E593EF252197F913616F97CE75D) Evidence outside Austria is not uniformly favorable, however. Norwegian results reported in the comparative literature have sometimes found weaker attitude–vote consistency among 16–17-year-olds than among older voters, demonstrating that institutional and educational context matters. [[24]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1007/s12290-013-0273-3)

**Does enfranchisement itself create competence?** There are competing theories. A “use it or learn it” hypothesis predicts that gaining the vote gives adolescents incentives to follow politics. A quasi-experiment in Ghent found an effect of youth enfranchisement on attention to politics but no general increase across its broader political-engagement measure. [[25]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/voting-at-16-does-lowering-the-voting-age-lead-to-more-political-engagement-evidence-from-a-quasiexperiment-in-the-city-of-ghent-belgium/172A2D9B75ECB66E98C9680787F302AD) Rosenqvist's Swedish regression-discontinuity analysis found no evidence that early voting eligibility itself causally increased political knowledge, weakening the stronger claim that merely giving sixteen-year-olds the vote will automatically make them substantially more informed. [[26]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-political-science/article/rising-to-the-occasion-youth-political-knowledge-and-the-voting-age/B52EB949B70DED5EACDA6B2932D3C017)

The more plausible policy interpretation is therefore interactive: **enfranchisement creates an opportunity for civic learning, but institutions determine whether it is used**. Schools, election authorities, parties, families and media environments can make the first eligible election salient; simply changing a statute without those complementary measures should not be expected to transform political knowledge.

Political preference stability also develops gradually rather than appearing at eighteen. A longitudinal study of Dutch participants aged twelve to thirty-one found increasing stability of economic-egalitarian and ethnocentric attitudes with age. [[27]](https://timothydavidson.com/Library/Articles/01.%20OLD%20CurriculumArticles-coursework/7.Rekker-2015-PoliticalAttitudesInAdolescence.pdf) But preference stability is a questionable franchise criterion. Democracies do not disenfranchise adults for changing party allegiance or opinions. Indeed, responsiveness to new information is often desirable. The relevant question is whether a citizen can meaningfully express a present preference, not whether that preference will remain unchanged for decades.

**Ageing and cognition.** Research on older adults paints an equally non-binary picture. Finucane and Gullion developed behavioral decision-competence measures with 205 younger adults aged 25–45, 208 adults aged 65–74 and 198 adults aged 75–97. Performance on demanding health, financial and nutrition decisions generally declined across age groups, but substantial variance was connected to health, social characteristics, numeracy and basic cognitive abilities rather than chronological age alone. [[28]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20545413/)

Rosi and colleagues similarly found that older adults performed worse when asked to apply decision rules to multiattribute choices; working memory and verbal fluency helped explain those differences. [[29]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29283001/) Such results are relevant because evaluating parties sometimes involves comparing complex policy packages. They nevertheless do not show that elderly citizens cannot identify their interests, values or preferred representatives, nor do the laboratory tasks establish a threshold beyond which voting decisions cease to be meaningful.

Longitudinal evidence further weakens any simple maximum-age inference. Del Missier and colleagues found small age-related decline in resistance to framing but no longitudinal decline in applying decision rules or resistance to sunk costs in their sample. [[30]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31971414/) Broader reviews emphasize that fluid cognitive abilities tend to decline with age while experience, accumulated knowledge, emotion regulation and some less cognitively demanding decision skills can remain stable or improve. [[31]](https://pensionresearchcouncil.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/PRC-CP-2016-01-Bruine-de-Bruin-Text.pdf) This heterogeneity makes calendar age an especially poor classifier.

An important principle follows: **evidence of an average group difference is not the same as evidence supporting group disenfranchisement**. To justify an upper cutoff of, for example, eighty-five, policymakers would need evidence not merely that average performance at eighty-five is lower than at fifty, but that chronological age reliably identifies people incapable of meaningful political choice, that the cost of their inclusion exceeds the cost of excluding all competent people above the threshold, and that no less restrictive intervention works. The current literature comes nowhere close to satisfying that standard.

## Comparative Legal Survey

International practice strongly converges on **minimum but not maximum** voting ages. Eighteen remains the dominant model; sixteen and seventeen are increasingly visible exceptions, while a few systems retain higher thresholds. European Parliament rules currently use sixteen in Austria, Belgium, Germany and Malta, seventeen in Greece and eighteen elsewhere in the European Union, though national rules can differ from European-election rules. [[17]](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/factsheets/en/sheet/21/the-european-parliament-electoral-procedures)

Two comparative conclusions are especially significant.

First, **legal adulthood is not universally treated as an indivisible package**. Germany, Belgium, Scotland, Wales and European elections show that different levels of government can use different thresholds. Austria demonstrates that national voting at sixteen can coexist with other age-specific rules without requiring every civil, criminal and commercial age boundary to move simultaneously. [[43]](https://www.oesterreich.gv.at/en/themen/transparenz_und_partizipation_in_der_demokratie/demokratie-und-wahlen/wahlen/1/Seite.320210)

Second, the survey finds no modern national analogue to a proposed upper franchise cutoff such as seventy-five, eighty or eighty-five. Brazil and Argentina provide the closest *age-related upper rule*, but their policy goes in the opposite direction: older people are relieved of an obligation to participate while retaining the right to participate. [[9]](https://international.tse.jus.br/en/elections/voters-abroad) That distinction is normatively important. Making voting voluntary for a group recognizes possible mobility, health or administrative burdens; removing its vote declares the group's political judgment categorically unworthy of representation.

**Guardianship and cognitive disability are a separate comparative issue.** Older electoral standards permitted narrowly controlled disenfranchisement after findings of mental incapacity, and some national systems historically tied voting rights to guardianship status. The CRPD framework increasingly treats automatic—and even disability-specific individualized—capacity exclusion as inconsistent with equal political participation. In *Bujdosó*, the CRPD Committee explicitly favored accessible procedures and assistance over capacity-based disenfranchisement. [[13]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=QFaC%2Fi9AOTk6l8Dh0NwBXr3tgTqon0X86ewcQ1E7Elcmt0UmKYVUHRqE5ZeuQ31eHq5kzfesRnSZQeX8SLmqMOlm2VZQOPaah56hfzzKodU%3D) A maximum age justified as an indirect dementia screen would therefore sit uneasily with the direction of modern disability-rights law.

## Empirical Evidence Synthesis

The empirical record is most useful when separated into four outcomes: **participation, political knowledge/vote quality, misinformation vulnerability, and decision competence**. None supports a simple theory in which political ability rises sharply at eighteen and then falls below an acceptable level at some old-age boundary.

**Participation follows a life-cycle curve more than a competence threshold.** Younger adults commonly vote less often than middle-aged and older adults. That fact is routinely used against lowering the voting age, but it is not evidence that young citizens are unable to vote competently. Residential mobility, registration costs, weaker partisan attachment and the disruption of education-to-work transitions are important life-cycle factors. Conversely, very high turnout among older voters does not prove greater cognitive competence. Turnout is behavior, not a cognitive examination.

New Zealand's 2023 general election offers a useful official illustration. Among **enrolled voters**, turnout initially fell after the youngest group, then increased steadily through older adulthood and peaked among 65–69-year-olds before a small decline at 70+. This pattern helps expose the weakness of both extreme positions. It would be wrong to infer that 25–29-year-olds deserve fewer political rights because they participate less; it would be equally wrong to infer that voters aged 65–69 are necessarily the most competent because they participate most. The franchise protects political status, while turnout reflects whether that status is exercised.

For sixteen-year-olds, Austrian studies are particularly informative because they observe actual election behavior rather than hypothetical survey intentions. Wagner and colleagues found no lower quality of vote choice among voters below eighteen. [[46]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261379412000212) Zeglovits and Aichholzer's broader work indicates that first eligibility at sixteen, while adolescents are often still embedded in relatively stable school and household contexts, can avoid some participation disadvantages associated with first eligibility at eighteen. [[47]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4864896/) Malta's first 2015 local elections allowing sixteen-year-olds also recorded turnout of 62.3% among the eligible 16–18 group, showing that adolescent enfranchisement need not produce mass abstention. [[48]](https://electoral.gov.mt/elections)

The evidence on **political knowledge** is more mixed, which is one reason reform should not be oversold. The Swedish natural experiment studied by Rosenqvist did not find that earlier eligibility caused a measurable increase in political knowledge. [[49]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-political-science/article/rising-to-the-occasion-youth-political-knowledge-and-the-voting-age/B52EB949B70DED5EACDA6B2932D3C017) Ghent's quasi-experiment detected increased political attention but not a general increase in the broader engagement index. [[25]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/voting-at-16-does-lowering-the-voting-age-lead-to-more-political-engagement-evidence-from-a-quasiexperiment-in-the-city-of-ghent-belgium/172A2D9B75ECB66E98C9680787F302AD) These studies imply that voting rights alone are not a substitute for civic education.

But political knowledge also cannot coherently function as an individualized franchise standard unless democracies are willing to test every adult. Many adults know little about institutions, candidates or policies; political ignorance is not unique to teenagers. A rule that excludes a knowledgeable seventeen-year-old while automatically including a poorly informed forty-year-old cannot honestly be described as an individualized competence rule—it is an administratively convenient age rule. That may still be justified, but the justification must be defended as such.

**Misinformation evidence is particularly damaging to attempts to derive franchise rules from isolated cognitive vulnerabilities.** Guess, Nagler and Tucker found a strong association between age and sharing fake-news-domain links in their 2016 U.S. Facebook sample: users over sixty-five shared nearly seven times as many as the youngest users after accounting for several covariates. Yet false-news sharing was rare overall. [[50]](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/less-than-you-think-prevalence-and-predictors-of-fake-news-dissem/)

A different conclusion emerges when researchers measure *discernment* rather than social-media sharing. Surveys in Germany and the United Kingdom found older respondents better at identifying fake news, while other work reviews findings in which older adults distinguish true from false headlines at least as well as or better than younger adults. [[51]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-34402-6) A large systematic review and meta-analysis of news judgments emphasizes the need to separate successful discrimination between true and false information from generalized skepticism toward all news. [[52]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02086-1)

The apparent contradiction is plausible: recognizing a false headline, encountering false information online and deciding whether to click or share are different behavioral processes. Digital familiarity, partisanship, attention, social rewards and platform habits can affect sharing independently of raw reasoning ability. That makes age-based disenfranchisement a grossly overbroad remedy for misinformation.

The empirical balance can be summarized as follows:

The most important methodological caveat is that **none of these measures is identical to democratic competence**. Good democratic citizenship does not require optimizing a multiattribute laboratory problem, passing a civics examination, resisting every misleading headline or selecting the option an expert considers welfare-maximizing. Voting also expresses interests, identities, values, retrospective judgments and judgments of trust. Empirical tests can tell us whether age differences exist; they cannot by themselves decide which differences are morally sufficient to extinguish political equality.

## Ethical and Intergenerational Analysis

**Liberal egalitarianism** starts from equal civic status. Voting is primarily an institutional expression of equal membership in a political community, rather than a prize for epistemic performance. Excluding people from the franchise consequently requires more justification than an ordinary administrative age boundary.

A minimum age can meet that standard when it reasonably approximates emerging autonomous agency and avoids a more intrusive individualized assessment. Once a group can understand political alternatives and communicate interests, however, equality favors a lower threshold. Evidence of adult-like deliberative capacity and coherent voting among many 16–17-year-olds weakens exclusion justified merely by the historical familiarity of eighteen. [[56]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6551607/)

On the same reasoning, a maximum age is strongly objectionable. It would remove equal political standing because a demographic group has a higher average probability of impairment. Adults in educational, occupational, or socioeconomic groups statistically associated with lower political knowledge are not excluded on the same basis. Applying that competence rationale selectively to old age demands an account of why age correlations uniquely warrant collective disenfranchisement.

**Utilitarian or consequentialist analysis** places more emphasis on election outcomes. It could, in principle, justify an age restriction if excluding a voter category reliably yielded substantially better policies, less manipulation, and greater aggregate welfare. That starting point seems more hospitable to competence thresholds.

The practical epistemic obstacles are severe. No politically neutral measure of the “correct” vote exists; policy preferences include legitimate conflicts about distribution and values; errors by voters may offset rather than accumulate; tests misclassify in both directions; and test designers can shape eligibility. A full welfare assessment must also count delegitimized government and systematic exclusion of affected citizens. Broad upper-age exclusions are highly unlikely to survive that assessment.

Screening for competence also creates Goodhart-like incentives. If a state examination determines political power, political actors have reasons to manipulate its curriculum, difficulty, language, and scoring. U.S. experience shows that literacy and knowledge tests can disenfranchise targeted populations. The Voting Rights Act accordingly permanently prohibits specified tests and devices nationwide, including educational-achievement and knowledge requirements. [[57]](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-files-statement-interest-regarding-section-201-voting-rights-act-0)

**Republican theories of citizenship** stress participation in collective self-government and freedom from arbitrary domination. Excluding people governed by the state raises the danger that others rule them without adequate political contestation. Young people affected by schools, policing, transport, and climate policy, and elderly people affected by pensions and health policy, have interests in precisely those areas where unrepresented government could dominate them.

Republicanism still values civic competence. Its usual response, though, is to develop citizens, institutions, and public deliberation, rather than select an electorate for presumed expertise. Civic education, accessible information, public forums, and institutional checks therefore fit this view better than examinations or age ceilings.

**Intergenerational equity** offers a particularly strong case for lowering the youth franchise, but not for weighting votes. Climate mitigation, sovereign debt, infrastructure, housing, pensions, and education allocate benefits and burdens across decades. Population ageing can increase older cohorts' numerical and participatory influence; the OECD identifies the declining relative political weight of younger people as a governance challenge. [[19]](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/youth-empowerment-and-intergenerational-justice.html)

Average policy preferences also vary by age. Comparative research, after efforts to separate lifecycle from cohort effects, finds older respondents more supportive of health and pension spending and less supportive of education spending. [[58]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0176268012000596) This should not be reduced to a zero-sum conflict between uniform young and old electorates. Parents and grandparents care about schools and climate; young citizens anticipate pensions and healthcare. Income, ideology, education, and partisanship often account for more political variation than age. Research also emphasizes the difficulty of disentangling ageing, cohort, and period effects. [[59]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-political-science-review/article/generations-and-age-in-electoral-research/CD7BA277B821017B5166E221290184D0)

Reducing the threshold from eighteen to sixteen has a modest, conceptually defensible intergenerational effect: it includes people already governed who will live longest with many current decisions. It still cannot represent people not yet born. Long-horizon institutions—future-generations commissioners, fiscal sustainability assessments, climate budgets, youth assemblies, or long-term impact statements—are needed for that problem.

**Weighted voting** and the Demeny proposal of parental extra votes are harder to justify for the same reason. Their attempt to rebalance demographics abandons equal voting power. Under the Venice Commission's formulation, each voter has, in principle, one vote, and where multiple votes exist, all voters have the same number. [[60]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e) Extra votes exercised by a parent enlarge that parent's power without necessarily representing the child's interests or future preferences.

**Proxy voting** and supported voting must also remain distinct. A proxy can be useful when an already-enfranchised voter authorizes someone to carry out the physical act of voting under strictly regulated conditions. The Venice Commission requires strict rules for proxies and separately bars family voting or control of another person's ballot. [[60]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e) That differs from permitting parents to determine votes for children legally excluded from the franchise. For disabled or cognitively impaired adults, the CRPD approach supports assistance that enables the citizen to make their own choice, not replacement with someone else's political judgment. [[20]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=QFaC%2Fi9AOTk6l8Dh0NwBXr3tgTqon0X86ewcQ1E7Elcmt0UmKYVUHRqE5ZeuQ31eHq5kzfesRnSZQeX8SLmqMOlm2VZQOPaah56hfzzKodU%3D)

## Policy Options, Recommendations, and Implementation

The evidence favors reforms that improve the quality and equality of **participation** over policies that attempt to purify the electorate.

| Policy option | Principal advantages | Principal disadvantages / risks | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Keep minimum at 18; no maximum** | Familiar; administratively simple; often aligned with civil majority; little transition cost. | Excludes 16–17-year-olds despite evidence of meaningful deliberative capacity; first vote often falls during a mobile life stage. | **Defensible, but not uniquely evidence-based.** |
| **Minimum 16; no maximum** | Expands representation; supported by Austrian experience; can integrate first voting with school civic education; improves intergenerational inclusion. | Requires registration, curricula and election administration changes; concerns about family influence and developmental variation remain. | **Preferred general model.** |
| **Minimum below 16** | Greater democratic inclusion; gives younger adolescents direct representation. | Much thinner real-election evidence; larger developmental differences; stronger dependence/family-coercion concerns. | **Not recommended nationally at present; pilots/research could be justified.** |
| **Maximum age, e.g. 80 or 85** | Very simple proxy for increasing population risk of cognitive impairment. | Excludes large numbers of competent citizens; age is highly imprecise; serious equality and discrimination concerns; no meaningful modern democratic precedent identified. | **Reject.** |
| **Individual cognitive or political-knowledge test** | Appears more individualized than an age ceiling. | No neutral definition of sufficient political competence; false classifications; high administrative burden; discriminatory potential; historically abused; conflicts with major rights standards. [[12]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=qV9dR8wqMb8BPrsp39jKyXmKRz7aYuI2KzClF9P0NIH4Q6Ao30lXBDMMy1gIhWRIAfi%2BzKpGFNh8dUrIfgfVAPbJXDkb6q3Q9rBb7RDPtyw%3D) | **Reject.** |
| **Universal civic education** | Raises competence without exclusion; particularly useful before first election; benefits every cohort. | Quality varies; risk of politicized curriculum; benefits require sustained implementation. | **Strongly recommend.** |
| **Media-literacy / misinformation resilience programs** | Addresses a genuine vulnerability across ages; experimental interventions can improve discernment. [[61]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abo6254) | Poorly designed interventions can induce excessive skepticism toward true information. [[62]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01884-x) | **Recommend with evaluation and political neutrality.** |
| **Pre-registration / automatic registration** | Reduces administrative barriers, particularly for first-time voters; can establish voting habits. | Requires reliable civil registers and privacy safeguards. | **Strongly recommend alongside age reform.** |
| **Compulsory voting** | Can substantially raise participation and reduce turnout inequality; longstanding systems such as Australia demonstrate feasibility. | Restricts abstention; enforcement and invalid-ballot effects require care; does not itself guarantee informed voting. [[63]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/politically-unengaged-distrusting-and-disaffected-individuals-drive-the-link-between-compulsory-voting-and-invalid-balloting/9DDF631F6ADDE16907A75C37B7FC3AFE) | **Jurisdiction-dependent; addresses participation, not age competence.** |
| **Supported/assisted voting** | Protects inclusion for disability, frailty or literacy barriers while preserving the voter's choice. | Requires strict secrecy and anti-coercion safeguards. | **Recommend.** |
| **Proxy votes for children** | Gives some formal political weight to children's interests. | Parent's preference is substituted for child's; unequal voting power; advantages adults with children. | **Reject as a substitute for direct suffrage.** |
| **Age-weighted voting** | Could mathematically rebalance generations or long-term stakes. | Violates political equality; politically manipulable; no principled weighting formula. | **Reject.** |
| **Youth / future-generations councils** | Adds structured input on long-term policy without altering equal suffrage; useful for those below voting age and future-oriented issues. | Advisory bodies can be ignored; selection must be representative. | **Recommend as a complement, not substitute, for voting rights.** |

**Recommended franchise rule.** On the evidence currently available, the best general rule is:

**All citizens should acquire the vote at sixteen and should retain it for life, subject only to the same narrow, lawful and rights-compatible eligibility conditions applicable without discrimination to the rest of the electorate.**

The case for sixteen is not that every sixteen-year-old is as knowledgeable as every adult. No voting-age threshold can satisfy that criterion. The stronger argument is that sixteen is the lowest age for which developmental evidence, meaningful real-world national-election experience and empirical studies of vote choice jointly make categorical exclusion difficult to justify. [[64]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6551607/)

Eighteen remains a reasonable fallback because electoral age rules inevitably contain a normative judgment and because the empirical literature cannot identify an objectively optimal boundary. A jurisdiction should not describe eighteen, however, as the point at which a scientifically immature brain becomes an adult voting brain. The evidence does not support that claim.

**No maximum age should be enacted.** Where advanced age creates difficulties, election law should respond to the *difficulty*: accessible polling stations, postal or mobile voting under anti-fraud safeguards, large-print and audio ballots, accessible information, transportation, supported voting and protections against undue influence. The proper response to cognitive disability should follow applicable human-rights law and favor support for the voter's own decision rather than a blanket chronological presumption. [[65]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=9SDS8tooB0EDg%2BceXbuvCdyv%2FJCvJ1oajr2we13bTEG8wCq%2BEMy9c1nfj90tZW%2FNGPTiVltYL3Yz9xYVTlN5eQ%3D%3D)

**Do not replace age limits with competence tests.** A political test faces an unavoidable question: competence to do *what*? Recall the constitution? Understand fiscal policy? Identify misinformation? Calculate probabilities? Explain party manifestos? Rational citizens can reasonably disagree over which knowledge matters. Once passing the test becomes a condition of political equality, its design becomes itself an object of partisan contestation. The international rejection of literacy/educational qualifications and the U.S. prohibition of voting knowledge tests reflect this danger. [[12]](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=qV9dR8wqMb8BPrsp39jKyXmKRz7aYuI2KzClF9P0NIH4Q6Ao30lXBDMMy1gIhWRIAfi%2BzKpGFNh8dUrIfgfVAPbJXDkb6q3Q9rBb7RDPtyw%3D)

**Address misinformation across the life course.** Neither the youngest nor oldest voters should be singled out as inherently unreliable. Proven interventions such as appropriately designed media-literacy guidance and psychological “prebunking” or inoculation can improve resilience without removing anyone's political status. [[61]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abo6254) Because some misinformation interventions can create generalized distrust, programs should test effects on recognition of *both true and false* information rather than rewarding skepticism indiscriminately. [[62]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01884-x)

**Address intergenerational equity outside the ballot box as well.** A voting-age reform should be paired with a long-term policy impact mechanism—such as an independent commissioner for future generations or mandatory intergenerational assessments for major fiscal, pension, climate and infrastructure legislation. Youth councils can give people below sixteen a structured voice, provided governments must formally respond to their recommendations. The OECD's intergenerational-governance work supports institution-building and youth participation rather than treating electoral demographics as the only lever. [[66]](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/youth-empowerment-and-intergenerational-justice.html)

**Implementation considerations.** A move to sixteen is administratively easier than a competence regime because age is an objective civil-status fact already used in voter registration. The main work lies in revising eligibility legislation, synchronizing voter rolls, preparing election administrators, developing politically neutral civic education and protecting ballot secrecy for minors.

Federal or multilevel systems should pay particular attention to harmonization. Allowing a sixteen-year-old to vote in a municipal or regional election and then removing eligibility for a subsequent higher-level election can create what researchers call “temporary disenfranchisement.” Evidence from Germany suggests that uncoordinated voting-age reforms can have adverse effects when young voters gain and then lose eligibility across election levels. [[67]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/temporary-disenfranchisement-negative-side-effects-of-lowering-the-voting-age/326BFAF1B57C0D40767294861D4E2FD3) Harmonization is therefore preferable, though constitutional federalism may make immediate uniformity impossible.

Registration rules should specify eligibility according to the voter's age **on election day**, not simply the date the electoral roll closes. Existing electoral practice already demonstrates the use of supplementary registers for people who reach statutory voting age after a register's initial preparation; the Venice Commission expressly recognizes this possibility. [[60]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e) Pre-registration at fourteen or fifteen, followed by automatic activation at sixteen, can further reduce administrative drop-off.

Schools should provide nonpartisan instruction about institutions, parties and candidate-information sources without directing students toward particular choices. Younger voters must retain secret ballots even from parents, teachers and school administrators. The Venice Commission's prohibition of “family voting” and control of one person's vote by another is especially important when enfranchising minors who remain economically dependent on their families. [[60]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e)

A reform should also avoid being enacted immediately before an election merely to seek a perceived partisan advantage. The Venice Commission recommends stability in fundamental electoral rules, generally discouraging late alterations to core electoral law close to an election. [[60]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e) A lead time of at least one full registration and education cycle is preferable.

A practical implementation sequence would be:

| --- |

Evaluation should report, by age cohort and relevant demographic characteristics, enrolment, turnout, invalid ballots, use of accessibility measures and survey-based political knowledge while protecting secrecy and privacy. The purpose should be to improve electoral administration—not to establish a new test that individual citizens must pass.

Transitional rules should address citizens who turn sixteen between registration deadlines and election day; already issued identification documents; overseas voters; and jurisdictions whose local, regional and national thresholds differ. Electoral authorities should conduct an equality and disability impact assessment before implementation and provide simple judicial or administrative remedies for incorrectly omitted voters. These mechanisms are consistent with the Venice Commission's emphasis on reliable, regularly updated registers and procedures for correcting registration errors. [[60]](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282002%29023rev2-cor-e)

The strongest overall policy package is therefore not simply “votes at sixteen.” It is **votes at sixteen + lifetime retention of voting rights + easy registration + nonpartisan civic education + universal misinformation resilience + accessible voting + institutions for future generations**. Such a package addresses both inclusion and informed participation without making political equality contingent on age stereotypes or state examinations.
