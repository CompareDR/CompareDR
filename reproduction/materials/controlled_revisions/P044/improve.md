# Turo vs. Getaround (2020–2025): How One Car-Sharing Startup Survived and the Other Collapsed

## TL;DR

- **Turo survived and Getaround failed** because of one decisive difference: Turo built a profitable, cash-generating business (net income of $154.7 million in 2022; roughly $958 million in revenue in 2024) while Getaround never stopped losing money (a $113.95 million net loss in 2023 on just $72.68 million of revenue) and ran out of cash. Turo stayed private and pulled its planned stock-market listing; Getaround rushed onto the stock market in 2022 and was effectively worthless within two years.
- **Getaround's public debut was a disaster caused by "redemptions."** When it merged with a shell company (a SPAC) to go public in December 2022 at a claimed ~$1.2 billion value, 94.01% of the shell's public investors pulled their money out first (24,325,000 shares redeemed, "removing 94.01% of the SPAC's trust," which had held $259 million), so almost no fresh cash reached Getaround. The stock fell more than 65% on day one and later traded for fractions of a penny. Getaround wound down its US operations in February 2025 and its shareholders approved a formal dissolution on July 29, 2026 — common shareholders are expected to receive nothing.
- **Both companies were battered by the same macro forces** — the COVID-19 travel collapse in 2020, the 2021 boom and 2022–2023 bust in tech valuations, rising interest rates, and a frozen market for new stock listings — but Turo's profitability gave it the "runway" (cash to survive on) to wait out the storm, while Getaround's chronic losses left it defenseless. Turo cut about 30% of staff in 2020 and about 15% (roughly 150 people) in 2025; Getaround cut staff repeatedly (2020, 2023, 2024) before laying off substantially all its US employees in February 2025.

## Key Findings

**The two business models.** Both companies are "peer-to-peer car sharing" marketplaces — think "Airbnb for cars," where private owners rent out their vehicles to strangers through an app, and the platform takes a cut. But they targeted different needs:

- **Turo** focused on *longer, trip-based rentals* — travelers renting a car for a weekend, a vacation, or a special occasion, often a luxury or specialty vehicle, frequently near airports. This is a direct substitute for traditional rental companies like Hertz and Avis.
- **Getaround** focused on *short, instant-access urban rentals* — renting a car by the hour for errands, and increasingly renting cars to "gig" workers who drive for Uber, Lyft, or DoorDash. Its signature was "Getaround Connect," hardware installed in cars allowing keyless smartphone unlocking with no human handoff. [Bitget][ref-01]

**Jargon in plain language (used throughout this report):**

- **Venture capital (VC):** money from professional investors who fund young, fast-growing private companies in exchange for ownership stakes, betting a few big winners will pay for many losers.
- **Series A/B/C/D/E:** successive rounds of venture funding, labeled alphabetically as a company matures. Later letters usually mean larger amounts and higher company values.
- **Valuation / post-money valuation:** what investors agree a company is worth. "Post-money" is the value immediately *after* new investment is added.
- **Down round:** a funding round (or listing) at a *lower* value than a previous one — a sign the company has lost ground.
- **Dilution:** when new shares are issued, existing owners' slices of the pie shrink.
- **IPO (initial public offering):** the first time a company sells shares to the general public on a stock exchange.
- **S-1 filing:** the detailed disclosure document a US company must file with regulators before an IPO, revealing its finances and risks.
- **SPAC (special purpose acquisition company):** a "blank-check" shell company that raises cash from public investors, then merges with a real private company to take it public faster than a traditional IPO.
- **Redemptions:** SPAC investors have the right to take their money back (with interest) rather than stay invested in the merged company. High redemptions mean the target company receives far less cash than advertised.
- **Enterprise value / equity value:** measures of a whole company's worth (enterprise value roughly includes debt; equity value is the worth of the shares).
- **GMV / gross bookings (Gross Booking Value, GBV):** the total dollar value of all transactions flowing through the marketplace before the platform's cut — a measure of scale, not profit.
- **Unit economics:** whether each individual transaction makes or loses money after direct costs.
- **Burn rate / runway:** how fast a company spends cash ("burn") and how many months of cash it has left ("runway").
- **Going concern:** an accounting warning that there is "substantial doubt" a company can survive the next 12 months.
- **Delisting:** removal of a company's shares from a major stock exchange (like the NYSE) for failing to meet its standards.
- **OTC markets:** "over-the-counter" trading venues for stocks not on major exchanges — much less liquid and prestigious.
- **NYSE / Nasdaq:** the two big US stock exchanges. **SEC:** the US Securities and Exchange Commission, the federal regulator of stock markets. **Federal Reserve ("the Fed"):** the US central bank, which raises or lowers interest rates. **Chapter 11 / Chapter 7 bankruptcy:** US legal processes — Chapter 11 lets a company reorganize and keep operating; Chapter 7 is liquidation, selling everything off.

## Details

### Turo: Year-by-Year Timeline

**2019 (pre-period context).** In July 2019, Turo raised a $250 million Series E led by IAC [TechCrunch][ref-02] (the media/internet conglomerate behind Match, Expedia, and others), pushing its post-money valuation to roughly $1.25 billion [TechCrunch][ref-03] and making IAC its largest shareholder (~31% stake). [Clay][ref-04] This was Turo's last major private round.

**2020 — COVID collapse and recovery.** When the pandemic hit and travel stopped, Turo's business cratered. In late March/April 2020 the company laid off **108 employees, about 30% of its staff**, as shelter-in-place orders eliminated rentals. Yet Turo engineered a striking turnaround: it introduced the "Turo Risk Score" pricing tool in April 2020, [sec][ref-05] and as travelers shifted to road trips and avoided planes, demand recovered. Full-year 2020 net revenue actually rose 6% to **$149.9 million**, though the company posted a **net loss of $97.1 million**.

**2021 — the boom year.** Revenue exploded **213% to $469 million**, [TechCrunch][ref-06] powered by a nationwide rental-car shortage (traditional firms like Hertz had sold off fleets during the pandemic) [Sacra][ref-07] and a spike in used-car prices that made Turo's supply of privately owned cars extremely valuable. Net loss narrowed to $40.4 million.

**2022 — profitability and the IPO filing.** On January 10, 2022, Turo publicly filed its S-1 to go public on the NYSE under ticker "TURO." Per Turo's S-1/A: "In 2022, we generated net revenue of $746.6 million, representing 59% growth … and a net income of $154.7 million, compared to a net loss of $40.4 million in 2021" — its **first annual net profit**. (That net income was predicated on a more modest operating income result of $33.8 million; the larger net figure was boosted by non-operating items.) Then the IPO window slammed shut as the Fed raised interest rates and tech stocks sold off.

**2023 — growth decelerates.** Revenue grew to **$879.7 million** (up 18%), but net income shrank dramatically to **$14.7 million**. Turo kept its IPO alive on paper, filing repeated S-1 amendments (11 in total) but never launching.

**2024 — near $1 billion, growth flat.** Revenue reached roughly **$958 million** (up ~9%). Turo reported it facilitated $2.5 billion in gross booking value and paid hosts $1.5 billion, with 340,000 vehicles, 140,000 hosts, and 3.5 million guests. [Turo][ref-08] The CEO said Turo had been EBITDA-positive for four straight years. [Sacra][ref-07] But growth had flattened, and gross margins were shrinking (from 52.1% to 44.8% in the first half of the year). [Forge][ref-09]

**2025 — reputational shock, IPO withdrawal, and layoffs.** On January 1, 2025, two horrifying events involved Turo vehicles: a rented Ford pickup truck was driven into a crowd on Bourbon Street in New Orleans, killing 14 (plus the attacker), and a Tesla Cybertruck rented through Turo exploded outside the Trump International Hotel in Las Vegas. Turo cooperated with law enforcement and said neither renter had a disqualifying criminal background. On **February 13, 2025**, Turo formally withdrew its IPO registration (Form RW to the SEC), stating it "does not wish to conduct a public offering of securities at this time." [Access IPOs][ref-10] In **April 2025**, Turo laid off about **15% of staff (~150 of nearly 1,000 employees)**; a spokesperson told Bloomberg the company "made the very difficult decision to reduce the size of our team by approximately 15%," citing "ongoing economic uncertainty."

### Getaround: Year-by-Year Timeline

**2018–2019 (context).** Getaround's high-water mark was a $300 million Series D led by SoftBank's Vision Fund in 2018 (valuing it around $800 million), followed by reports in 2019 of raising at a ~$1.7 billion valuation. It spent heavily, including ~$300 million to acquire European rival Drivy in 2019.

**2020 — two rounds of COVID layoffs, then a rebound round.** In January 2020 — *before* COVID — Getaround laid off about **150 employees (roughly 25% of staff)**, with CEO Sam Zaid partly attributing it to turmoil at its backer SoftBank. When the pandemic hit, bookings dropped ~75%, and in March 2020 the company laid off about **100 more US employees** and sought a buyer. It recovered as short-distance driving rebounded, rehired furloughed staff by July, and in October 2020 raised a **$140 million Series E** (with SoftBank's Vision Fund participating, led by PeopleFund).

**2022 — the SPAC merger.** Unable to raise more private venture money on good terms, Getaround chose to go public by merging with **InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp**, a SPAC. Announced May 11, 2022, the deal "represents a combined company pro forma equity value of approximately $1.2 billion assuming no redemptions from InterPrivate's $259 million Trust … expected to result in at least $225 million and up to $434 million in gross proceeds … including a convertible note commitment of up to $175 million provided by affiliates of Mudrick Capital Management." The deal closed in **December 2022**. But **94.01% of InterPrivate's public shareholders redeemed** their shares (24,325,000 shares, "removing 94.01% of the SPAC's trust"), draining the $259 million trust down to roughly $15 million. Getaround's own 8-K said it "raises approximately $228 million," but this came mostly from the $175 million Mudrick convertible notes and ~$37.5 million of converted bridge notes, not fresh public cash. Getaround ended 2022 with only ~$64.3 million in cash. [Business Wire][ref-11] On its first trading day, December 9, 2022, the stock **fell more than 65%**. [CNBC][ref-12]

**2023 — filings crisis and asset purchase.** Getaround failed to file its annual report (10-K) on time, drawing a compliance warning from the NYSE in April 2023. It laid off about **10% of staff** in February 2023. [TechCrunch][ref-13] In May 2023 it acquired the assets of HyreCar, a gig-economy car-rental marketplace, for **$9.45 million**. [ZoomInfo][ref-14] Full-year 2023 revenue was **$72.68 million** (up 22%), [Getaround][ref-15] but it lost **$113.95 million**. [Getaround][ref-15]

**2024 — leadership churn, delisting, market retreats.** In February 2024, founder-CEO Sam Zaid stepped down; **Eduardo Iniguez** (a former HyreCar CEO) became CEO, backed by Mudrick Capital's Jason Mudrick as board chair. That same month, Getaround cut **30% of its North American staff** (having ended 2023 with ~290 full-time workers). It suspended operations in New York State (April 1, 2024) because the state's 2022 P2P Carsharing Act made required insurance roughly 50 times more expensive than for rental-car companies. The NYSE suspended trading in **July 2024** (market cap below the $15 million minimum) and delisting proceeded; shares moved to the OTC markets (OTCQB). CEO Iniguez resigned in October 2024; COO AJ Lee became interim CEO. [barchart][ref-16]

**2025–2026 — wind-down and dissolution.** On **February 7, 2025**, the board approved an orderly wind-down of US operations; on **February 12, 2025**, Getaround announced the immediate shutdown of its US car-sharing and HyreCar businesses, canceling all rentals and warning renters that insurance would lapse February 13. Substantially all US employees were laid off, most separating February 14, 2025. The company cited an "ongoing lack of liquidity" [Coverager][ref-17] — it had run out of money. Its European business survived initially and was **sold to Denmark's GoMore**: per Getaround's SEC proxy, "On April 30, 2026, the Company completed the sale of its European business to GoMore for a purchase price of approximately 31.5 million euros … plus … up to 2.3 million euros of receivables"; creditor Mudrick's consent eliminated ~$121.7 million of senior secured debt. Getaround did **not** file for bankruptcy; instead its board resolved on June 5, 2026 to dissolve under Delaware law (DGCL §275), and shareholders approved the dissolution and plan of liquidation at a special meeting on **July 29, 2026**. Because liabilities (including senior secured debt) are expected to exceed assets, "no cash distributions to common stockholders are anticipated."

### Valuation Declines and Their Employment Effects

**Getaround: a financing shortfall and continuing losses, not a share-price fall alone.** Its headline $1.2 billion SPAC value described what the merged company was supposed to be worth; it was not cash available to pay bills. With 94.01% of SPAC investors redeeming, the merger left a thin cash cushion and substantial Mudrick convertible debt. Getaround later told regulators that redemptions "significantly reduced the proceeds from the transaction and resulted in the Company having insufficient capital to fund its business operations and continue as a going concern." Losses of over $100 million a year continued, while high interest rates prevented access to cheap capital. Against that operating and financing background, the stock declined from its ~$10 SPAC reference price to fractions of a penny, leaving essentially zero market value by 2025. [SF Gate][ref-18]

The employment sequence spans different business conditions and must be read chronologically:

- January 2020: ~150 laid off (~25% of staff). As described in the company timeline, this was before the pandemic; the CEO partly attributed it to turmoil at SoftBank.
- March 2020: ~100 more US employees laid off (later rehired), during the collapse in bookings. This preceded the SPAC financing shortfall.
- February 2023: ~10% of staff laid off after the merger, while the company remained loss-making.
- February 2024: ~30% of North American staff laid off (ended 2023 with ~290 full-time workers), during the continuing financial and operating difficulties.
- February 2025: substantially all remaining US employees laid off in the US wind-down, for which the company cited lack of liquidity.

Thus, the later cuts sit within the documented chain of insufficient financing, continuing losses and eventual cash exhaustion. The record does not establish that a fall in valuation by itself caused each round of layoffs, or that the later SPAC event explains the earlier cuts.

**Turo: operating adjustments while remaining private.** Its two employment setbacks were (1) the 2020 COVID layoffs of ~30% of staff (108 people), when travel demand collapsed, and (2) the 2025 layoffs of ~15% (~150 people) after the IPO withdrawal, for which the earlier timeline records the company's explanation of ongoing economic uncertainty. Its later profitable business gave it scope to remain private; it did not experience an observed public-share-price collapse.

Caplight's estimate of around $2.2 billion in early 2025 is a secondary-market estimate for a private company, not an official funding round or public market capitalization. It exceeds the roughly $1.25 billion 2019 round valuation, so these figures do not document a "down round." The stalled IPO nevertheless suggested that a public offering would likely value Turo below early hopes of $2–3 billion. That expectation and the secondary estimate are different measures, and neither alone establishes the reason for its staffing cuts.

### Macroeconomic Context

- **2020 COVID shock:** Travel and rental demand collapsed; both companies undertook emergency layoffs. This common shock is distinct from Getaround's earlier pre-pandemic cut.
- **2021 zero-interest-rate boom:** Nearly free borrowing encouraged investment in fast-growing technology firms and inflated valuations. A rental-car shortage and used-car price spike supercharged Turo's 2021 growth. Cox Automotive/Manheim reported wholesale used-vehicle prices in December 2021 at "a record high of 236.2, a 46.6% increase from a year ago."
- **2022 Fed rate hikes and the SPAC/IPO bust:** Rapid interest-rate rises led investors to mark down unprofitable technology firms. Turo faced a frozen IPO market; Getaround entered public trading as the SPAC market collapsed. The same financing environment therefore met different company cash positions.
- **2023–2024 normalization:** Rebuilding of traditional rental fleets and falling used-car prices slowed Turo's growth toward single digits.
- **2025 IPO market:** Listings remained thin, and Turo's withdrawal further reduced the already sparse technology IPO pipeline.

### Insurance, Regulation, and Airport Fights

Operating costs add a separate company-level channel. **Insurance**, car-sharing's largest recurring expense, became roughly 50 times more expensive than for rental firms under New York's 2022 P2P Carsharing Act. This forced Getaround's April 2024 state exit and contributed to losing one of its largest markets. **Airport access** was a different exposure for Turo: traditional rental companies lobbied for federal restrictions on P2P airport car-sharing, and cities including Los Angeles pursued legal challenges. Airport-origin trips represented ~36% of Turo's gross bookings in 2023, a material risk explicitly identified in its filings. These business pressures should be considered alongside cash and financing, not collapsed into a single valuation-driven explanation.

## Recommendations

These are analytical takeaways for a reader trying to understand or make decisions about this sector.

1. 1. **Judge car-sharing platforms by profitability and cash runway, not headline valuations or GMV.** The single clearest predictor of survival here was whether the company made money per transaction and held enough cash. Turo's positive net income and multi-year EBITDA-positive record let it wait out a closed IPO market; Getaround's structural losses killed it. *Threshold that would change the view:* a currently-unprofitable platform demonstrating two-plus consecutive years of positive operating cash flow.
2. 2. **Treat SPAC mergers with high redemption rates as a red flag, not a funding event.** Getaround's 94.01% redemption meant the "$1.2 billion" deal delivered almost no usable public cash. *Benchmark:* if a SPAC target discloses redemptions above ~50–70%, assume the advertised valuation and cash proceeds are largely fictional and scrutinize the debt taken on to close the gap.
3. 3. **For anyone considering pre-IPO Turo shares (secondary markets):** recognize that Turo is genuinely profitable but growth has flattened to single digits and margins are compressing, and its withdrawn IPO plus the 2025 reputational incidents mean a near-term public listing is unlikely. Value it as a mature, cash-generating marketplace, not a hyper-growth story. *Threshold to revisit:* a re-accelerated revenue growth rate back above ~15%, or a refiled S-1.
4. 4. **Watch insurance and airport regulation as the sector's swing factors.** State-level insurance laws (as in New York) can render entire markets unprofitable overnight, and airport access lawsuits directly threaten Turo's most lucrative segment. Regulatory outcomes here should be monitored as leading indicators of margin health.

## Caveats

- **Turo is private**, so many figures come from its S-1 filings (audited, reliable through 2023 and H1 2024) and press estimates. The ~$958 million 2024 revenue figure is an estimate; Turo said it was "on track" for $1 billion in 2025 (a forward-looking projection, not a result). Turo's early-2025 ~$2.2 billion valuation is a secondary-market data-provider estimate (Caplight), not an official priced round.
- **Getaround's full-year 2024 financials were never fully filed** — its 10-K was delayed amid the wind-down — so 2024 figures are partial/trailing (~$76.9 million trailing revenue through September 2024).
- **Getaround's exact first-day (Dec 9, 2022) closing price** could not be confirmed to a primary source; the well-documented facts are a ">65% decline" on debut and a $3.29 intraday high that day, against a ~$10 SPAC reference price.
- **Getaround did not file for bankruptcy** (no Chapter 7 or Chapter 11); it pursued a voluntary Delaware dissolution approved by shareholders on July 29, 2026. Some early press loosely described the collapse as a "bankruptcy," which is inaccurate.
- **Layoff numbers** for Getaround's 2024 and 2025 rounds were often given as percentages; the company declined to disclose exact headcounts, so absolute figures are derived from its last reported employee counts (283 full-time at end-2022; ~290 at end-2023).
- Turo's reported 2020 net loss ($97.1M) and Getaround's 2020 revenue (~$58.7M) come from filings and reputable reporting; some aggregator figures for Getaround's early net losses vary and should be treated as approximate.
