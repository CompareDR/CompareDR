# Cloud Computing Auto-Scaling: Technology Classification, Challenges, and Research Solutions

This report follows a source-screening plan centered on independent primary research and authoritative technical documentation: first, classify mainstream auto-scaling mechanisms by when scaling decisions are made and by the decision model they use; second, compare their working principles, strengths, and limitations; third, examine five recurring application challenges and anchor each challenge in a specific published study and its proposed solution. **Strict exclusion applied throughout:** the article *“Auto-Scaling Techniques in Cloud Computing: Issues and Research Directions”* by Saleha Alharthi, Afra Alshamsi, Anoud Alseiari, and Abdulmalik Alwarafy, together with the four URLs specified by the user, was excluded from the research process and is neither used, cited, quoted, nor relied upon here. This exclusion was part of the research plan from the outset, as requested. 

## Auto-Scaling Technology Classification

Cloud auto-scaling changes the amount or shape of compute capacity allocated to an application in response to changing demand. Horizontal scaling changes the number of instances, VMs, containers, or pods, whereas vertical scaling changes the CPU, memory, or other resources assigned to existing instances. Modern systems often combine both forms and may also jointly optimize placement, VM type, or resource ratios. The most useful high-level distinction is between **reactive methods**, which decide after observing a change in measured system state, and **proactive methods**, which anticipate future demand and provision capacity before the demand materializes. In practice, hybrid systems increasingly combine the two because prediction reduces provisioning delay while reactive control provides a safety mechanism when forecasts are wrong. [[1]](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/autoscaling/horizontal-pod-autoscale/)

### Reactive Methods

**Threshold-based auto-scaling.** The simplest and most widely deployed reactive architecture monitors one or more metrics—commonly CPU utilization, memory use, request rate, queue length, response time, or application-specific metrics—and compares them against configured targets or thresholds. When a threshold is crossed for a specified interval, the controller scales out/up; when load falls below a lower threshold, it scales in/down. Kubernetes HPA illustrates the general mechanism: it computes a desired replica count from the ratio between the current metric value and the configured target, applies tolerances and readiness checks, and can use stabilization windows to avoid repeatedly adding and removing pods. [[2]](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/autoscaling/horizontal-pod-autoscale/)

The attraction is operational simplicity. Threshold rules are transparent, cheap to compute, easy to explain, and require neither workload-training data nor a detailed mathematical performance model. They are therefore suitable when the relationship between a monitored metric and required capacity is reasonably stable. Their principal weaknesses are **reaction delay**, manual threshold tuning, hysteresis/flapping problems, and the difficulty of translating a multidimensional workload into a small number of fixed thresholds. A workload can exceed an SLO during VM or container startup because reactive scaling begins only after the metric has already deteriorated. Static parameters can also become inappropriate after changes in workload mix or application implementation. Research on fuzzy elasticity controllers explicitly identified the burden of specifying precise quantitative rules as a limitation of traditional threshold approaches. [[3]](https://researcher.itu.dk/en/publications/autonomic-resource-provisioning-for-cloud-based-software/)

Threshold methods can nevertheless be made substantially more sophisticated. Tournaire, Castel-Taleb, and Hyon formulate threshold selection as an optimization problem in which VM activation/deactivation thresholds are chosen to minimize the sum of energy costs and penalties for failing performance requirements. Their work shows that threshold-based scaling is not inherently synonymous with ad-hoc configuration: thresholds can be derived mathematically from queue-load models and cost objectives. [[4]](https://doi.org/10.1145%2F3603532)

**Control-theoretic methods.** Feedback-control auto-scalers treat an application as a dynamic system. A controller continuously observes an output such as response time, utilization, or throughput; compares it with a reference value or SLO; computes the error; and adjusts resource allocation so that the observed system converges toward the desired operating point. Depending on the design, this can resemble proportional/integral control, model-predictive control, optimal control, or adaptive control. Quattrocchi and colleagues' ScaleX, for example, uses control theory for vertical scaling, while their QN-CTRL combines queueing-theoretic performance modeling with optimal control. Their evaluation against cloud-provider auto-scalers found that the theoretically grounded approaches generally produced a better trade-off between SLA violations and resource use across their tested workloads. [[5]](https://iris.imtlucca.it/handle/20.500.11771/26338)

Compared with simple thresholds, control methods offer explicit reasoning about stability, transient behavior, overshoot, and settling time. They can continuously calculate the amount of resource adjustment rather than merely deciding that a threshold has been crossed. They are especially attractive when a usable input-output model is available. Their limitations arise when the cloud system is nonlinear, time-varying, noisy, subject to long provisioning delays, or difficult to identify accurately: a controller tuned for one regime may perform poorly in another. Adaptive designs partly address this by re-estimating the model. Gandhi et al.'s DC2, for instance, uses Kalman filtering to learn changing application parameters and then uses the learned model to drive scaling decisions without requiring offline application profiling. [[6]](https://www.usenix.org/conference/icac14/technical-sessions/presentation/gandhi)

**Fuzzy-logic and rule-based adaptive control.** Fuzzy auto-scalers occupy an intermediate position between fixed rules and fully learned policies. Instead of requiring a crisp rule such as “scale out at CPU > 70%,” fuzzy systems define linguistic states such as low, medium, and high utilization using membership functions and combine them through fuzzy inference rules. This allows uncertainty and gradual transitions to be represented directly. Jamshidi and colleagues' autonomic resource-provisioning work used type-2 fuzzy logic so operators could specify elasticity rules qualitatively and the controller could reason under uncertainty; experiments were designed to test its ability to tolerate unexpected workload spikes while maintaining acceptable user experience. [[7]](https://researcher.itu.dk/en/publications/autonomic-resource-provisioning-for-cloud-based-software/)

Their main advantages are tolerance of noisy measurements, smoother decisions than hard thresholds, and the ability to encode operator knowledge without exact numerical boundaries. Their main limitations are the design of membership functions and rule bases, growth in rule complexity as the number of inputs rises, and limited ability to discover entirely new policies unless the fuzzy system is itself adapted or learned. [[7]](https://researcher.itu.dk/en/publications/autonomic-resource-provisioning-for-cloud-based-software/)

**Queueing-, optimization-, and model-based reactive methods.** Another mainstream family uses explicit performance or optimization models. Queueing models estimate how request arrival rates, service rates, concurrency, and replica counts affect delay or utilization, while optimization algorithms choose resource allocations that minimize cost under SLO constraints. These methods can reason directly about the resource-performance relationship rather than using a metric threshold as a proxy. Quattrocchi et al.'s QN-CTRL is an example of queueing-theoretic modeling coupled to optimal control, and Guo, Stolyar, and Walid propose an online “shadow” algorithm that uses a virtual queueing system to jointly guide VM auto-scaling and VM-to-host packing while adapting continuously to changing application demand. [[8]](https://iris.imtlucca.it/handle/20.500.11771/26338)

The advantage is principled resource allocation and the ability to incorporate cost, placement, and performance constraints in a single objective. The disadvantages are model error, computational overhead, and assumptions that may not hold for complex microservices, heterogeneous machines, interference, or nonstationary workloads. Online approximations and adaptive models are therefore often used instead of repeatedly solving a global optimization problem from scratch. [[9]](https://www.nist.gov/publications/online-vm-auto-scaling-algorithms-application-hosting-cloud)

### Proactive Methods

**Statistical forecasting and time-series prediction.** Proactive auto-scalers forecast a future workload or resource metric and provision capacity early enough to absorb the expected change. Traditional implementations can use autoregression, moving-average models, exponential smoothing, or related time-series techniques when workload patterns contain stable trends or seasonality. Their fundamental advantage is that they can hide instance-startup and warm-up delay by requesting resources before a traffic increase occurs. Their weakness is forecast dependence: abrupt regime changes, unprecedented flash crowds, and prediction-horizon errors can turn forecast error directly into over-provisioning or SLO violations. The need to forecast far enough ahead to cover resource provisioning time is central to proactive scaling. [[10]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/10114033)

**Machine-learning and deep-learning prediction.** ML-based proactive scaling learns a mapping from historical workload and system telemetry to future demand or future resource requirements. Models range from support-vector machines and feed-forward networks to recurrent, convolutional, and graph-based neural networks. Golshani and Ashtiani formulate auto-scaling as a sequence-modeling problem: a temporal convolutional neural network predicts future request load, a neural model maps predicted load to resource demand, and TOPSIS multi-criteria decision making chooses among scale-out, scale-down, and no-change actions while considering SLA violations, underutilization, and cost. Their reported evaluation found lower prediction error and approximately a 4% improvement over compared forecasting approaches. [[11]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0743731521000836)

Deep prediction models can capture nonlinear temporal relationships that fixed thresholds cannot and can incorporate many metrics simultaneously. Their limitations include the requirement for representative training data, vulnerability to concept drift, model-training and inference overhead, reduced interpretability, and the risk that an accurate predictor still yields poor scaling if the mapping from forecast demand to capacity is wrong. Moreover, an apparently low average forecast error can conceal rare peaks that matter most for SLO compliance. [[12]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0743731521000836)

**Reinforcement-learning methods.** RL reframes auto-scaling as sequential decision making. The agent observes a state such as workload, CPU/memory utilization, response time, and current replicas; executes a scaling action; and receives a reward combining performance, SLO, and resource-cost terms. Over repeated interaction, it learns a policy that seeks to maximize long-term cumulative reward. RL can therefore learn the *decision policy itself*, rather than using a separate prediction model followed by a hand-designed controller. The intelligent Kubernetes scaling system studied by Radhakrishnan et al. uses RL agents to learn scaling threshold values under QoS constraints; experiments reported response-time improvements of up to 20% relative to the default scaling paradigm in their setting. [[13]](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2021.3061890)

RL's major advantage is adaptability to complex nonlinear environments where an analytical performance model is unavailable. It can optimize multiple objectives and learn policies from interaction. However, naïve online exploration can generate unsafe scaling actions, learning can be slow, workload transfer is difficult, and reward design strongly shapes policy behavior. AWARE directly addresses these production problems using meta-learning for faster adaptation and bootstrapping for safer exploration; its experiments reported 5.5× faster adaptation to new workloads than the comparison transfer-learning approach, while the bootstrapped training setup increased CPU and memory utilization and substantially reduced SLO violations during training. [[14]](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc23/presentation/qiu-haoran)

**Hybrid predictive/reactive auto-scaling.** Production-oriented designs increasingly combine prediction with reactive safeguards. Prediction starts capacity changes before anticipated demand arrives; a reactive path detects forecast error, flash crowds, failures, or other deviations and corrects the allocation. This architecture reduces the fundamental risk of relying exclusively either on delayed feedback or on imperfect prediction. TAHA, a 5G/B5G VNF auto-scaling design, explicitly uses a proactive component to reduce SLA violations and a reactive component as protection against forecasting error; its evaluation under dynamic traffic reported large latency reductions relative to both non-scaling and comparison schemes. [[15]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/11160960)

The trade-off is additional system complexity: two decision loops must be coordinated so that they do not issue contradictory actions, and operators must determine how much authority to give a prediction versus observed runtime conditions. Nevertheless, hybrid control is a natural architecture for workloads combining recurring patterns with unpredictable bursts. [[15]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/11160960)

## Main Challenges and Solutions

### Energy Efficiency

**Manifestation of the challenge.** Cloud elasticity can reduce energy use by eliminating idle capacity, but poorly designed auto-scaling can also waste energy through chronic over-provisioning, unnecessary VM activation, inefficient physical-machine placement, or frequent transitions. Energy optimization is additionally coupled to performance: aggressive consolidation saves power but can increase contention and QoS violations, while generous spare capacity improves responsiveness at the cost of idle power. An auto-scaler therefore must decide not only *how many* resources an application needs, but also *which VM types and physical machines* should host them. [[16]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231220315873)

A representative study is Saxena and Singh's **“A proactive autoscaling and energy-efficient VM allocation framework using online multi-resource neural network for cloud data center.”** Their solution has three stages: an Online Multi-Resource Feed-forward Neural Network forecasts multiple future resource demands simultaneously; VM capacity is auto-scaled according to clusters of predicted requirements; and the resulting VMs are consolidated onto energy-efficient physical machines. The central idea is to match predicted CPU/memory-type requirements more closely to VM capacities and then minimize the number of active physical machines, attacking both over-provisioning and host-level energy waste. [[17]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231220315873)

This study illustrates why energy-aware auto-scaling is broader than a simple scale-in rule. Provisioning, VM sizing, and placement should be optimized jointly: otherwise an auto-scaler can reduce logical instance count while still leaving a physically inefficient placement. A separate model-driven study similarly formulated VM configuration and energy consumption as a constraint-satisfaction optimization problem, reinforcing the value of explicitly incorporating energy into the scaling objective. [[18]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167739X11000902)

### Dynamic Nature of Telecom Services

**Manifestation of the challenge.** Telecom and 5G workloads are particularly difficult because virtualized or cloud-native network functions must react to rapidly changing traffic while satisfying strict latency and service-continuity constraints. Multi-domain environments add further complications: separate administrative domains may not be willing or permitted to exchange raw operational data, yet an end-to-end service may still require coordinated forecasting and scaling. Network traffic can also include bursts, heterogeneous service classes, session state, and geographically distributed demand, making a single fixed scaling rule inadequate. [[19]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/9319704)

A specific example is **Subramanya et al.'s “Centralized and Federated Learning for Predictive VNF Autoscaling in Multi-Domain 5G Networks and Beyond.”** The authors model VNF auto-scaling as time-series forecasting: deep-learning models predict the future number of VNF instances needed from anticipated traffic, supporting both horizontal and vertical scaling. Crucially, they compare centralized learning with federated learning so multiple domains can train a predictive scaling model without pooling all raw domain data. They evaluate the approach using a commercial operator dataset and implement an AI-driven Kubernetes orchestration prototype on a multi-access-edge-computing platform. [[20]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/9319704)

The proposed solution targets two telecom-specific problems simultaneously: forecasting compensates for the latency of reactive VNF scaling, while federated learning addresses cross-domain data isolation and privacy. More recent work continues the same direction by combining traffic prediction, closed-loop learning, and multiple VNF lifecycle states so capacity can react to highly dynamic heterogeneous traffic without repeatedly paying the full initialization and teardown cost of functions. [[21]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/10631271)

### Big Data Processing

**Manifestation of the challenge.** Big-data frameworks such as Spark execute parallel stages whose CPU, memory, shuffle, and I/O demands can change substantially over the lifetime of one job. A static allocation sized for peak demand wastes capacity during low-demand stages, but scaling executors during execution is difficult because executors can contain cached intermediate data and because removing or adding workers may introduce migration, recomputation, and scheduling overhead. Resource elasticity is therefore constrained not just by current utilization but by the dataflow semantics and state of the computation. [[22]](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCC.2021.3108043)

A published solution is **iSpark**, presented in *“Dynamic Resource Provisioning for Iterative Workloads on Apache Spark.”* The authors observe that iterative applications frequently need more computation early in execution and progressively less in later iterations. iSpark measures real-time utilization, identifies underutilized executors, dynamically scales executor count, and preempts executors while preserving cached intermediate data to maintain data consistency. In the reported testbed experiments, the system improved average utilization of individual executors by 35.2%, raised cluster utilization from 32.1% to 51.3%, and reduced overall job-completion time by 20.8% for the evaluated iterative applications. [[23]](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCC.2021.3108043)

The broader lesson is that big-data auto-scaling often requires **framework-aware elasticity** rather than infrastructure-only scaling. The auto-scaler benefits from knowing stage structure, cached state, I/O behavior, and executor usefulness. Recent empirical work also shows that generic autoscaling alone is not guaranteed to outperform a carefully selected static configuration: an analysis of Spark jobs on Google Dataproc Serverless found that fixed node sizes and fixed memory-to-CPU ratios can limit the resource-efficiency benefits of autoscaling. [[24]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/10825367)

### Cost-Effective Graph Processing

**Manifestation of the challenge.** Large-scale graph algorithms are typically iterative and communication-heavy. Graph partitions interact repeatedly, so changing the cluster size can trigger expensive repartitioning and data movement. Public clouds add heterogeneous VM types with different prices and performance characteristics, turning graph scaling into a joint problem of instance count, instance type, partition placement, and iteration progress. A naïve scale-out policy can shorten execution time yet increase total monetary cost because additional workers may deliver poor marginal performance once communication or partition skew dominates. [[25]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/8798698)

A direct study of this problem is **“A Cost-Efficient Auto-Scaling Algorithm for Large-Scale Graph Processing in Cloud Environments with Heterogeneous Resources.”** The authors develop algorithms that choose both the number and types of VMs according to the computation requirements of convergent graph applications. The design adds a profiling mechanism to characterize execution and a dynamic graph-repartitioning mechanism intended to redistribute partitions efficiently as the cluster configuration changes. In the reported evaluation, the system outperformed Giraph and reduced dollar cost by more than 50% relative to Giraph in the tested settings. [[25]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/8798698)

Its importance is that graph auto-scaling must optimize **cost per completed graph computation**, not merely instantaneous utilization. Efficient scaling depends on accounting for convergence behavior, heterogeneity, repartitioning overhead, and the performance-to-price ratio of VM types. [[25]](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/8798698)

### Workload Prediction

**Manifestation of the challenge.** Prediction is the core enabling problem for proactive auto-scaling because new resources usually have nonzero startup and warm-up times. The predictor must therefore forecast demand sufficiently far into the future to permit provisioning, while coping with seasonality, trends, changing user behavior, bursts, and concept drift. Underprediction creates insufficient capacity and SLO violations; overprediction creates unnecessary cost. Prediction accuracy alone is also insufficient, because the auto-scaler must convert a workload forecast into an estimate of future CPU, memory, replica, or VM requirements and then select an action under multiple objectives. [[26]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0743731521000836)

Golshani and Ashtiani's **“Proactive auto-scaling for cloud environments using temporal convolutional neural networks”** provides a concrete end-to-end solution. Their temporal convolutional network learns temporal workload patterns and predicts future service-request rates from historical windows. A second neural mapping converts predicted workload plus system information into expected resource requirements, and a TOPSIS-based multi-criteria layer evaluates alternative scaling actions according to SLA violations, resource underutilization, and cost. The approach therefore treats workload prediction, capacity estimation, and scaling policy as distinct but connected components rather than assuming that a good request-rate forecast automatically produces a good scaling decision. [[11]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0743731521000836)

A complementary direction is meta-reinforcement learning. Xue et al.'s KDD 2022 work, **“A Meta Reinforcement Learning Approach for Predictive Autoscaling in the Cloud,”** treats predictive scaling as a sequential resource-management problem and uses meta-RL to improve adaptation across changing workload conditions. This addresses an important weakness of conventional predictors: a model trained on one workload regime may deteriorate when the workload distribution changes. [[27]](https://doi.org/10.1145/3534678.3539063)

Across these challenge areas, the research trend is toward **multi-layer auto-scaling**: forecasting or adaptive learning estimates future demand; a performance model translates demand into resource need; an optimizer or control policy decides how much and in what form to scale; and a reactive feedback path corrects prediction and modeling errors. Primary studies in energy-aware placement, telecom VNF orchestration, Spark, graph processing, and predictive control show that application-specific semantics increasingly determine whether auto-scaling actually improves cost, QoS, and utilization rather than merely changing the number of running instances. [[28]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231220315873)
