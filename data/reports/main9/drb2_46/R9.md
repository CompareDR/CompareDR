# Auto-Scaling Technology Classification

Auto-scaling is the automated adjustment of computing resources—VMs, containers, pods, functions, storage, or network functions—to match changing workload demand while maintaining quality-of-service (QoS), service-level objectives (SLOs), and cost efficiency. The field is commonly organized around **reactive** methods, which respond after monitored conditions change, and **proactive** methods, which forecast or infer future demand and act before overload occurs. In practice, the strongest production systems increasingly combine both: reactive scaling remains essential as a safety net, while proactive scaling reduces provisioning delay and SLA violations when demand is predictable.

## Reactive Methods

Reactive auto-scaling relies on current or recently observed system state. It is the dominant operational model in public clouds and Kubernetes because it is simple, explainable, and comparatively easy to implement. Its core weakness is that it acts **after** load has already changed; therefore, detection delay, provisioning delay, container cold start, VM boot time, and load-balancer registration can still cause temporary SLA violations.

### 1. Threshold-Based and Rule-Based Scaling

Threshold-based scaling is the most familiar reactive technique. Operators define rules such as: “if CPU utilization exceeds 70% for five minutes, add two replicas” or “if memory utilization falls below 30%, remove one replica.” The auto-scaler continuously monitors metrics and triggers scaling actions when thresholds are crossed. Industrial auto-scalers often use this model because it is transparent and operationally intuitive. The ACM Computing Surveys review by Qu et al. describes rule-based approaches as widely adopted in industry, noting that users can define thresholds over either low-level metrics such as CPU utilization or high-level metrics such as response time and request rate ([Qu et al., 2018](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/AutoScaleWebAppClouds-ACMCS.pdf)).

| Method | Core principle | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static threshold rules | Trigger scale-out/scale-in when a metric crosses fixed limits | Simple, explainable, low overhead, easy to audit | Poor under bursty traffic; requires manual tuning; may oscillate |
| Dynamic threshold rules | Adjust thresholds based on recent workload or learned behavior | More adaptive than static rules | Still reactive; learning or tuning errors can cause instability |
| Multi-threshold rules | Use upper/lower bounds, cooldowns, and step scaling | Reduces unnecessary scaling and flapping | Can still lag behind sudden workload spikes |

The main advantage of threshold-based scaling is operational reliability: engineers can reason about cause and effect. However, thresholds are fragile. A CPU threshold that works for one workload may fail for another, especially for I/O-bound, memory-bound, or latency-sensitive services. Threshold-based systems also suffer from **oscillation**, where resources are repeatedly added and removed in a short period. This is why cooldown periods, stabilization windows, and hysteresis are typically added.

Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) is a representative threshold-based mechanism, but it has an important nuance: when multiple metrics are configured, HPA evaluates each metric separately and scales to the highest computed replica count. More complex composed metrics often require custom or external metrics APIs and adapter infrastructure, increasing development and maintenance overhead ([Pusztai, n.d.](https://dsg.tuwien.ac.at/~sd/papers/Diss_Pusztai_Thomas.pdf)).

### 2. Feedback-Control and Self-Adaptive Reactive Scaling

Control-theoretic auto-scalers use feedback loops to compare observed performance against a target and adjust resources accordingly. A controller may aim to keep response time below a threshold, CPU utilization near a set point, or queue length within a desired range. Examples include proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controllers, adaptive controllers, and self-tuning feedback mechanisms.

Compared with static rules, feedback-control methods are more systematic: they model scaling as a control problem and can continuously tune the scaling action. Qu et al. describe self-adaptive auto-scalers that tune themselves based on observed control quality and can update parameters in analytical or machine-learning models at runtime ([Qu et al., 2018](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/AutoScaleWebAppClouds-ACMCS.pdf)).

**Advantages**

- More stable than naive threshold rules when tuned correctly.
- Can explicitly target QoS metrics such as latency.
- Can adapt to changes in workload or application behavior.

**Limitations**

- Controller design is non-trivial.
- Wrong gain parameters can cause oscillation or slow response.
- Model mismatch is common in microservice systems where dependencies change frequently.

### 3. Analytical and Queueing-Model-Based Reactive Scaling

Analytical methods model the application as a queue or network of queues. The scaler estimates how many servers, pods, or service instances are needed to process current demand while meeting response-time targets. Queueing theory is especially relevant for web applications, multi-tier applications, and service pipelines. Qu et al. note that single-layer queueing models are insufficient for multi-tier or many-service applications; queueing networks are needed to model relationships among tiers or components ([Qu et al., 2018](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/AutoScaleWebAppClouds-ACMCS.pdf)).

**Core working principle**

1. Measure arrival rate, service time, utilization, queue length, and response time.
2. Fit or update a queueing model.
3. Estimate the minimum resources required to keep delay below target.
4. Apply scale-out or scale-in.

**Advantages**

- Provides a mathematically grounded link between workload and performance.
- Useful for latency-sensitive applications where CPU alone is insufficient.
- Can support “what-if” reasoning.

**Limitations**

- Requires parameters such as service time that are difficult to measure directly.
- Assumptions such as Poisson arrivals or stable service distributions may not hold.
- Multi-service applications require complex queueing networks.

### 4. Reactive Scaling in Microservices and Service Mesh Environments

Microservice systems create additional reactive-scaling complexity because one service’s overload can propagate downstream. Reactive scaling at the service level can create conflicting or redundant actions if dependencies are ignored. Service mesh technologies can help by exposing traffic routing, retries, latency, circuit-breaking, and per-service telemetry, making them useful for mitigating traffic uncertainty and informing scaling policies. A 2026 review of autoscaling stateful microservices identifies service mesh integration, workload prediction, and Kubernetes resource management as important mechanisms for handling traffic uncertainty ([Rhee & Yang, 2026](https://jceim.org/index.php/ojs/article/view/182)).

**Advantages**

- Richer traffic observability than node-level metrics alone.
- Supports routing-aware and dependency-aware decisions.
- Can help isolate failure domains.

**Limitations**

- Adds telemetry and control-plane complexity.
- Service mesh metrics must be carefully mapped to scaling actions.
- Stateful services remain difficult because state migration and data locality matter.

## Proactive Methods

Proactive auto-scaling predicts future demand or system state and provisions resources before overload appears. Its purpose is to overcome the inherent delay of reactive scaling. Proactive scaling is strongest when workloads have periodicity, seasonality, or recurring patterns; it is weakest when traffic spikes are adversarial, random, or caused by rare external events.

### 1. Time-Series Forecasting

Time-series models forecast future workload using historical data. Common methods include ARIMA, exponential smoothing, Prophet, seasonal decomposition, and Markov models. They are especially useful for web traffic with daily or weekly seasonality.

**Core working principle**

1. Store historical request rate, CPU utilization, latency, or queue length.
2. Decompose or model trend, seasonality, and residual variation.
3. Forecast demand over a short horizon.
4. Convert forecast demand into target replicas or resources.

A recent survey of predictive models for IaaS auto-scaling discusses time-series, machine-learning, Markov, and reinforcement-learning models, noting that predictive models can improve resource allocation but may struggle with abrupt workload changes ([JATIT, 2023](http://www.jatit.org/volumes/Vol101No23/7Vol101No23.pdf)).

**Advantages**

- Interpretable compared with many deep-learning models.
- Effective for periodic workloads.
- Lower training cost than deep neural models.

**Limitations**

- Linear models struggle with non-linear bursts.
- Prediction errors can cause overprovisioning or SLA violations.
- Requires sufficient historical data.

### 2. Machine-Learning-Based Resource Estimation

Machine learning is used to learn the relationship between workload, resource allocation, and performance. Regression, random forests, support vector machines, Bayesian models, and online-learning methods can estimate how many resources are needed for a given demand level. Qu et al. emphasize that resource estimation is central to auto-scaling: underestimation causes SLA violations and prolonged provisioning, while overestimation increases cost through overprovisioning ([Qu et al., 2018](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/AutoScaleWebAppClouds-ACMCS.pdf)).

**Advantages**

- Learns application-specific behavior.
- Can incorporate multiple metrics: request rate, CPU, memory, latency, and external signals.
- Online learning can adapt as applications evolve.

**Limitations**

- Training data quality strongly affects accuracy.
- Online learning may perform poorly during convergence.
- Models may become stale after code, workload, or infrastructure changes.

### 3. Deep-Learning-Based Forecasting

Deep learning models such as LSTM, GRU, BiLSTM, CNN-LSTM, Transformers, and hybrid attention models are increasingly used for workload forecasting. They capture non-linear and temporal dependencies better than classical models, particularly when workloads are multivariate and bursty. A 2026 Springer study on federated LLM training and edge autoscaling evaluates LSTM, GRU, BiLSTM, CNN, CNN-LSTM, and Transformer models on Bitbrains CPU traces, reporting that CNN-LSTM produced the most accurate predictions and required the fewest pods across evaluated scenarios ([Kumar et al., 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11276-026-04224-1)).

**Advantages**

- Strong performance on non-linear and multivariate workloads.
- Can forecast CPU, memory, bandwidth, and workload traces jointly.
- Useful for cloud-edge environments with dynamic demand.

**Limitations**

- Requires training data and computational resources.
- Interpretability is weaker than rules or classical models.
- Generalization across applications is not guaranteed.

### 4. Reinforcement Learning and Deep Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning (RL) formulates auto-scaling as a sequential decision problem. The agent observes state—such as CPU utilization, latency, queue length, replica count, and cost—and chooses actions such as scale out, scale in, or hold. Rewards penalize SLA violations, cost, and instability. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) extends this to high-dimensional state spaces using neural networks.

Cloud scheduling methods are commonly categorized into dynamic programming, randomized algorithms, heuristics, meta-heuristics, hybrid algorithms, and machine learning; among these, machine learning is explicitly identified as the category capable of predicting future system states. The 2024 Artificial Intelligence Review article further argues that DRL is promising for complex cloud scheduling scenarios where classical heuristics fail to evaluate solution quality effectively ([Li et al., 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-024-10756-9)).

**Advantages**

- Learns policies that jointly optimize cost, latency, and stability.
- Suitable for complex, dynamic environments.
- Can support multi-objective optimization.

**Limitations**

- Needs exploration, training time, and safe-learning mechanisms.
- Poorly designed reward functions produce undesirable behavior.
- Convergence and explainability remain major operational barriers.

### 5. Hybrid Reactive-Proactive Scaling

Hybrid methods combine forecasting with reactive correction. A predictor provisions ahead of expected load, while reactive rules correct forecast errors. This is increasingly the most practical architecture because it balances anticipation with robustness. The stateful microservices review notes that autoscaling research is moving beyond purely reactive policies toward hybrid reactive-proactive approaches using ML/DL workload prediction to handle variable load and traffic uncertainty ([Rhee & Yang, 2026](https://jceim.org/index.php/ojs/article/view/182)).

**Concrete opinion based on the reviewed evidence:** for most production environments, **hybrid scaling should be considered the default target architecture**. Pure reactive scaling is too slow for cold starts and bursty traffic; pure proactive scaling is too dependent on forecast accuracy. A hybrid design with conservative prediction, reactive guardrails, cooldowns, and SLO-aware rollback is more robust than either extreme.

# Main Challenges and Solutions

## Energy Efficiency

### Problem manifestation

Auto-scaling was historically optimized for performance and cost, but energy consumption is now a first-class operational concern. Overprovisioning wastes power, while poorly timed scale-in actions can harm performance. Energy-aware scaling requires workload-level energy visibility, but cloud platforms traditionally expose CPU, memory, and network metrics more readily than per-workload energy consumption.

This is especially difficult in Kubernetes because multiple containers share the same node. Without attribution, operators know node-level power draw but cannot determine which pod, namespace, or application is responsible. The result is a gap between sustainability goals and actionable scheduling decisions.

### Example solution: Kepler for workload-level power attribution

Kepler, the Kubernetes-based Efficient Power Level Exporter, addresses this by exposing energy consumption metrics at container, pod, and node levels through Prometheus. It reads hardware sensors and attributes power to workloads based on resource utilization, enabling per-workload visibility that can feed energy-aware autoscaling or placement policies ([Sustainable Computing IO, 2026](https://github.com/sustainable-computing-io/kepler)). CNCF describes Kepler as a Prometheus exporter that measures pod- and node-level energy by reading hardware sensors and attributing power to containers, giving operators visibility into where energy is consumed ([CNCF, 2026](https://www.cncf.io/projects/kepler/)).

The significance is practical: energy-aware auto-scaling is not feasible unless the control loop can observe energy at the same granularity at which it makes placement and scaling decisions. Kepler supplies that missing telemetry layer. In telecom contexts, the NGMN report similarly argues that power metrics must be aligned with workload resource usage and exposed to higher-level management systems to support closed-loop optimization ([NGMN Alliance, 2024](https://www.ngmn.org/wp-content/uploads/GFN_Metering-Virt.-RAN-Infrastructure_V1.0.pdf)).

**Assessment:** Kepler is not itself a complete auto-scaler; it is an enabling measurement layer. Its value is highest when integrated with Kubernetes schedulers, HPA/KEDA-like controllers, or custom policies that optimize for performance-per-watt rather than CPU utilization alone.

## Dynamic Nature of Telecom Services

### Problem manifestation

Telecom services differ from ordinary web applications because they must satisfy strict latency, reliability, and availability requirements while running across distributed cloud, edge, and network-function infrastructures. Virtual network functions (VNFs), cloud-native network functions (CNFs), and vRAN workloads are dynamic: traffic moves geographically, radio conditions change, network slices have different priorities, and failures must be handled before users notice degradation.

Traditional offline service assurance is too slow. Telecom operators need near-real-time telemetry, analytics, and orchestration loops that can scale, heal, or relocate services while respecting ETSI NFV MANO roles and service-level constraints.

### Example solution: closed-loop telemetry-aware scheduling

Intel’s closed-loop automation white paper describes a platform resilience prototype that integrates telemetry, analytics, and automated action with NFV MANO layers. It uses platform telemetry such as temperature, power, hardware health, and performance counters, then triggers telemetry-aware scheduling or corrective actions through the appropriate orchestration layer ([Intel Network Builders, n.d.](https://builders.intel.com/docs/networkbuilders/closed-loop-platform-automation-service-healing-and-platform-resilience.pdf)).

This approach is relevant to auto-scaling because telecom scaling decisions cannot be based only on CPU or request rate. They must consider hardware health, service placement, redundancy, latency, and QoE. The Hexa-X-II 6G work also reflects this direction, describing monitoring and telemetry frameworks, multi-cluster orchestration, and RL-driven autoscaling mechanisms for multi-cluster deployments ([Hexa-X-II, 2024](https://hexa-x-ii.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Hexa-X-II_D6-3_v1.0.pdf)).

**Assessment:** telecom auto-scaling is moving from “add more replicas” toward **closed-loop service assurance**. The main unresolved issue is coordination: multiple control loops—energy, latency, reliability, and slice isolation—can conflict unless governed by policy and intent management.

## Big Data Processing

### Problem manifestation

Big data workloads create scaling pressure because data volume, velocity, and heterogeneity exceed the capacity of fixed infrastructure. Batch analytics, stream processing, and large-scale storage systems require elastic compute and storage, but scaling them is harder than scaling stateless web services. Data locality, shuffle cost, storage consistency, and long-running jobs make naive scale-out inefficient.

Hashem et al. identify major cloud big-data challenges including scalability, availability, data integrity, transformation, data quality, heterogeneity, privacy, legal issues, and governance. They also describe cloud computing as attractive for big data because it provides large-scale infrastructure for storage and processing ([Hashem et al., 2015](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~jmarty/projects/lowLatencyNetworking/papers/OntologiesForReusableData/TheRiseofBigDataInCloudComputing.pdf)).

### Example solution: cloud-based Hadoop and MapReduce-style elastic processing

A practical solution discussed in the big-data cloud literature is the use of distributed storage and processing frameworks such as HDFS, NoSQL systems, and MapReduce-like programming models on elastic cloud infrastructure. In this model, workloads are split across clusters, and additional compute nodes can be provisioned for parallel processing. Hashem et al. describe cloud usage for big data with distributed storage, query engines, Hadoop Distributed File System, and MapReduce-style processing pipelines ([Hashem et al., 2015](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~jmarty/projects/lowLatencyNetworking/papers/OntologiesForReusableData/TheRiseofBigDataInCloudComputing.pdf)).

For auto-scaling, the lesson is that scaling policy must be data-aware. Adding compute nodes may not help if data must be heavily reshuffled or if storage I/O is the bottleneck. Therefore, big-data auto-scaling should consider job stage, data locality, queue backlog, shuffle volume, and storage throughput, not just CPU utilization.

**Assessment:** the best big-data auto-scaling policies are workload-stage-aware. Scaling mappers, reducers, stream processors, or storage nodes with the same policy is inefficient because each stage has different bottlenecks.

## Cost-Effective Graph Processing

### Problem manifestation

Graph processing appears in social networks, fraud detection, knowledge graphs, routing, dependency graphs, service-function chains, and cloud scheduling. It is difficult to scale cost-effectively because graph workloads are irregular: high-degree vertices create skew, traversals generate unpredictable communication, and graph partitions often cause cross-node traffic. In cloud-native systems, graph structure also appears in microservice dependency graphs and NFV service-function-chain graphs.

Cost-effective graph processing therefore has two related problems:

1. **Data graph processing:** how to process large graph datasets without excessive compute and network cost.
2. **Infrastructure graph optimization:** how to place and scale graph-structured services such as microservices or VNFs while minimizing latency, bandwidth, and cost.

### Example solution: graph-aware NFV placement and learning-based orchestration

Recent NFV research treats service-function chains as graph-structured placement problems. The 2026 survey on traffic-aware NFV placement reports that optimization objectives now extend beyond latency to include cost, energy efficiency, reliability, security, scaling elasticity, and online decision-making. It also notes the growing use of graph neural networks and deep reinforcement learning for VNF placement and service-function-chain fault tolerance, while warning that explainability, training stability, and benchmark realism remain open challenges ([Rana, 2026](https://ijcaonline.org/archives/volume187/number85/rana-2026-ijca-926371.pdf)).

A representative solution direction is to combine graph-aware modeling with online optimization: represent services, VNFs, links, and dependencies as a graph; predict or observe traffic demand; then decide placement, scaling, and migration actions to reduce cost and latency. This directly connects graph processing with auto-scaling because scaling a node in the graph changes traffic flow and downstream bottlenecks.

**Assessment:** graph-aware auto-scaling should not scale components independently. Scaling a vertex without considering edge traffic and downstream capacity can increase cost while failing to improve end-to-end latency. The strongest future systems will combine dependency graphs, traffic prediction, and constrained optimization.

## Workload Prediction

### Problem manifestation

Workload prediction is the central challenge of proactive scaling. Underprediction causes SLA violations; overprediction wastes money and energy. Forecasting is difficult because workloads combine periodic behavior, sudden bursts, long-tail events, marketing campaigns, failures, and adversarial traffic. In security-sensitive contexts, denial-of-service traffic can trigger unnecessary scale-out and increase operating cost, showing that availability and security interact directly with auto-scaling ([Al-Dhuraibi et al., 2014](http://users.encs.concordia.ca/~abdelw/papers/CASCON14-Autoscaling.pdf)).

Prediction is also complicated by stateful microservices. Scaling stateful services requires preserving persistent state, session continuity, and data locality while managing migration overhead, cold-start latency, and QoS degradation during transitions ([Rhee & Yang, 2026](https://jceim.org/index.php/ojs/article/view/182)).

### Example solution: forecasting-driven proactive autoscaling with deep learning

The 2026 Springer study on federated LLM training in edge environments proposes an integrated framework coupling workload forecasting with autoscaling. It uses workload traces, including CPU utilization and communication overhead, to train forecasting models and map predictions to pod replica allocations. The study evaluates LSTM, GRU, BiLSTM, CNN, CNN-LSTM, and Transformer models on real workload traces, finding that proactive autoscaling guided by forecasts outperforms reactive autoscaling in resource performance and stability, with CNN-LSTM producing the most resource-efficient replica behavior ([Kumar et al., 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11276-026-04224-1)).

Another important line is DRL-based cloud scheduling. Li et al. argue that DRL is well suited to complex cloud scheduling because it combines deep neural networks with reinforcement learning and can learn scheduling policies in scenarios where classical algorithms struggle ([Li et al., 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-024-10756-9)).

**Assessment:** prediction should be treated as a probabilistic input, not an unquestioned command. Production systems should use forecast confidence, anomaly detection, fallback reactive rules, and cost-aware safety margins. The evidence favors hybrid predictive-reactive systems over purely predictive systems.

## Additional Cross-Cutting Challenges

### Reliability of the Auto-Scaling Process

Auto-scaling is itself a QoS risk. If the scaling process fails—because metrics are missing, APIs fail, controllers crash, or cloud quotas are exhausted—the application may violate performance and scalability requirements or incur unnecessary cost. The CASCON survey explicitly identifies reliability of the auto-scaling process as under-managed and notes that failures can cause QoS violations and unnecessary cost ([Al-Dhuraibi et al., 2014](http://users.encs.concordia.ca/~abdelw/papers/CASCON14-Autoscaling.pdf)).

**Solution direction:** auto-scalers should be designed like critical control-plane software: replicated controllers, safe defaults, audit logs, rate limits, quota awareness, and rollback policies.

### Hybrid and Multi-Cloud Interoperability

Hybrid-cloud auto-scaling remains difficult because public and private clouds expose incompatible auto-scaling mechanisms. The CASCON survey identifies interoperability barriers when parts of an application run on private cloud and other parts run on public cloud ([Al-Dhuraibi et al., 2014](http://users.encs.concordia.ca/~abdelw/papers/CASCON14-Autoscaling.pdf)).

**Solution direction:** Kubernetes-based abstraction, external metrics APIs, service mesh telemetry, and policy-driven orchestration can reduce—but not eliminate—provider-specific differences.

### Security-Aware Auto-Scaling

Auto-scaling can amplify attacks. For example, DoS traffic may trigger scale-out, increasing operational cost while not improving legitimate user experience. This is a direct interaction between security, cost, and availability ([Al-Dhuraibi et al., 2014](http://users.encs.concordia.ca/~abdelw/papers/CASCON14-Autoscaling.pdf)).

**Solution direction:** integrate traffic classification, anomaly detection, rate limiting, and bot mitigation before scaling decisions. Scaling should respond to legitimate demand, not merely raw request volume.
