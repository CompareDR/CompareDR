# Auto-Scaling Technology Classification

Auto-scaling controllers are commonly separated by *scaling timing*. A reactive autoscaler "performs actions … when workload change has already happened," whereas a proactive autoscaler provisions ahead of predictable change ([Qu et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1609.09224v6)). Reactive control is conventionally implemented as a MAPE-K (Monitor–Analyze–Plan–Execute over a Knowledge base) feedback loop, which provides the canonical autoscaling architecture ([Qu et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1609.09224v6); [Chen et al. 2018](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/33018/1/PubSub10569_Chen.pdf)). Because the trigger is a measured breach, every reactive method must answer two questions: which metric(s) and thresholds define the breach, and how the ensuing scale-up/scale-down actions are damped to avoid oscillation. Proactive methods invert this logic: they forecast future demand from historical data and provision capacity before the demand arrives, eliminating or shrinking the detection-and-provisioning lag that causes SLA violations during traffic spikes.

## Reactive Methods

### Threshold / rule-based scaling

**Principle.** The dominant production method sets static or dynamic thresholds on CPU, memory, request rate, queue length, or response time, scaling out on breach and scaling in on sustained low usage. AWS Auto Scaling exposes target tracking (maintains a target metric value), step scaling (adds/removes capacity in steps proportional to the alarm breach), and simple scaling (a single threshold with a cooldown), plus scheduled actions ([AWS Auto Scaling](https://aws.amazon.com/autoscaling/)). Azure Autoscale evaluates live metrics against predefined rules and requires a scale-out/scale-in rule pair ([Azure autoscaling guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/auto-scaling)). Google Compute Engine's autoscaler uses target utilization signals, computes a recommended VM count for each signal, and adopts the largest; an initialization period (default 60 s) prevents instances still booting from skewing scale-out decisions ([Google Compute Engine autoscaler](https://cloud.google.com/compute/docs/autoscaler)). Kubernetes HPA scales on CPU/memory utilization with `desiredReplicas = ceil[currentReplicas × (currentMetricValue / desiredMetricValue)]`; a tolerance of 0.1 suppresses minor fluctuations, scale-up can occur immediately, and scale-down is constrained by a stabilization window (default 300 s) ([Kloia: Advanced HPA in Kubernetes](https://www.kloia.com/blog/advanced-hpa-in-kubernetes)).

**Advantages.** Simplicity, transparency, and broad platform support.

**Limitations.** Reaction lag, threshold-tuning burden, and oscillation when thresholds are too tight or metrics noisy. The aggregation windows differ by platform: HPA's controller sync period defaults to 15 s, Azure and Google evaluate averaged metrics over a configured time grain, and AWS alarms aggregate over a specified period. Vertical scaling is a complementary dimension — Azure notes it "often requires making the system temporarily unavailable while it's being redeployed" and is therefore less commonly automated than horizontal scaling ([Azure autoscaling guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/auto-scaling)).

### Queuing-theory-based scaling

**Principle.** This method models the service as a queueing system and computes the number of servers needed to keep queue length or response time below a target, given the arrival rate λ and per-server service rate μ. In the M/M/c (Erlang-C) model — Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, *c* identical servers — the system is stable only when utilization ρ = λ/(cμ) < 1; Erlang's C formula gives the probability that an arrival must wait, and the average response time is C(c, λ/μ)/(cμ − λ) + 1/μ ([M/M/c queue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M/M/c_queue)). Solving that relationship for *c* at a target response time yields the required server count. The M/G/1 variant generalizes to arbitrary service-time distributions for single-server analysis.

**Advantages.** Principled, closed-form capacity estimation tied directly to a performance target, avoiding trial-and-error threshold tuning.

**Limitations.** The Poisson-arrival, exponential/general-service, and steady-state assumptions break down for bursty, non-stationary cloud workloads; λ and μ are hard to estimate dynamically; and the models ignore boot latency and multi-resource coupling. Qu et al. classify queueing-based approaches within the reactive family because they still react to measured load ([Qu et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1609.09224v6)). A specific peer-reviewed study mapping M/M/c or M/G/1 to a concrete autoscaling controller was not verified in the source material; the model mechanics above are verified against the Erlang-C reference, so autoscaling-specific empirical claims should be treated as unverified.

### Control-theory-based scaling

**Principle.** This family treats autoscaling as a feedback control problem and applies PID controllers, adaptive control, model predictive control (MPC), and autonomic MAPE-K loops to reduce oscillation and over/under-provisioning. Ullah et al. provide a standalone taxonomy of control-theoretic elasticity, noting that feedback controllers "are designed to be stable in order to avoid oscillation and settle quickly to the steady state by appropriately responding to disturbances," making them well suited to SLO objectives such as response time and throughput ([Ullah et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10586-018-2807-6)). A PID controller adjusts capacity from the proportional, integral, and derivative terms of the error between desired and measured metrics; adaptive control retunes its own parameters as workload dynamics shift; MPC uses a system model to predict behavior over a finite horizon and optimizes capacity subject to constraints; and the MAPE-K loop is the IBM autonomic pattern that underpins many of these designs ([Qu et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1609.09224v6); [Chen et al. 2018](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/33018/1/PubSub10569_Chen.pdf)).

**Advantages.** Principled stability and constraint handling, explicitly targeting oscillation reduction.

**Limitations.** Requires an accurate system model, careful controller tuning, and (for MPC) non-trivial online computation; model mismatch degrades performance. The specific stability conditions and head-to-head empirical comparisons of PID/MPC autoscalers versus naive threshold rules were not verified in the source material; MPC-specific quantitative results remain unverified.

### Fuzzy-logic-based scaling

**Principle.** Fuzzy controllers use membership functions and a fuzzy rule base (IF-THEN rules) to map imprecise, noisy metric values onto linguistic labels (e.g., "low," "medium," "high") and then to scaling actions, handling multi-indicator uncertainty and conflicting objectives without a precise mathematical model. Arabnejad et al. implement and compare two fuzzy-logic autoscalers in OpenStack — Fuzzy SARSA learning (FSL) and Fuzzy Q-learning (FQL) — in which a fuzzy inference system reduces the state space and the RL component learns and modifies fuzzy scaling rules at runtime; they report that both handle sudden and periodic load while reducing cost and preventing SLA violations ([Arabnejad et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.07114)).

**Advantages.** Tolerance of metric uncertainty, interpretable rules, and natural combination of multiple indicators.

**Limitations.** The design cost of the rule base and membership functions is substantial, rules can explode combinatorially as inputs grow, and the controller may need online learning to adapt to drift. The performance depends on the quality of the thresholds ([Arabnejad et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.07114)).

### Scheduled / time-based scaling

**Principle.** This method provisions capacity according to a pre-defined schedule for predictable periodic demand and is typically used as a complement to reactive rules. Azure's example scales out to 10 instances on weekdays and in to 4 on weekends ([Azure autoscaling guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/auto-scaling)); Google Compute Engine permits up to 128 scaling schedules per instance group, each defining a minimum capacity over a start time, duration, and recurrence ([Google Compute Engine autoscaler](https://cloud.google.com/compute/docs/autoscaler)); AWS supports scheduled actions ([AWS Auto Scaling](https://aws.amazon.com/autoscaling/)).

**Advantages.** Handles known periodic demand deterministically and reduces exposure to reactive lag.

**Limitations.** It cannot absorb unexpected spikes, schedules require ongoing maintenance, and capacity is wasted if actual demand deviates from the schedule.

### Node-level reactive provisioning (Karpenter)

**Principle.** Karpenter is a Kubernetes node autoscaler that provisions nodes just-in-time in response to pending pods. It "focuses on scheduling pods that the Kubernetes scheduler has marked as unschedulable," batches those pending pods, binpacks them, and dynamically selects instance types — without pre-defined node groups — and can operate alongside static capacity such as EKS Managed Node Groups or EC2 Auto Scaling Groups and alongside Cluster Autoscaler in a mixed model ([Karpenter FAQ](https://karpenter.sh/v1.0/faq/)).

**Advantages.** Directly addresses node-provisioning lag by right-sizing nodes to actual pod resource requests and removing node-group constraints.

**Limitations.** It is still reactive to pods that are already unschedulable, and aggressive binpacking can trade fragmentation for utilization.

**Shared failure modes and selection.** The unifying weakness of reactive methods is reaction lag: capacity is added only after demand has already arrived, so cold-start and VM boot latency create a window of under-provisioning. Google's autoscaler treats this explicitly — the initialization period (default 60 s) exists because "while an application is initializing on an instance, the instance's usage data might not reflect normal circumstances," and its predictive mode scales out five minutes ahead of forecasted load to compensate ([Google Compute Engine autoscaler](https://cloud.google.com/compute/docs/autoscaler)). A second failure mode is oscillation/thrashing, defined by Qu et al. as the autoscaler frequently performing opposite actions within a short period ([Qu et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1609.09224v6)); platforms damp it with cooldown periods ([IBM](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/autoscale)), HPA's tolerance and 300 s downscale-stabilization window ([Kloia](https://www.kloia.com/blog/advanced-hpa-in-kubernetes)), and Azure's requirement for paired scale-out/scale-in rules ([Azure](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/auto-scaling)). Over- and under-provisioning are the cost/SLA poles: over-provisioning wastes monetary cost, under-provisioning violates SLAs ([Qu et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1609.09224v6); [AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/autoscaling/)). The threshold-tuning burden is explicit in the fuzzy-controller literature, where "the performance depends on the quality of the thresholds" ([Arabnejad et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.07114)), and metric noise is amplified when monitoring intervals are very short, which raises monitoring cost and is "likely to cause oscillations" ([Qu et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1609.09224v6)). In practice, production stacks combine these — scheduled actions and target tracking as the reactive baseline, with predictive or control-based layers added as a safety net.

## Proactive Methods

Proactive auto-scaling inverts the reactive logic: rather than waiting for a monitored metric to breach a threshold, the system forecasts future demand from historical data and provisions capacity before the demand arrives. The forecast is converted into a capacity plan whose lead time must exceed the instance or pod initialization time; if the lead time is too short, a proactive decision degenerates into a reactive one. In practice, a forecast is turned into a scaling action by aggregating historical metrics into time windows, inferring a point forecast (ideally with a confidence bound), translating the forecast into a required replica count with a safety margin, and provisioning early enough that new instances finish initializing before the predicted load arrives. Forecast uncertainty is the central risk: over-forecasting inflates the safety margin and causes over-provisioning, while under-forecasting causes SLA violations.

### Time-series / statistical forecasting

**Principle.** Fit trend and seasonality to historical workload (moving average, exponential smoothing, Holt-Winters, ARIMA/SARIMA) and extrapolate. Required input is historical workload time series (CPU, request rate); forecast horizon is minutes to hours, multi-step ahead. Arbat et al. survey ARIMA/exponential smoothing for proactive autoscaling ([Arbat et al. 2022](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/21509/21258)).

**Advantages.** Simple, interpretable, low training cost, no large datasets.

**Limitations.** Struggles with non-stationary, bursty workloads; model must be re-fit as patterns shift.

**Vendor implementations.** Google Cloud predictive autoscaling forecasts future load from a MIG's history and scales out in advance. It requires 3 days of CPU-based autoscaling history before predictions begin, uses up to 3 weeks of load history, and is restricted to CPU utilization as the scaling metric. It predicts daily and weekly patterns but explicitly does not predict monthly, annual, or one-time events, nor patterns shorter than 10 minutes; for those, Google recommends schedule-based autoscaling. It requires applications with long initialization times (initialization period > 2 minutes) to benefit, and it "scales out the MIG in advance of predicted load, so that new instances are ready to serve when the load arrives" ([Google Cloud predictive autoscaling](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/autoscaler/predictive-autoscaling)). AWS predictive scaling for Amazon EC2 Auto Scaling analyzes historical load data to detect daily or weekly patterns, forecasts future capacity needs, and proactively increases capacity. It is suited to cyclical traffic, recurring on-and-off workloads, and applications with long initialization times, and AWS notes it can "potentially save you money on your EC2 bill by helping you avoid the need to over provision capacity." Predictive scaling schedules minimum capacity; dynamic target-tracking scaling then adjusts on top of the forecast ([AWS predictive scaling](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/autoscaling/ec2/userguide/ec2-auto-scaling-predictive-scaling.html)).

### Supervised ML regression

**Principle.** Learn a mapping from engineered features to future demand (SVR, KNN, random forest, XGBoost, multiple linear regression, MLP). Required input is historical metrics plus engineered features (time-of-day, day-of-week, lagged values); forecast horizon is minutes to hours. Representative work includes an SVM-based study ([Singh et al. 2023](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10114033/)) and MLScale, which combines a neural network with multiple linear regression ([Lemke 2022](https://www.net.in.tum.de/fileadmin/TUM/NET/NET-2022-07-1/NET-2022-07-1_11.pdf)).

**Advantages.** Captures nonlinear relationships; no explicit seasonality modeling needed.

**Limitations.** Requires feature engineering and labeled training data; cold start for new services. Lemke 2022 note that MLScale's supervised approach is fundamentally reactive because it lacks the anticipatory capability of RL ([Lemke 2022](https://www.net.in.tum.de/fileadmin/TUM/NET/NET-2022-07-1/NET-2022-07-1_11.pdf)).

### Deep learning sequence models

**Principle.** Capture long-range temporal dependencies and nonlinear patterns with LSTM, GRU, CNN, Transformer; multi-step ahead prediction. Required input is long historical sequences (window lengths typically hours); forecast horizon is hours, multi-step. Arbat et al. report that their WGAN-gp Transformer achieves up to 5.1% higher prediction accuracy and 5× faster inference than the LSTM baseline on Google Cluster, Facebook Hadoop, Azure 2017, and Wikipedia traces ([Arbat et al. 2022](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/21509/21258)). Golshani et al. propose a CNN-based proactive auto-scaler ([Golshani et al. 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0743731521000836)), and Manhary et al. use a BiLSTM for workload forecasting ([Manhary et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14962-5)).

**Advantages.** Highest accuracy on complex, seasonal workloads; handles sudden spikes better than statistical models.

**Limitations.** Data-hungry; high training/inference cost; accuracy degrades with longer horizons; retraining needed for concept drift. Manhary et al. note that Transformer-based forecasting models such as TFT introduce computational overhead that "limits real-time deployment," with 3.1× higher GPU memory usage than LSTM-based methods ([Manhary et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14962-5)).

### Reinforcement learning

**Principle.** Learn a scaling policy from reward signals (SLA, cost, energy) via environment interaction; state/action/reward formulation. Forecast horizon is one or few steps ahead (policy-dependent). Representative work includes RLPAS (SARSA) ([Lemke 2022](https://www.net.in.tum.de/fileadmin/TUM/NET/NET-2022-07-1/NET-2022-07-1_11.pdf)), MARL Ape-X ([Manhary et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14962-5)), and a PPO-based stability-aware framework ([Shaikh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28790v1)).

**Advantages.** Optimizes long-term cumulative reward; balances multiple objectives; adapts to environment.

**Limitations.** Exploration risk during training; slow convergence; sim-to-real gap; hyperparameter sensitivity. Manhary et al. note that existing DQN methods "require millions of time steps to converge" ([Manhary et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14962-5)).

**Classification note.** RL autoscalers are placed under Proactive in this taxonomy because the learned policy anticipates future states rather than reacting to a current threshold breach. Lemke 2022 supports this placement: their SARSA-based RLPAS "predicts required resource spikes and provisions resources proactively," giving it a "decisive advantage compared to the reactive MLScale" ([Lemke 2022](https://www.net.in.tum.de/fileadmin/TUM/NET/NET-2022-07-1/NET-2022-07-1_11.pdf)). However, there is a classification tension: value-based methods such as Q-learning or DQN that act on the current state without an explicit forecast are functionally reactive. Manhary et al. characterize traditional RL as failing "to anticipate workload trends, leading to a 26% increase in SLA violations during traffic spikes" ([Manhary et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14962-5)). The proactive label is most defensible when the RL agent is coupled with a forecasting model or a reward structure that penalizes future SLA violations.

**Attention augmentation.** Shaikh et al. propose a stability-aware framework that unifies workload forecasting and control via an Attention-Enhanced Double-Stacked LSTM integrated within a PPO agent. The deep temporal attention mechanism "selectively weight[s] historical states, effectively filtering high-frequency noise while retaining critical precursors of demand shifts," addressing the "temporal blindness" of standard DRL agents that operate on a single-step observation window. Validated on real-world Azure Functions traces, the approach reduces 90th percentile latency by approximately 29% and replica churn by 39% relative to a single-layer LSTM baseline ([Shaikh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28790v1)).

### Hybrid / ensemble methods

**Principle.** Combine statistical and learned models, or ML forecasting with RL/control (LSTM+RL, LSTM-MARL). Required input is historical data plus environment interaction; forecast horizon is hours, multi-step. Manhary et al. propose LSTM-MARL-Ape-X, coupling BiLSTM forecasting with multi-agent reinforcement learning in a distributed Ape-X architecture ([Manhary et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14962-5)). Zhong et al. combine LSTM forecasting with RL ([Zhong et al. 2019](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1742-6596/1237/2/022033)).

**Advantages.** Forecasting accuracy plus decision optimization; end-to-end training possible.

**Limitations.** Most complex; highest computational cost; integration and tuning burden.

**Hybrid safety nets in production.** In practice, reactive and proactive modes are combined as a safety net rather than deployed in isolation. Google Cloud predictive autoscaling and AWS predictive scaling layer forecasts on top of reactive signals (CPU utilization, target tracking), so real-time metrics still trigger scale-out if the forecast is wrong. KEDA provides event-driven autoscaling for Kubernetes: it monitors event sources (e.g., Kafka topics) and "feeds that data to Kubernetes and the HPA (Horizontal Pod Autoscaler) to drive rapid scale of a resource," scaling to zero when no events are pending and activating on arrival; its scaling-modifiers feature lets users compose multiple triggers with formulas, and it supports caching, pausing, and activation thresholds ([KEDA](https://keda.sh/docs/latest/concepts/scaling-deployments/)). Kubernetes HPA and VPA provide the reactive baseline; Karpenter provisions nodes just-in-time to reduce node-provisioning lag; Knative's KPA scales from zero based on request rate. Scheduled actions (e.g., AWS scheduled scaling, Google Cloud schedule-based autoscaling) handle predictable periodic demand that predictive models may not capture (one-time events, sub-10-minute patterns), complementing the learning-based methods. This hybrid architecture — forecast-driven pre-provisioning plus reactive safety nets — is the dominant production pattern because it bounds the downside of forecast error while capturing the latency benefits of acting before demand arrives.

**Evidence boundaries.** The full texts of Singh et al. 2023, Golshani et al. 2021, Zhong et al. 2019, Dogani et al. 2023, and Machiraju et al. 2026 could not be fetched from their primary URLs; their mechanisms are described from search-result abstracts and secondary citations, and specific numerical results from those papers should be treated as unverified until the primary sources are consulted. The exact input window lengths, feature sets, and accuracy metrics for several deep-learning autoscalers are not consistently reported across the accessible sources. For reactive methods, the full texts of Alipour & Liu (CASCON 2014) and Dogani et al. (2023) could not be retrieved; the specific empirical costs of reactive lag and the MPC-specific stability conditions remain open questions.

# Main Challenges and Solutions

## Energy Efficiency

**Problem.** Auto-scaling decisions change power draw through four channels: (1) the number of active servers, VMs, or containers; (2) CPU frequency and voltage (DVFS); (3) idle-but-powered resources; and (4) cooling and other non-IT infrastructure. Naive reactive scaling increases energy when it over-provisions to avoid SLA violations, leaves scaled-in resources idle-but-powered, causes VM/container sprawl, or triggers frequent scale-up/down cycles whose boot and teardown energy is never amortized. Cañete et al. observe that in edge infrastructures of up to 500 nodes, non-IT appliances such as cooling and lighting can consume ~33%–52% of total energy, so node-count decisions propagate directly to facility-level power ([Cañete et al. 2023](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/193716/1/CloudAM2022.pdf)). The SLA-vs-energy trade-off is fundamental: maintaining headroom for QoS keeps servers powered at low utilization, while aggressive scale-in saves energy but risks violations. The benchmark concept of energy proportionality — an ideal system whose power draw is proportional to utilization and near-zero at idle — is one that real servers fall well short of, since a server at low utilization still draws a large fraction of its peak power; idle-but-powered resources are therefore the core waste that autoscaling must eliminate. The urgency is macro-scale: Katal et al. project data-center energy consumption rising from 200 TWh in 2016 to 2967 TWh in 2030 ([Katal et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9424070/)). Common metrics include kWh, PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness), carbon intensity/carbon footprint, idle power, and energy proportionality.

**Anchor study: Cañete et al. 2023 — proactive energy-aware edge auto-scaling.** The problem is that edge auto-scaling must reduce energy without violating QoS, and naive reactive scaling causes frequent, energy-intensive scaling cycles. The proposed solution is a proactive horizontal auto-scaling framework for edge infrastructures that models both base (idle) and dynamic (application execution) energy of edge nodes and the energy of the node-scaling mechanism itself; a workload predictor forecasts future demand to provision/de-provision nodes ahead of time. EdgeCloudSim simulations with a Shanghai Telecom workload show up to a 92.5% decrease in energy consumption and a failed request rate of up to 0%, with reasonable execution times ([Cañete et al. 2023](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/193716/1/CloudAM2022.pdf)). The approach reduces the costly, energy-intensive frequent scaling of reactive methods, but edge heterogeneity and limited resources make prediction and scaling more complex, and wrong predictions risk failed requests.

**Trade-off and complementary mechanism.** Energy-aware autoscaling is not simply "scale down more aggressively," because that trades energy for SLA violations. Instead, solutions jointly optimize a QoS-constrained energy objective. Dougherty et al.'s SCORCH captures VM configuration options in feature models, transforms them into constraint satisfaction problems, and uses a constraint solver to derive the optimal auto-scaling queue (pre-booted VM configurations) that meets an auto-scaling response-time guarantee while minimizing power consumption and operating cost; an Amazon EC2 case study reports a 50% or better reduction in both power consumption and operating cost ([Dougherty et al. 2012](https://www.cs.wm.edu/~dcschmidt/PDF/Model-driven-Auto-scaling-of-Green-Cloud-Computing-Infrastructure.pdf)). VM consolidation and DVFS interact with these decisions: consolidation packs workloads onto fewer hosts so idle hosts can be powered down, while DVFS adjusts CPU frequency and voltage to match provisioned capacity; carbon-aware scheduling extends the same logic by shifting workloads in time or space toward lower-carbon energy. The residual challenges are prediction error, edge and resource heterogeneity, and the need to amortize boot/teardown energy across scaling decisions.

## Dynamic Nature of Telecom Services

**Problem.** Network Functions Virtualization (NFV) decouples network functions from dedicated hardware and runs them as software-based Virtualized Network Functions (VNFs); VNFs are chained into Service Function Chains (SFCs), and all traffic matching a policy must traverse the chain in sequence ([Toosi et al., ElasticSFC](http://www.buyya.com/papers/ElasticSFC.pdf)). This ordering makes scaling a chain-aware problem: adding or removing a VNF changes the path and the end-to-end latency of every flow, not just the load on one stateless service. In 5G, core functions such as the UPF, AMF, and SMF are deployed as VNFs, while network slicing and multi-access edge computing (MEC) add orchestration domains; ETSI NFV MANO is the reference management-and-orchestration architecture, and 5G service classes (eMBB, URLLC, mMTC) impose different throughput and latency requirements.

Telecom traffic is also strongly bursty. DASA notes that data traffic "usually has peaks during a day while having relative low utilization in the rest of time (e.g., in the midnight)," so operators conventionally leave spare capacity to handle peaks ([Ren et al., DASA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05803)). Static over-provisioning for peaks therefore coexists with under-utilization at off-peak times, which is precisely the condition auto-scaling should remove.

Two further constraints distinguish telecom scaling from generic cloud scaling. First, scale-out is not instantaneous: DASA reports that a VNF instance "cannot be available immediately" and that startup lag "could be as long as 10 minutes or more" in Microsoft Azure, varying over time ([Ren et al., DASA PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.05803)). Second, legacy network equipment often remains powered on alongside virtualized resources, so the marginal capacity gained by adding one VNF depends on the legacy block's size—DASA calls this the "non-constant issue" and shows that adding one VNF can increase capacity by 100% when legacy capacity equals one VNF, but by less than 1% when legacy capacity equals 100 VNFs ([Ren et al., DASA PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.05803)). Finally, the monitoring/metering layer itself can become a bottleneck: Zafar et al. show that VNF auto-scaling needs a comprehensive metering component to store and process metrics, and that time-series storage size, memory consumption, and alarm-retrieval delay directly affect scaling responsiveness ([Zafar et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/22/19/7597)).

**Anchor study: DASA (Ren et al. 2016).** The problem is that telecom operators statically over-provision for peaks, while VNF startup lag and legacy capacity make naive reactive scaling ineffective. DASA models the system as a queue in which legacy network equipment is a reserved block of servers and VNF instances are powered on and off according to the number of job requests. It derives closed-form operation-cost and performance metrics, then uses a recursive algorithm to choose the number of VNF instances that minimizes operation cost subject to a latency upper-bound. The reported result is that DASA "can significantly reduce operation cost given the latency upper-bound," and the analytical model lets operators evaluate the cost-performance trade-off without wide deployment ([Ren et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05803)). Its distinctive contribution is making legacy capacity and VM setup lag explicit inputs to the scaling decision. The constraints are the latency upper-bound, legacy capacity (the "non-constant issue"), and VM setup time.

**Complementary mechanism: ElasticSFC (Toosi et al. 2019).** ElasticSFC defines a unified framework for elastic service chains and a heuristic that minimizes operational cost while meeting the end-to-end latency of the chain. It is one of the first approaches to combine horizontal and vertical VNF scaling simultaneously, and it adds dynamic bandwidth allocation through dynamic flow scheduling and VNF migration. Evaluation in the extended CloudSimSDN simulator with realistic network policies and web-application workload traces shows that the algorithm "can reduce the cost of SFC deployment and SLA violation significantly" ([Toosi et al. 2019](http://www.buyya.com/papers/ElasticSFC.pdf)). The quantitative cost/SLA percentages are not stated in the retrieved portion of the paper and remain unverified here.

**Evidence boundary.** A traffic-aware hybrid reactive/proactive approach for VNF resources in 5G/B5G (TAHA, Tiwari & Sivalingam, IEEE ICC 2025) is indexed ([IEEE Xplore record](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/11160960/)), but its abstract could not be retrieved in this research pass; its specific mechanism, cost/latency results, and constraints are unverified and are not asserted as evidence here.

## Big Data Processing

**Problem.** Big data frameworks — Apache Spark, Apache Flink, Apache Hadoop — run long batch or streaming jobs whose resource needs vary sharply across execution stages. In Spark, a job is divided into stages separated by shuffle barriers; one stage may be CPU-bound (e.g., a transformation) while the next is I/O-bound (e.g., a shuffle read), so the resource profile changes at every stage boundary. Shuffle-heavy phases write and read large volumes of intermediate data, creating disk I/O bottlenecks and memory pressure. Data skew leaves most executors idle while a few stragglers dominate runtime. Streaming workloads (Spark Structured Streaming, Flink) add bursty, non-stationary arrival rates and low-latency requirements that static allocation cannot serve efficiently.

Scaling mid-job is difficult because tasks are not freely migratable. Spark's minimum allocation granularity is one CPU core per task, and its stage-level `ResourceProfile` mechanism — which can set different executor sizes per stage — requires user-code changes and lacks SQL support ([Wu et al., PVLDB 2024](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p3759-shi.pdf)). A large-scale production study of ByteDance's Spark infrastructure — over 1.7 million jobs and 500 PB of shuffled data daily — quantified the consequences: average CPU utilization was only 47.98% and memory utilization 42.95%, with data scan and shuffle block time consuming more than 45% of total compute time. The study identified three root causes: slow I/Os causing CPU/memory idleness, coarse-grained resource control causing wastage, and sub-optimal job configurations yielding low utilization ([Wu et al., PVLDB 2024](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p3759-shi.pdf)).

**Anchor study: Wu et al., PVLDB 2024 — fine-grained resource control and shuffle-service redesign.** The problem is that at million-job scale, coarse-grained resource control and slow shuffle I/O dominate inefficiency. The proposed solution is fine-grained resource control (`milliCores`, `memoryBurst`) and shuffle-service redesign. The reported result is a 22% CPU-utilization improvement; the authors attribute this to fine-grained resource control and shuffle-service redesign, not to elastic scaling alone ([Wu et al., PVLDB 2024](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p3759-shi.pdf)). The boundary is that this is a large-scale production Spark infrastructure study; the improvement comes from resource control and shuffle redesign, so it does not by itself demonstrate that generic autoscaling improves big data efficiency.

**Complementary evidence.** Vennu and Yepuru conducted a controlled performance study of autoscaling a containerized Spark application on Kubernetes, comparing HPA and VPA against a no-autoscaling baseline using TPC-DS workloads. With VPA, Spark achieved better execution time than the baseline because vertical scaling adjusts per-pod CPU and memory requests to match actual usage, reducing over-provisioning on shuffle-heavy stages; however, VPA introduced CPU-utilization overhead. With HPA, Spark suffered overhead in both execution time and CPU utilization because horizontally adding executor pods triggers cold-start latency and the CPU-based scaling signal does not correlate with the stage-dependent, shuffle-dominated resource profile of Spark jobs. The thesis concluded that neither generic Kubernetes autoscaler fully aligns with Spark's internal resource model ([Vennu & Yepuru 2022](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1641422/FULLTEXT02.pdf)). Will et al. evaluated whether autoscaling batch data-processing jobs based on resource demand actually improves resource efficiency on Google Dataproc Serverless and found no significant resource efficiency gain from autoscaling over static resource allocations; they traced this to the inelasticity of node size (fixed CPU-to-memory ratios) and the inelasticity of the memory-to-CPU ratio ([Will et al. 2025](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/346542/); [arXiv:2501.14456](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14456)). Production services that consume framework-aware signals report 25–40% cost reductions: Databricks optimized autoscaling removes idle workers mid-job using Spark shuffle and executor statistics ([Databricks 2018](https://www.databricks.com/blog/2018/05/02/introducing-databricks-optimized-auto-scaling.html)), and Google Cloud Dataproc autoscaler uses YARN metrics with shuffle-aware decommissioning ([Google Cloud 2023](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/data-analytics/understanding-dataproc-autoscaler-enhancements/)).

## Cost-Effective Graph Processing

**Problem.** Cost-effective auto-scaling is unusually hard for large-scale graph processing because the dominant execution model—Bulk Synchronous Parallel (BSP), used by Pregel, Apache Giraph, and Spark's GraphX—forces every worker to finish a *superstep* before any worker can begin the next, so capacity cannot be trimmed mid-iteration without stalling the whole job ([Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](http://www.buyya.com/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf); [Spark GraphX documentation](https://spark.apache.org/docs/latest/graphx-programming-guide.html)). Graph algorithms such as PageRank, BFS, and community detection are also irregular—poor data locality, high data-access-to-computation ratio, and workload skew across partitions—so under a pay-as-you-go cloud model, static provisioning must be sized for the heaviest partition and the longest superstep, over-provisioning and wasting money for the rest of the run ([iGiraph, IEEE Cloud 2016](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/~rbuyya/papers/iGiraph-Cloud2016.pdf)). Providers offer heterogeneous VMs and pricing models (on-demand, spot, reserved), and users must pick "the best combination of resources (appropriate number of resources with the right type) out of the available resource pool at any iteration" ([iGiraph, IEEE Cloud 2016](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/~rbuyya/papers/iGiraph-Cloud2016.pdf); [Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](http://www.buyya.com/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)). Virtualized commodity hardware adds imperfect VM placement and uncontrollable bandwidth/latency, so scaling decisions directly affect both runtime and dollar cost ([Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](http://www.buyya.com/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)).

**Anchor study: Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021 — cost-aware heterogeneous VM selection and superstep-aware scaling.** The problem is that BSP superstep barriers and irregular graph workloads make static provisioning over-provision, and homogeneous VM scaling cannot match per-superstep computation requirements. The proposed solution develops algorithms that "automatically adjust the number and types of virtual machines according to the computation requirements for convergent graph applications," coupled with a smart profiling mechanism and a characteristic-based dynamic repartitioning method that distributes partitions across VMs according to VM types. At each iteration, the system selects the appropriate VM count and instance types from the cloud's heterogeneous pool, while repartitioning the graph so that heavier partitions land on more powerful VMs. The reported result is a greater-than-50% dollar-cost reduction compared with Apache Giraph, while also delivering faster execution ([Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](http://www.buyya.com/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)). The approach is designed for *convergent* graph applications, where the active vertex set shrinks over iterations, so reducing VM count across supersteps is safe; it depends on cloud VM pricing and availability, and its savings shrink for non-convergent or communication-bound graphs.

**Complementary mechanisms.** iGiraph (Heidari, Calheiros, and Buyya, IEEE Cloud 2016) is the direct predecessor and supplies the cost-model foundation: it uses dynamic repartitioning based on messaging patterns to reduce the number of VMs during processing—merging or migrating partitions and vertices across workers—and leverages high-degree-vertex concepts at partition level together with the convergent level of the running algorithm; it works only with homogeneous resources instead of heterogeneous VMs ([iGiraph, IEEE Cloud 2016](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/~rbuyya/papers/iGiraph-Cloud2016.pdf); [Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](http://www.buyya.com/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)). FaaSGraph (Liu et al., ASPLOS 2024) attacks the same cost problem from the infrastructure side: IaaS deployments reserve servers for peak load and keep them always-on, which is inelastic and wasteful—the paper observes 85.7% resource waste over a day and tail latency rising from 1.5 s to over 30 s during a query spike. Its solution is a serverless-native graph computing scheme that co-designs a data-centric serverless execution model with a graph processing paradigm, reporting up to 8.3× end-to-end performance improvement, up to 52.4% memory-usage reduction, and 85.7% monetary-cost reduction versus state-of-the-art IaaS-based methods ([FaaSGraph, ASPLOS 2024](https://gaosen.org/resources/papers/FaaSGraph.pdf)). The "Non-Expert Tax" study (Wang, Lyu, and Kalavri, BiDEDE 2022) provides complementary evidence that misconfigured or provider-conservative auto-scaling can itself raise cost: auto-scaling policies "scale out aggressively… but are conservative when scaling down," leaving users to pay for underutilized resources, with a tax reaching up to 544% for short-term jobs and up to 332% per month for periodic workloads in Amazon Kinesis Data Analytics and Google Cloud Dataflow ([Wang et al., BiDEDE 2022](https://sites.bu.edu/casp/files/2022/05/Wang22NonExpert.pdf)).

## Workload Prediction

**Problem.** Cloud workloads exhibit what Arbat et al. describe as "random and dynamic burstiness," with "sudden spikes" showing "unprecedented changes in user request patterns over time" across real-world traces from Google Cluster, Facebook Hadoop, Azure Cloud 2017, and Wikipedia ([Arbat et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.06501v1)). This non-stationarity causes concept drift: a model trained on historical patterns may fail when the workload shifts. Manhary et al. report that traditional RL techniques "fail to anticipate workload trends, leading to a 26% increase in SLA violations during traffic spikes," and that DQN methods "require millions of time steps to converge," making them impractical for real-time use ([Manhary et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14962-5)). New services face a cold-start problem: Google Cloud's predictive autoscaler requires 3 days of CPU-based autoscaling history before it can generate predictions and uses up to 3 weeks of load history to feed its model ([Google Cloud predictive autoscaling docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/autoscaler/predictive-autoscaling)). The forecast-to-decision pipeline itself has two stages — a workload predictor that forecasts changes in user requests, and an auto-scaling module that performs scale-out (adding VMs) and scale-in (terminating idle VMs) — and prediction error propagates directly into VM over-provisioning and under-provisioning rates ([Arbat et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.06501v1)).

**Anchor study: Arbat et al. 2022 — WGAN-gp Transformer.** The problem is that cloud workloads are bursty and non-stationary; LSTM baselines have high inference overhead and struggle with long-range dependencies; prediction error causes VM over/under-provisioning. The proposed solution is WGAN-gp Transformer, a time-series forecasting model that replaces LSTM recurrences with a Transformer generator and a multi-layer perceptron critic, trained using a Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty (WGAN-gp) and the MADGRAD optimizer; it predicts job arrival rates (JARs) to feed a predictive auto-scaling module. Evaluated on four real-world traces — Google Cluster Trace, Facebook Hadoop Trace, Azure Cloud 2017 VM Trace, and Wikipedia Trace — WGAN-gp Transformer achieves 5× faster inference time and up to 5.1% lower prediction error against the state-of-the-art LSTM baseline (Jayakumar et al. 2020). When applied to auto-scaling on Google Cloud, the WGAN-gp Transformer-based mechanism "outperforms the LSTM-based mechanism by significantly reducing VM over-provisioning and under-provisioning rates." The authors attribute the improvement to the Transformer's self-attention mechanism, which captures long-range dependencies without the recurrence-induced complexity that slows LSTM inference as input sequences grow ([Arbat et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.06501v1)). The model is publicly available at [github.com/shivaniarbat/wgan-gp-transformer](https://github.com/shivaniarbat/wgan-gp-transformer).

**Complementary mechanism: Manhary et al. 2025 — LSTM-MARL-Ape-X.** Manhary et al. propose LSTM-MARL-Ape-X, a unified framework coupling a bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) with feature-wise attention for workload forecasting with multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) in a distributed Ape-X architecture for resource allocation. The BiLSTM achieves 94.56% prediction accuracy with 2.7 ms inference latency; the MARL component with variance-regularized credit assignment reduces SLA violations by 72% compared to single-agent DQN; and the adaptive prioritized replay converges 3.2× faster than uniform sampling. Validated on Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud traces, the framework achieves 94.6% SLA compliance, a 22% reduction in energy consumption, 6.5% higher SLA compliance than Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT), and linear scalability to over 5,000 nodes with sub-100 ms decision latency ([Manhary et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14962-5)). The authors motivate the LSTM-based design by noting that TFT's O(n²) sequence-length complexity and 3.1× higher GPU memory usage make it expensive for real-time deployment. Google Cloud's predictive autoscaler operationalizes forecasting in production by scaling a managed instance group (MIG) in advance of predicted load, using historical CPU utilization data; it works with real-time data as well as historical data to cover both current and forecasted load, requires 3 days of CPU-based autoscaling history before generating predictions, uses up to 3 weeks of load history, supports only CPU utilization as the scaling metric, and predicts only daily and weekly patterns — it does not predict monthly, annual, or one-time events, or patterns shorter than 10 minutes ([Google Cloud predictive autoscaling docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/autoscaler/predictive-autoscaling)).

**Residual challenges.** Even the verified state of the art leaves significant gaps. First, cold start remains a hard constraint: Google Cloud requires 3 days of history, and Manhary et al. note that DQN methods need millions of time steps to converge. Second, concept drift demands continuous retraining, but the literature does not report a standardized retraining cadence. Third, forecast uncertainty handling is still heuristic: Google Cloud combines predictions with real-time data, and Arbat et al. demonstrate reduced over/under-provisioning but do not report confidence intervals or safety margins. Fourth, multi-resource coupling — predicting CPU, memory, and network demand jointly rather than independently — is not addressed in the verified studies. Finally, the trade-off between model complexity and inference latency persists: Manhary et al. show that TFT's O(n²) complexity is prohibitive, yet their own BiLSTM-MARL framework requires distributed multi-agent training infrastructure to scale to 5,000 nodes.
