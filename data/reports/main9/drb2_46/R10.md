# Auto-Scaling in Cloud Computing: A Technology and Research Report

Cloud auto-scaling is the automatic, policy-driven adjustment of compute, memory, and networking resources that an application consumes, in response to changes in incoming workload. It is the operational realization of the broader cloud property called *elasticity*, and it spans both horizontal scaling (varying the number of VMs, containers, or serverless instances) and vertical scaling (varying the resources allocated to a single instance). This report organizes the field along two axes: a taxonomy of the techniques used to make scaling decisions (reactive vs. proactive), and a survey of the specific challenges that arise when these techniques are deployed in production cloud environments, each paired with a representative published research study.

---

# Auto-Scaling Technology Classification

Most published taxonomies divide auto-scaling techniques into two broad families. *Reactive* methods trigger a scaling action only after a metric, threshold, or analytical condition is observed to have been breached; they are the dominant approach used in commercial cloud platforms (AWS Auto Scaling, Azure Monitor Autoscale, the Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler). *Proactive* methods forecast future demand from historical patterns and trigger scaling *before* the demand materializes, in order to absorb the non-negligible boot/start-up latency of new instances [1][2]. In practice, the cleanest production systems are hybrids: a reactive safety net layered under a proactive predictor, so that forecasting errors do not produce SLA violations.

## Reactive Methods

Reactive methods share a control-loop structure (MAPE-K: Monitor–Analyze–Plan–Execute over a Knowledge base). What distinguishes them is *how* the Analyze step decides that a scaling action is needed: by a static threshold, by a queueing-theoretic performance model, or by a feedback control law.

### Threshold/Rule-Based Methods

The simplest and most widely deployed family. The autoscaler continuously monitors one or more metrics (CPU utilization, memory utilization, request rate, queue length, or a custom application metric) and, when a metric crosses a user-defined upper threshold, adds resources; when it crosses a lower threshold, removes them. A *cooldown* period is required between actions to prevent oscillation. Cloud providers expose this pattern in several variants: AWS's "Simple Scaling" and "Step Scaling", Azure Monitor Autoscale's rule engine, and Kubernetes' Horizontal Pod Autoscaler with its target-average-value policy [2][3].

*Advantages.* Transparency: a rule is auditable in one line. Latency from breach to action: milliseconds. Native integration with every major cloud, which is why this is still the default in production. Cost of operation: near zero [1][2].

*Limitations.* Rules have to be tuned by the operator; mis-tuned thresholds either oscillate (flapping) or react too late. The decision ignores the slope of the metric, so it cannot anticipate an upcoming spike, only respond to one. It is also brittle under non-stationary workloads: a threshold that works for the daytime traffic curve will over- or under-scale at night.

### Queuing-Theory-Based Methods

Queuing theory gives autoscaling an analytical foundation. The system is modeled as a network of queues (typically M/M/c), and the scaler computes the number of servers needed to keep a target service level (e.g., mean response time, queue-length probability) rather than responding to a raw utilization threshold. Quattrocchi et al. (2024) developed QN-CTRL, a queuing-network-based autoscaler that learns a queueing model of the deployed application and then solves an optimal control problem on top of that model. In their extensive evaluation against AWS, GCP, and Azure autoscalers, QN-CTRL and their control-theory counterpart ScaleX outperformed industrial solutions in the SLA-vs.-resource trade-off in most scenarios [4].

*Advantages.* The model gives a principled relationship between resource count and user-visible performance (latency, throughput), so the threshold becomes a derived quantity, not a free parameter. It is also explainable, which matters for SLA negotiation [1][4].

*Limitations.* Standard queuing models assume stationary or near-stationary arrival rates, an assumption cloud workloads routinely violate. They are also better suited to vertical or "right-sizing" decisions than to container sprawl, because the queue abstraction flattens out some of the operational reality of booting new instances.

### Control-Theory-Based Methods

Control theory treats the autoscaler as a feedback controller. The reference value is an SLO (e.g., 95th-percentile latency under 300 ms), the controlled variable is some measurable system output, and the control input is the number of allocated resources. Padala et al. (2009) introduced AutoControl, which combines an online model estimator with a multi-input, multi-output (MIMO) controller that simultaneously allocates CPU and disk resources to multiple co-located applications, and demonstrated detection and mitigation of CPU and disk-I/O bottlenecks under RUBiS and TPC-W benchmarks [5]. The Quattrocchi et al. ScaleX system follows the same control-theoretic line for cloud containers and was shown to compare favorably against the rule-based and target-tracking autoscalers of AWS, Azure, and GCP [4].

*Advantages.* Formal stability and convergence guarantees; the controller can be designed to track a multi-resource SLO and can be analyzed mathematically. Well-suited to systems with inertia (e.g., VM boot times) [1][5].

*Limitations.* The main practical pain is gain selection: an incorrectly tuned controller oscillates or diverges, and the tuning is workload-specific. Pure reactive control still reacts to a deviation rather than to a forecast, so a fast ramp in load can still cause a transient SLA breach before the controller catches up.

## Proactive Methods

Proactive methods add a *prediction* stage to the MAPE-K loop. A forecasting model is trained on historical telemetry; its output is fed either to a fixed threshold policy applied to the predicted value, or directly to a planning stage that decides how many instances to provision for the next interval.

### Time Series Analysis

The classical proactive approach. Autoregressive (AR), moving-average (MA), ARIMA, Seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA), Holt-Winters exponential smoothing, and the Facebook Prophet library are all members of this family. They are well matched to workloads with strong daily or weekly periodicity. Calheiros, Masoumi, Ranjan, and Buyya (2015) realized a workload prediction module based on ARIMA for SaaS applications hosted on public clouds, evaluated it on real request traces, and reported an average prediction accuracy of up to 91%, which translated into efficient resource utilization with minimal QoS impact [6].

*Advantages.* Low training cost; only one or two hyperparameters; the model is interpretable and well understood. Strong performance on stationary or seasonal series [1][6].

*Limitations.* Linearity is the central assumption, so ARIMA-family models under-fit bursts and complex non-linear patterns. They also need regular retraining to track concept drift, and their accuracy collapses for non-stationary cloud traces with mixed diurnal and weekly cycles.

### Machine Learning Methods

Classical supervised learning — Random Forest, Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM), Support Vector Regression, and linear/polynomial regression — predicts future CPU, memory, or request volume from engineered features such as time-of-day, day-of-week, rolling-window statistics, and calendar events. They handle non-linear relationships that time-series models miss and benefit from feature engineering that injects domain knowledge [7].

*Advantages.* Captures non-linear feature interactions; tolerates noisy and high-dimensional inputs; once trained, inference is fast. Easier to incorporate exogenous signals (campaigns, holidays) than ARIMA [1][7].

*Limitations.* Requires significant training data and careful feature engineering; vulnerable to overfitting on small samples. Less transparent than time-series models, and accuracy still degrades under concept drift unless the model is retrained on a rolling window.

### Deep Learning Methods

Deep architectures — Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Units (GRU), Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN), and Transformer-based models — learn temporal representations directly from raw sequences, often capturing long-range dependencies that classical models cannot. They have become the dominant proactive approach in the recent literature, frequently combined with auto-scaling engines like Kubernetes HPA. Hybrid LSTM+MLP and Bi-LSTM designs have been used to forecast multi-resource needs (CPU, memory, bandwidth) in service-function-chain scenarios; LSTM-based predictors have been shown to outperform threshold-based policies under sudden workload changes [1][2].

*Advantages.* State-of-the-art accuracy on complex, multi-variate, non-stationary traces. Can model long-term and short-term seasonality jointly [1][7].

*Limitations.* Higher computational cost for both training and inference; sensitive to hyperparameter choices; less interpretable, which complicates operator trust; the *AI Overhead Paradox*: in some cases, the cost of running the model itself offsets the efficiency gains it enables [1].

### Reinforcement Learning

RL reframes the scaling problem as a sequential decision-making problem. An agent observes the system state (current load, queue depth, resource count), takes an action (scale up, scale down, hold), and receives a reward shaped by QoS, cost, and energy. The agent learns a policy that maximizes cumulative reward. Q-learning, SARSA, Deep Q-Networks (DQN), and policy-gradient methods (DDPG, PPO) have all been applied. Garí, Monge, Pacini, Mateos, and García Garino (2021) provide the canonical survey of RL-based autoscaling, covering model-based and model-free approaches, the use of deep networks to approximate the Q-function, and combinations with fuzzy logic for state generalization [8]. RL has been shown to adapt to new workloads 5.5× faster than transfer-learning baselines and to reduce SLO violations by an order of magnitude in published experiments [1].

*Advantages.* No a priori model of application performance is needed; the policy is data-driven and continuously updated; well suited to multi-objective optimization (cost + SLO + energy) [1][8].

*Limitations.* Long convergence time; poor performance in early training, which is dangerous in production; exploration risk (trying a bad action on a live system); and difficulties in scaling to large state spaces without function approximation, which reintroduces the deep-learning caveats above.

### Hybrid and Ensemble Methods

Most modern research combines two or more of the above. Statistical models (ARIMA, Exponential Smoothing) are stacked with deep learners (LSTM, Random Forest) under a meta-learner to balance linear stability with non-linear accuracy; recent work has reported 40–60% reductions in mean absolute error over single-model baselines [9]. Fuzzy logic is used to soften the discrete threshold decisions of classical reactive autoscalers, producing a "fuzzy controller" that has been shown to outperform threshold rules under multi-metric input [1]. The dominant trend in the literature is therefore not "reactive vs. proactive" but *layered*: a forecasting layer (often deep learning) feeding a thresholded or reinforcement-learned decision layer, with a reactive safety net underneath.

# Main Challenges and Solutions

The methods above operate on toy or benchmark traces far more cleanly than they do on production cloud systems. The literature has converged on a recurring set of challenges where generic threshold or LSTM-based approaches fail, and where domain-specific or hybrid solutions are required. Five of the most prominent are summarized below, each paired with a representative published research study.

## Energy Efficiency

The problem. Cloud data centers account for an estimated 1–1.5% of global electricity use, a fraction that is rising with AI workloads. A long-standing observation is that servers consume roughly 70% of their peak power even when fully idle, and that production servers typically operate at 10–50% of capacity, so over-provisioning drives both energy waste and CO₂ emissions [10]. Energy-aware auto-scaling therefore has two objectives: minimize energy use and meet an SLA, in tension with each other.

The proposed solution. Beloglazov and Buyya (MGC 2010) introduced an *adaptive utilization thresholds* approach to dynamic VM consolidation. Instead of using a fixed upper threshold on host CPU (which is brittle to workload variability), the upper threshold is computed online as a function of the current load's statistical properties (mean, standard deviation), so that the threshold itself tracks the workload. VMs are migrated from over-loaded hosts (driven above their adaptive threshold) and from under-loaded hosts (below the lower threshold), and idle hosts are switched off. The technique was validated against PlanetLab traces from more than a thousand servers, and in their later work (IEEE TPDS 2013) reached approximately 88% of the optimal offline algorithm's performance, while substantially reducing energy and SLA violations compared with fixed-threshold baselines [10][11].

## Dynamic Nature of Telecom Services

The problem. Fifth-generation (5G) mobile core networks, virtualized through Network Function Virtualization (NFV), impose a particularly demanding combination: traffic varies by orders of magnitude between day and night; multiple service classes (eMBB, URLLC, mMTC) co-exist on the same infrastructure; and many Network Functions (e.g., UPF, AMF, SMF) are stateful, so horizontal scaling requires a careful redesign. Boot time for a VNF is non-negligible, and operators typically retain legacy equipment to absorb unpredictable spikes, which complicates the cost-vs.-latency trade-off [12].

The proposed solution. Ren, Phung-Duc, Yu, and Chen (2016, arXiv:1604.05803) developed the Dynamic Auto Scaling Algorithm (DASA) for 5G core networks. The NFV-enabled EPC is modeled as a queueing system in which legacy equipment is a reserved block of servers and VNF instances are powered on or off according to the number of in-flight job requests. DASA is shown analytically and via simulation to significantly reduce operational cost for a given latency upper bound, and the closed-form model provides a quick way to evaluate the cost–performance trade-off without a wide deployment [12]. Subsequent work has layered RL and queueing-network controllers (e.g., ScaleX/QN-CTRL) on top of this VNF setting, with reported SLA improvements over the original DASA scheme [4].

## Big Data Processing

The problem. Big data frameworks (Hadoop MapReduce, Spark, Flink) execute batch and streaming jobs on large YARN-managed clusters. The workload they receive is itself dynamic — different stages of a job need different parallelism, and end-users submit jobs in unpredictable bursts. Naïve static cluster sizing wastes energy; naïve threshold-based scaling reacts too slowly to the front-loaded demand of MapReduce map tasks, while HDFS-aware scaling is constrained by the time-consuming block re-replication of HDFS DataNodes [13].

The proposed solution. Nemouchi, Boudouda, and Zarour (2022, IJOCI) proposed DSHYARN, a dynamic scaling approach for Hadoop YARN that automatically adds or removes nodes based on workload. Two algorithms (scale-up and scale-down) are implemented and integrated with the YARN resource manager. In a sentiment-analysis case study on Twitter data, DSHYARN improved CPU and RAM utilization, reduced job completion time, and cut energy consumption by 16% under average workload compared with the baseline [13]. The production counterpart of this research is now embedded in managed services: Google Cloud's Dataproc autoscaler, for example, reads Hadoop YARN's pending, available, allocated, and reserved resource metrics every cooldown period and rescales the cluster with separate `scaleUpFactor` and `scaleDownFactor` knobs, explicitly designed to absorb MapReduce's two characteristic memory spikes (one at map-task launch, one at reduce-task launch) [14].

## Cost-Effective Graph Processing

The problem. Graph processing (social graphs, web graphs, IoT graphs) is iterative, partition-skewed, and memory-intensive. Frameworks such as Pregel, Giraph, and GraphLab are designed for HPC or commodity clusters and assume persistent, dedicated resources. When such a framework is ported to a pay-as-you-go public cloud, the cost of static provisioning becomes prohibitive: you pay for the entire cluster even when only a few partitions are active. The cloud's heterogeneous VM types open an additional design dimension — should the autoscaler scale the *number* of VMs, the *type* of VM, or both? [15].

The proposed solution. Heidari and Buyya (IEEE TSE 2021) developed a cost-efficient auto-scaling algorithm for large-scale graph processing on heterogeneous cloud VMs. The system adjusts both the number and the type of VMs according to the computation requirements of convergent graph applications, supported by a profiling mechanism and a dynamic repartitioning approach that redistributes graph partitions as the resource set changes. Evaluated against Apache Giraph, the algorithm decreased monetary cost by more than 50% while improving or matching makespan [15]. The more general lesson from this line of work is that for graph workloads, *type-aware* scaling (picking the right VM family for the partition-skew profile) is at least as important as count-based scaling.

## Workload Prediction

The problem. The accuracy of the workload-prediction model is the limiting factor of every proactive autoscaler. A reactive system can recover from a mis-prediction in one interval; a proactive system, having pre-provisioned too many or too few resources, may pay for that error over the entire next interval. Real cloud traffic is non-stationary (concept drift), multi-variate, geographically distributed, and shaped by external events (campaigns, releases, cache invalidations) that do not appear in the resource metrics alone [16].

The proposed solution. Le Duc, Nguyen, and Östberg (Electronics 2025) introduced a CDN-tailored prediction and autoscaling framework that combines four models — Seasonal ARIMA, LSTM, Bi-LSTM, and Online Sequential Extreme Learning Machine (OS-ELM) — inside an adaptive feedback loop that switches between them based on the current workload regime. The predictions drive a queue-based resource estimator, enabling proactive cache-server scaling. The framework was evaluated on 18 months of real production CDN traffic, capturing realistic seasonality, cache–tier interactions, and propagation delays, and was shown to maintain high accuracy while reducing computational overhead through model switching [16]. A complementary strand of work (Badjajian & Hazzouri, 2025) shows that RNN-LSTM predictors reduce MAE and RMSE by roughly 40% over ARIMA baselines on OpenStack and AWS private-cloud traces, and integrate cleanly with OpenStack autoscaling through Terraform-driven deployment, confirming the practical value of the deep-learning branch in production environments [17].
