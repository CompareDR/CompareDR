# Universal Basic Income in Practice: Effectiveness and Long-Term Consequences in Finland, Canada, and the United States

## The comparative verdict

The post-2015 “UBI pilot wave” rarely tested UBI. Across Finland, Canada, and the United States, the experiments were partial basic incomes, income-tested guaranteed incomes, or targeted unconditional cash transfers—not the canonical model of a universal, unconditional, individual, regular, and adequate cash payment. The practical consequence is that the post-2015 evidence base is approximation evidence: it can speak to the effects of cash transfers on poor and near-poor populations, but it cannot directly answer whether a full, national, adequate UBI would be affordable, sustainable, or behaviourally neutral.

Within that boundary, the record is consistent on some points and divided on others. Unconditional cash reliably improved income stability, perceived economic security, and mental health in the short run. Employment effects were small and context-dependent: Finland’s national randomised experiment found no aggregate employment gain; the largest U.S. randomised controlled trial (RCT) found a moderate labour-supply reduction; Stockton’s small pilot found a pre-pandemic full-time employment gain. Long-run consequences—fiscal affordability, macroeconomic effects, intergenerational change—were not observed in any pilot; they come from microsimulation and macroeconomic modelling, whose conclusions are assumption-sensitive. The strongest policy signal is negative or conditional: no focus country adopted a national UBI, and Canada’s most rigorous fiscal analysis rejected both a full UBI and the pilot as an inadequate instrument for studying it.

## What the pilots actually tested

A basic income is conventionally defined as “a periodic cash payment unconditionally delivered to all on an individual basis, without means-test or work requirement” ([BIEN](https://basicincome.org/)). The Stanford Basic Income Lab adds the “regular” pillar and spells out five defining features—universal, unconditional, cash, individual, periodic—while distinguishing Guaranteed Income (GI) as “an unconditional, periodic, cash payment that is targeted to specific residents within a community, typically those below some income threshold” ([Stanford Basic Income Lab](https://basicincome.stanford.edu/about/what-is-basic-income/)). The Stanford umbrella review compiles 16 reviews and concludes that “a fully universal and unconditional basic income has never been implemented at scale,” so all pilot evidence is necessarily approximation evidence drawn from programmes that share some but not all UBI features ([Hasdell 2020](https://basicincome.stanford.edu/uploads/Umbrella%20Review%20BI_final.pdf)). The canonical model also implies adequacy—a payment sufficient to cover essential living costs—though advocates disagree on whether the payment should be a full income floor or a partial base on top of other income ([Hasdell 2020](https://basicincome.stanford.edu/uploads/Umbrella%20Review%20BI_final.pdf)).

Read across the table, the pilots deviated from canonical UBI on at least one pillar. Finland’s pilot was a *partial* basic income: the €560 payment was deducted from existing basic social benefits, so it did not raise the disposable income of the unemployed control-equivalent population and could not test a full income floor ([Intereconomics](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). Ontario’s pilot was income-tested and reduced by 50 cents for every dollar earned, placing it firmly in the guaranteed-income category ([Ontario brochure](https://files.ontario.ca/170508_bi_brochure_eng_pg_by_pg_proof.pdf)). The U.S. pilots were targeted by design: SEED required residence in neighbourhoods at or below the city’s median household income, and Baby’s First Years targeted low-income mothers ([SEED](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6039d612b17d055cac14070f/t/6050294a1212aa40fdaf773a/1615866187890/SEED_Preliminary+Analysis-SEEDs+First+Year_Final+Report_Individual+Pages+.pdf); [Baby’s First Years](https://www.babysfirstyears.com/)). Alaska’s Permanent Fund Dividend is the one near-universal case, but its annual lump sum is far below an adequate income, making it a quasi-universal contrast rather than a UBI ([APFC](https://apfc.org/)). Mincome, included here as a clearly labelled historical anchor, was a negative income tax (NIT)—a benefit that phases down as earned income rises—not a flat universal payment ([Canadian Museum for Human Rights](https://humanrights.ca/story/manitobas-mincome-experiment)).

The same pattern holds outside the three focus countries. An EPPI-Centre rapid evidence review identified 38 basic-income experiments across Europe, North America, and Asia since 2015, most targeting small numbers of low-income households for roughly two years ([EPPI-Centre](https://eppi.ioe.ac.uk/cms/Portals/0/IPPO%20IPR%20Basic%20Income%20Experiments%20main%20report_LO0100322.pdf)). Scotland’s Citizens’ Basic Income feasibility study (2018–2020) stopped at feasibility over delivery and cost constraints; the Netherlands relaxed conditionalities on social assistance rather than testing universality; Barcelona’s B-MINCOME was municipal and targeted; Germany’s Pilotprojekt Grundeinkommen was crowdfunded and small; South Korea’s youth transfers are age-targeted; and Ireland’s Basic Income for the Arts was sector-specific. Sweden and Denmark remained at debate stage ([EPPI-Centre](https://eppi.ioe.ac.uk/cms/Portals/0/IPPO%20IPR%20Basic%20Income%20Experiments%20main%20report_LO0100322.pdf)). This reinforces the distinctiveness of Finland’s national individual-level RCT and of the U.S. combination of municipal pilots with two large RCTs.

## Finland: the only national randomised basic-income experiment

Finland’s 2017–2018 basic income experiment was the first nationwide randomised field experiment of a partial basic income in a developed country, but it was deliberately narrow and time-limited: 2,000 unemployed people aged 25–58 received €560 per month tax-free and unconditionally for two years, the payment was largely offset by deducting existing minimum benefits, and the model was neither extended nor adopted.

**Design and implementation.** The experiment was created by the Experiment Act (1528/2016), accepted on 29 December 2016, and ran from January 2017 to December 2018 ([Experiment Act 1528/2016](https://www.finlex.fi/fi/laki/alkup/2016/20161528); [pre-analysis plan](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/versions/140147/docs/version/document)). The target group was people aged 25–58 who were receiving basic daily allowance or labour market support in November 2016 ([Intereconomics](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). A simple random sample of 2,000 formed the treatment group; participation was mandatory to avoid selection bias, and the remainder of the target group—roughly 173,000–175,000 people—formed the control group ([ESPN Flash Report 2019/17](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)). The treatment group received €560 per month with no work requirement, no needs assessment, and no conditions ([ESPN Flash Report](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)).

Benefit interaction was the central design feature. The €560 was deducted from basic social benefits, so for someone who remained out of work the net benefit level was about the same as under the basic unemployment allowance; participants could still receive housing allowance and social assistance ([ESPN Flash Report](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en); [pre-analysis plan](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/versions/140147/docs/version/document)). The Ministry excluded child supplements from the basic income, so participants with children under 18 lost €90–170 per month depending on the number of children ([pre-analysis plan](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/versions/140147/docs/version/document)). Microsimulation showed disposable income rising once earnings exceeded €300 per month, with a peak relative gain of about 35% at roughly €2,000 of earnings; the European Commission commentary called the model “too generous and unrealistic” because taxation was not included ([ESPN Flash Report](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)).

**Employment effects.** The headline result is a null: replacing minimum unemployment benefits with an unconditional €560/month payment did not produce a statistically significant aggregate increase in employment. Three figures circulate and measure different quantities. **49.6 days is a level, not a difference**: the treatment group was employed for 49.6 days on average in calendar year 2017, versus 49.3 days for the control group; the raw difference of 0.3 days was statistically insignificant ([EC ESPN Flash Report](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)). **“About half a day higher” is the first-year treatment-minus-control difference** (49.6 − 49.3 = 0.3 days). **“6 days” is the treatment-minus-control difference over the reference period November 2017–October 2018**, during which the treatment group averaged 78 days of employment ([Finnish Government, 6 May 2020](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)).

The incentive change was large: participation tax rates fell by 23 percentage points for an unemployed person accepting a low-paying full-time job, and the effective marginal tax rate at a €2,000 monthly wage fell from 66% to 43% ([Hämäläinen & Verho 2022](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp9875.pdf)). Yet first-year employment was statistically unchanged. That undercuts a simple benefit-trap hypothesis: for the average unemployed participant, removing conditionality and lowering implicit tax rates did not move people into work. Equally, the absence of a significant decline undermines the income-effect hypothesis that an unconditional payment would cause recipients to work less ([VATT summary](https://vatt.fi/en/-/the-finnish-basic-income-experiment-failed-to-produce-any-sizable-short-term-employment-effects)).

The second-year estimate cannot be read as a clean basic-income effect. Finland introduced an “activation model” at the beginning of 2018 that tightened entitlement criteria for unemployment benefits, and it applied asymmetrically across the two groups. The official release states plainly that “the effects of the second year of the experiment cannot be separated from the effects of the activation model” ([Finnish Government](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)). The 6.6-day second-year estimate should therefore be treated as an upper bound on the basic-income effect, contaminated by a simultaneous policy change ([Salokangas 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml)). The only robust subgroup pattern is that couples with children improved; the official release warns that “the results for different groups are still uncertain due to the small number of observations and the numerous tests” ([Finnish Government](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)).

**Wellbeing, health, and administrative effects.** The clearest and most consistent effects were non-employment outcomes. Basic-income recipients reported higher subjective wellbeing, less mental strain, greater trust in others, greater perceived economic security, and greater agency than the control group ([Hiilamo 2020](https://www.helsinki.fi/en/news/society-and-learning/basic-income-experiment-finland-yields-surprising-results); [Salokangas 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml)). The most precisely estimated of these is mental strain: Hiilamo et al. (2025) analyse the same survey (MHI-5 instrument, dichotomised; response rate 20%; treated n=569, control n=1,028) and find that the treated group had 39% lower odds of mental strain (adjusted odds ratio 0.61, 95% confidence interval 0.45–0.83), with an adjusted risk difference for poor mental health of −0.08 (95% CI −0.12 to −0.03) ([Hiilamo et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12555240/); [MPIDR working paper](https://www.demogr.mpf.de/papers/working/wp-2025-035.pdf)). Secondary summaries of the official report report life satisfaction of 7.32 versus 6.76 and trust of 6.8 versus 6.3, but these point estimates could not be re-verified against the primary PDF in this review ([official report record](https://julkaisut.valtioneuvosto.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/161361/Report_TheBasicIncomeExperiment20172018inFinland.pdf)).

A register-based study extends the survey findings. Hämäläinen et al. (2025) find that basic income reduced psychotropic/psychiatric medication purchases by 8–11% and reduced primary-care visits for mental health, mainly through reduced antidepressant use (a 5–12% reduction in quarterly defined daily doses), with no significant difference in the probability of any psychotropic use ([Hämäläinen et al. 2025, *Journal of Public Economics*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272725001781); summarised in [Salokangas 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml)). Critically, these register-based effects largely faded after the experiment ended. The trial also removed the obligation to report income to the unemployment office and to participate in active labour-market measures; 58.9% of treatment recipients versus 67.8% of controls reported experiences with bureaucracy, but no administrative cost savings were quantified ([EC/ESPN 2019](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en); [Hiilamo 2020](https://www.helsinki.fi/en/news/society-and-learning/basic-income-experiment-finland-yields-surprising-results)).

**Limitations.** The sample was restricted to unemployed persons aged 25–58, so the pilot cannot test whether an unconditional payment would reduce work among already-employed people—the classic income-effect concern. The two-year horizon cannot capture long-run labour-supply adjustments. The partial benefit substitution means the experiment tested an in-work incentive improvement more than a pure income guarantee. The activation-model reform breaks clean identification of the second year. The survey response rate was 23% (31% treatment, 20% control), no baseline survey was fielded, and the government itself cautioned that “it is not possible to say with certainty that the better wellbeing of the test group was due specifically to receipt of a basic income” ([Finnish Government](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)). The pilot contained no fiscal-cost analysis; parallel microsimulation of partial basic income at €450–€750 per month found that budget-neutral flat tax rates of 40–50.5% would be required ([Intereconomics](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). A later consolidated evaluation concludes that the modest increase in economic autonomy “did not translate into major life changes” and that broad UBI schemes may provide shallow support while displacing targeted services ([Salokangas 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml)).

## Canada: a cancelled pilot, a contested long-run experiment, and a fiscal verdict

### Ontario: cancellation left only partial and qualitative evidence

The Ontario Basic Income Pilot (OBIP) was an income-tested guaranteed income—not a universal basic income—that operated for barely a year of its planned three-year term before the Ford government cancelled it in July 2018. OBIP provided payments of up to **C$16,989 per year for a single person** and **C$24,027 per year for a couple**, reduced by **50% of any earned income**, with an additional **C$6,000 per year for a person with a disability** ([Ontario Newsroom, April 2017](https://news.ontario.ca/opo/en/2017/04/giving-more-people-an-opportunity-to-get-ahead-and-stay-ahead.html); [Mendelson 2019, Maytree](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Lessons-from-Ontarios-Basic-Income-Pilot.pdf)). Benefits phased out entirely once earned income reached C$33,978 for individuals and C$48,054 for couples ([Mendelson 2019](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Lessons-from-Ontarios-Basic-Income-Pilot.pdf)). Approximately 6,000 Ontarians enrolled across Hamilton, Brantford/Brant County, Thunder Bay, and Lindsay, of whom **4,000 agreed to receive monthly payments** in exchange for participating in surveys and research ([Cavalluzzo LLP](https://www.cavalluzzo.com/basicincomeclassaction)). The Ford government announced cancellation on 31 July 2018; final payments were made on 25 March 2019 ([Cavalluzzo LLP](https://www.cavalluzzo.com/basicincomeclassaction)).

Because the government ceased evaluation activities, the most systematic evidence comes from an independent academic-community research partnership. *Southern Ontario’s Basic Income Experience*, authored by Ferdosi, McDowell, Lewchuk, and Ross, surveyed 217 former recipients using a 70-question anonymous online survey and 40 in-depth qualitative interviews ([McMaster University](https://labourstudies.mcmaster.ca/app/uploads/2024/06/southern-ontarios-basic-income-experience.pdf); [CBC News, March 2020](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/hamilton/basic-income-mcmaster-report-1.5485729)). Key self-reported findings included: nearly 80% reported their health improved during the pilot; over 80% reported a positive effect on mental well-being; 83% described feeling stressed or anxious less often; 81% felt more self-confident; more than half reported using less tobacco and 48% drinking less alcohol; nearly three-quarters of respondents who were working when the pilot began continued working; many reported moving to higher-paying, more secure jobs; respondents reported improved diet, better housing security, and 66% reported better relationships with family members. “Almost all survey respondents indicated that the pilot’s cancellation forced them to place on hold or abandon certain life plans” ([CBC News, March 2020](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/hamilton/basic-income-mcmaster-report-1.5485729)). These findings are self-reported and lack a control group, so they cannot support causal claims about OBIP’s effects relative to counterfactual conditions. The report’s authors acknowledged that “we just don’t have the data to understand what happened in the long run. This is the tragedy of the pilot not running for three years” ([CBC News, March 2020](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/hamilton/basic-income-mcmaster-report-1.5485729)).

Michael Mendelson’s October 2019 Maytree report identifies three principal design shortcomings: lack of a “saturation” site (unlike Mincome’s Dauphin site), problems of enrollment, and use of the income tax system to test recipients’ income, which created delays and complexity ([Maytree](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Lessons-from-Ontarios-Basic-Income-Pilot.pdf)). He recommends either an ambitious tax-based basic income with automatic community-level enrollment on an opt-out basis, or a modest experiment making existing social assistance unconditional in a test community, with independent external administration and guaranteed funding from start to finish. A class action against the Ontario government was certified on 4 March 2024 (*Bowman v. Ontario*, 2024 ONSC 1327); the Statement of Claim alleges breach of contract and seeks $200 million in general damages ([Cavalluzzo LLP](https://www.cavalluzzo.com/basicincomeclassaction)). The cancellation’s effect on Canadian UBI momentum is difficult to quantify precisely, but it converted a prospective policy experiment into a cautionary tale and a litigation legacy.

### Mincome: the closest long-horizon evidence, and its limits

Mincome (the Manitoba Basic Annual Income Experiment, 1974–1979) is a historical guaranteed-annual-income field experiment with a negative-income-tax structure—a benefit that phases down as earned income rises rather than a flat payment. The guarantee was set relative to Statistics Canada’s low-income cut-off. In Winnipeg, randomly selected low-income households received one of three payment levels for a four-person household: C$3,800, C$4,800, or C$5,800 per year. In Dauphin, the “saturation site,” every low-income household received C$4,800 per year, and payments were reduced by 50 cents for every dollar of employment income. Payments were monthly; participants were not required to work or justify spending, but they did complete an intake interview, quarterly surveys, and monthly income reports. The overall experiment ran from 1974 to 1979; the Dauphin saturation payments lasted three years (1975–1977) ([Canadian Museum for Human Rights](https://humanrights.ca/story/manitobas-mincome-experiment); [Calnitsky](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~dcalnits/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/Calnitsky_More_Normal_than_Welfare_CRS.pdf)).

Evelyn Forget’s post-2015 reanalysis—matching a rediscovered participant roster against Manitoba health-administration and education records—reported an **8.5% reduction in hospital separations** relative to controls, concentrated in accident/injury and mental-health codes; increased high-school continuation; and a relative decline in fertility among young women ([Forget 2011, *Canadian Public Policy* 37(3):283–305](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23764242/)). By 1985, seven years after payments stopped, differences between Dauphin and comparison communities had faded on the measures examined ([Progress.org summary](https://www.progress.org/wiki/forget-mincome-town-with-no-poverty/)).

Those findings are contested. David A. Green’s 2022 reanalysis argues that the data “contain statistically significant negative pre-trends in the relative hospital usage” between Dauphin and its comparators before the experiment, and that the treatment effect itself “is not statistically significant at any standard significance level” once those trends are addressed; he also raised the possibility that a new hospital in Dauphin generated supply-induced demand ([Green 2022, *Canadian Public Policy* 48(4):539–548](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cpp/issued/v48y2022i4p539-548.html)). Forget replied that Green’s critique “depends heavily on data from 1974 and 1975 which were biased by the pre-announcement of MINCOME” and maintained that “MINCOME was associated with a significant decline in hospitalization relative to pre-existing trends” ([Forget 2022, *Canadian Public Policy* 48(4):549–556](https://www.jstor.org/stable/27343238)). The exchange appeared in the same issue and remains unresolved. The Campbell systematic review includes Mincome as the experiment with both an RCT site and a saturation site and rates the wider guaranteed-income evidence base as carrying a high risk of bias ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)).

### British Columbia: the most comprehensive fiscal rejection

The BC Expert Panel on Basic Income—chaired by David A. Green with Jonathan Rhys Kesselman and Lindsay Tedds—concluded in its final report, *Covering All the Basics: Reforms for a More Just Society* (released January 2021; dated December 2020), that a universal basic income was neither the most effective nor the most affordable route to poverty reduction in British Columbia. Modelling a flat C$20,000 annual payment to all residents aged 18–64, the panel put the cost at roughly **C$51 billion per year**, close to the province’s approximately C$60 billion annual budget, and instead recommended a package of **65 targeted reforms** estimated at **C$3.3–C$5 billion annually** ([Kesselman, Finances of the Nation summary](https://financesofthenation.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/2021-BC-Expert-Panel-Basic-Income-FON-rvd2.pdf); [CBC News, 28 Jan 2021](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/guaranteed-basic-income-not-best-for-bc-1.5891561)). The panel’s cost-effectiveness result is the core finding: a UBI at the poverty-line guarantee lifted only about 8,000 people out of poverty per billion dollars spent, whereas an income-tested refundable tax credit (RTC) with the same maximum benefit cut the poverty rate by 68% and lifted over 44,000 people per billion dollars ([progress.org summary of the BC panel report](https://www.progress.org/wiki/bc-basic-income-panel-final-report/)). The panel concluded that “UBIs are orders of magnitude more expensive than income-tested basic incomes that provide similar poverty reduction” ([progress.org](https://www.progress.org/wiki/bc-basic-income-panel-final-report/)).

On financing, the panel found that the standard Canadian proposal—funding an RTC by eliminating the basic personal amount and other tax credits—raises too little revenue in British Columbia’s low-bottom-bracket tax structure and shifts the burden onto middle earners while sparing top incomes; a revenue-neutral C$10,000 RTC with a low benefit-reduction rate would require roughly a 50% increase in BC income-tax rates ([progress.org](https://www.progress.org/wiki/bc-basic-income-panel-final-report/)). The panel therefore concluded that once financing is priced in, the low-disincentive case for a UBI over an income-tested alternative “largely disappears.” Its 65 recommendations emphasise targeted basic incomes for three groups—youth aging out of government care, persons with disabilities, and women fleeing violence—plus expansion of in-kind benefits and reforms to existing income assistance ([CBC News](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/guaranteed-basic-income-not-best-for-bc-1.5891561); [Financial Post, 4 Feb 2021](https://financialpost.com/opinion/william-watson-basic-income-report-one-size-fits-no-one/)). The BC government accepted the recommendation not to pursue a universal basic income or a basic-income pilot ([BC Gov News, 2021](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2021SDPR0005-000152)).

Critically for the question of pilot design, the panel determined that a basic income pilot would not be useful, “chiefly because pilots are, by their nature, temporary and cannot provide evidence on long-term impacts,” and because a pilot cannot test the major taxation changes needed to finance a real-world UBI ([Wikipedia, “British Columbia Basic Income Expert Panel Report”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Basic_Income_Expert_Panel_Report)). It also formalised a “policy stability” criterion requiring that policies have sufficiently broad support that they will not simply be undone in the next political cycle—a definition that reads as a direct response to the Ontario experience ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Basic_Income_Expert_Panel_Report)). Green’s commissioned research paper details the administrative obstacles to tax-delivered universality: in 2015, 3.5% of the population over age 19 was not in the Canada Revenue Agency system at all and 7.4% were linked non-filers, rising to 19.2% in the bottom earnings decile; a tax-delivered benefit would miss precisely the lowest-income people unless supplemented by outreach ([Green 2021](https://econ.cms.arts.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2021/03/Claims-Made-For-or-About-a-Basic-Income_DGreen1.pdf)).

### Canadian synthesis

Canada’s evidence base combines three structurally different streams: a long-run saturation experiment (Mincome), a cancelled provincial quasi-experiment (Ontario), and a fiscal microsimulation (BC panel). Each compensates for the others’ limitations, but none alone supports a national UBI inference. The Campbell systematic review provides the most authoritative quality assessment: across 27 studies on 10 experiments, it found “some concerns” for at least one risk-of-bias domain in **all 27 studies** and “high risk” for at least one domain in **25 of 27**; attrition was the most common high-risk domain (21 studies) and analysis/reporting bias the next (22 studies). Only two studies reported the a priori food-insecurity outcome, both showing improvement (SMD −0.57, 95% CI −0.65 to −0.49; SMD −0.41, 95% CI −0.57 to −0.26), and the second a priori outcome—poverty level—was **not reported in any included study** ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). The Canadian experience supports the argument that pilots are more useful when embedded in longer policy commitments: Ontario’s cancellation converted a prospective evaluation into litigation, participant harm, and a legacy of broken trust, while Mincome’s long-run evidence survived because the experiment concluded before the political context shifted.

## United States: two large RCTs, a child experiment, and quasi-universal contrasts

### Stockton SEED: a small pilot with an influential pre-pandemic result

Stockton Economic Empowerment Demonstration (SEED) launched in February 2019 as the first mayor-led guaranteed-income initiative in the United States. It paid $500 per month for 24 months (originally 18 months, extended by six months after additional funding arrived during the pandemic) to residents of census tracts at or below Stockton’s median household income of $46,033; there was no individual income test and no work requirement ([SEED first-year report](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6039d612b17d055cac14070f/t/6050294a1212aa40fdaf773a/1615866187890/SEED_Preliminary+Analysis-SEEDs+First+Year_Final+Report_Individual+Pages+.pdf); [West et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160253/)). The first-year report describes 125 recipients, while the final peer-reviewed study reports 131 treatment participants and a control group of 200, with power set at 0.80, alpha 0.05, and a minimum detectable effect of *f* = 0.30. Recruitment used a stratified random sample of households in 42 eligible census tracts; 4,200 households received mailers, and consented participants were assigned by simple randomisation. Payments were disbursed monthly via a prepaid “Focus Card,” chosen because roughly 9.7% of Stocktonians lacked a bank account from 2013–2017. The $3 million budget was entirely philanthropic, including a $1 million grant from the Economic Security Project, and the evaluation was funded by the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation’s Evidence for Action program ([SEED first-year report](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6039d612b17d055cac14070f/t/6050294a1212aa40fdaf773a/1615866187890/SEED_Preliminary+Analysis-SEEDs+First+Year_Final+Report_Individual+Pages+.pdf); [West et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160253/)).

SEED’s central, and most-cited, result is a **pre-pandemic employment gain**: full-time employment among recipients rose from 28% to 40% between February 2019 and February 2020, a +12 percentage-point increase, against a +5-point rise (32% to 37%) in the randomised control group ([SEED first-year report](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6039d612b17d055cac14070f/t/6050294a1212aa40fdaf773a/1615866187890/SEED_Preliminary+Analysis-SEEDs+First+Year_Final+Report_Individual+Pages+.pdf); [Results4America](https://catalog.results4america.org/case-studies/guaranteed-income-stockton)). The demonstration also reduced month-to-month income volatility: the coefficient of variation was 19% in the treatment group versus 26% in the control group in year one, statistically significant, though the difference narrowed during the pandemic year ([Evidence for Action fact sheet](https://evidenceforaction.org/sites/default/files/2023-04/SEED-Fact-Sheet.pdf)). The share able to pay an unexpected expense with cash rose from 25% to 52% (+27 percentage points), against +3 points in the control group ([Results4America](https://catalog.results4america.org/case-studies/guaranteed-income-stockton)). Recipients overwhelmingly spent the cash on basics: food accounted for nearly 37% of spending, sales/merchandise 22%, utilities 11%, and auto costs 10%; less than 1% went to alcohol or tobacco ([SEED press release](https://www.stocktondemonstration.org/press-landing/guaranteed-income-increases-employment-improves-financial-and-physical-health)). Recipients reported lower mental distress, better energy and physical functioning, and greater agency than controls, though the fact sheet notes that these gains dissipated during the pandemic or after payments ended ([Evidence for Action fact sheet](https://evidenceforaction.org/sites/default/files/2023-04/SEED-Fact-Sheet.pdf); [West et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160253/)).

The +12 percentage-point versus +5-point contrast is a **pre-pandemic** (February 2019–February 2020) first-year comparison, not a full two-year result. Because the headline employment gain predates COVID-19, it cannot be attributed to pandemic-era labour-market recovery, whereas the weaker year-two employment trend *is* confounded by the pandemic’s disruption of employment, childcare, and public aid. West et al. (2023) state explicitly that the pandemic’s onset created a history effect that threatened both internal and external validity, prompting them to analyse the data at three separate time points ([West et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160253/)). SEED’s evidence is bounded by its design: the treatment arm was small (131 including alternates) against 200 controls, and the power analysis detected only moderate effects (*f* = 0.30). Targeting was categorical by neighbourhood income, not universal, and participants self-selected by responding to mailers; the programme excluded unhoused residents because participation required a permanent address ([West et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160253/); [Results4America](https://catalog.results4america.org/case-studies/guaranteed-income-stockton)). The 24-month duration and $500 monthly payment—below a full poverty-level income—mean SEED tested a partial, time-limited guaranteed income rather than an adequate, permanent UBI. Its most durable effect may be political: it led to the founding of Mayors for a Guaranteed Income in June 2020 and helped normalise unconditional cash in U.S. municipal and state policy ([SEED press release](https://www.stocktondemonstration.org/press-landing/guaranteed-income-increases-employment-improves-financial-and-physical-health); [California CDSS](https://www.cdss.ca.gov/inforesources/guaranteed-income-pilot-program)).

### OpenResearch: a moderate labour-supply reduction with no job-quality payoff

The OpenResearch Unconditional Cash Study (ORUS) is the largest unconditional cash RCT conducted in the United States to date by amount disbursed. It gave 1,000 low-income participants in Illinois and Texas $1,000 per month unconditionally for three years (2020–2023), against a control group of 2,000 participants receiving $50 per month. Participants were aged 21–40 with 2019 household income not exceeding 300% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL); average household income was about $29,900, so the transfer represented roughly a 40% increase in household income ([Vivalt et al. 2024, NBER WP 32719](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32719/w32719.pdf)). The trial was registered on the AEA RCT Registry on 17 November 2020 (AEARCTR-0006750) and ran for three years ([AEA RCT Registry](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/6750/); [CBS News, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/sam-altman-universal-basic-income-study-open-research/)).

The headline result is a **moderate labour-supply reduction**. Recipients worked about 1.3 fewer hours per week and were roughly 2 percentage points less likely to be employed—equivalent to roughly eight fewer employment days per year ([OpenResearch](https://www.openresearchlab.org/projects/unconditional-cash-study)). The revised NBER abstract strengthens this to a 4.1 percentage-point decrease in labour-market participation, and reports that partners of participants also reduced hours by a comparable amount; the earlier July 2024 version reported a 2.0 percentage-point decrease and a smaller income effect, so the participation figure is version-sensitive ([Vivalt et al. 2024, NBER WP 32719](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32719/w32719.pdf)). Earned income excluding transfers fell by about $1,500–$1,800 per year, and household income excluding the transfer fell by $2,500–$4,100 (about 5% of the control-group average) ([Vivalt et al. 2024](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32719/w32719.pdf); [UChicago ORUS summary, Sept 2024](https://urbanlabs.uchicago.edu/attachments/b509fc06212b1970b619eed0e4d1d6002988bf5e/store/1950a74168ef0a877a2234070602021bdf3316c37c77886b6d752481476f/ORUS+September+2024.pdf)).

The most important interpretive nuance is that the freed time did not go to productive investment. Recipients were 10% more likely to be job-searching and 9% more likely to have applied for a job, but applied to fewer jobs; the largest increase in time use was leisure, with smaller increases in transport and finance-related activities. The study found **no impact on job quality**, with confidence intervals tight enough to rule out even small improvements, and no significant human-capital or degree-attainment effects, although younger participants may have pursued more formal education ([Vivalt et al. 2024](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32719/w32719.pdf); [digitalgovernmenthub summary](https://digitalgovernmenthub.org/library/the-employment-effects-of-a-guaranteed-income-experimental-evidence-from-two-u-s-states/)). This is the finding that most directly qualifies the “longer job search yields better matches” hypothesis: recipients were more selective about jobs, but that selectivity did not translate into measurably better employment. On consumption, spending rose by at least $310/month, led by food, rent, and transport; savings balances increased by $1,000–$2,300, but borrowing also rose and net worth may be slightly lower. Reported financial health improved, but subjective-wellbeing gains observed in the first year had faded by the end of the study ([UChicago ORUS summary](https://urbanlabs.uchicago.edu/attachments/b509fc06212b1970b619eed0e4d1d6002988bf5e/store/1950a74168ef0a877a2234070602021bdf3316c37c77886b6d752481476f/ORUS+September+2024.pdf); [Vivalt et al. 2024](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32719/w32719.pdf)). Health effects were mixed: recipients were 10% more likely to receive any dental care, medical spending rose by $20/month, problematic drinking fell by 20%, and 14% more pursued education or job training; however, hospitalisations rose by 26% and emergency-department visits by 10% relative to the control group ([OpenResearch](https://www.openresearchlab.org/projects/unconditional-cash-study); [CBS News, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/sam-altman-universal-basic-income-study-open-research/)).

The $50/month control arm matters for interpretation: the estimated treatment-versus-control differences are *net of* any effect of the $50 payment, so the measured labour-supply reduction is the marginal effect of $1,000/month relative to $50/month, not relative to nothing. Readers should treat the employment effect as a dose-response estimate between two positive transfer levels. ORUS is targeted, not universal: it samples low-income working-age people in two states over three years, and cannot speak to national labour-supply, inflation, fiscal cost, or universal-program effects. The automation and AI narrative that motivated the study’s tech-sector origins is an advocacy rationale, not an empirical finding of the trial ([Wired, 2018](https://www.wired.com/story/y-combinator-learns-basic-income-is-not-so-basic-after-all/); [CBS News, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/sam-altman-universal-basic-income-study-open-research/)).

### Baby’s First Years: child-focused cash transfers

Baby’s First Years (BFY) is the first large-scale U.S. RCT of unconditional cash transfers to families with young children. It recruited 1,000 mother–infant pairs with household incomes below the federal poverty threshold in four metropolitan areas—New York City, the greater New Orleans area, the greater Omaha area, and the Twin Cities—in the postpartum wards of 12 hospitals. Within each metro area, participants were randomised on a 40/60 basis: 40% to a “high cash” gift of $333/month ($4,000/year) and 60% to a “low cash” comparator of $20/month, both delivered on the same branded debit card. The cash difference is $3,760/year, roughly an 18% increase in financial resources given families’ low income at birth ([Baby’s First Years trial registration](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/3262); [JPAL, Magnuson & Gennetian, Nov 2025](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)).

The “76 months” figure is the **payment duration**, not the follow-up horizon. The cash gift was originally planned for 40 months, extended to 52 months because of COVID-19 disruptions, and then extended to 76 months (participants were informed in August 2022); payments ended in 2025. The research follow-up extends to age 8 (96 months), with assessment waves at ages 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 8, and EEG-based brain-activity measures at ages 1, 4, 6, and 8 ([Baby’s First Years trial registration](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/3262)).

The study’s first published outcome was a positive one: at age 1, infants whose mothers received $333/month showed more high-frequency brain activity than those in the $20/month group ([Noble et al. 2022, *PNAS*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2115649119)). That early signal did not persist into broader developmental gains. By age 4, the study found **no significant differences between treatment and control groups in children’s development or health outcomes** on most measures ([Krause et al. 2025, NBER WP 34040](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34040/revisions/w34040.rev0.pdf)), and the age-4 brain-activity follow-up reported no significant impacts on preschoolers’ beta or gamma power ([Troller-Renfree et al. 2026, *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12861262/)). The age-4 nulls are the study’s headline result and a key contrast with the age-1 EEG finding ([Noble et al. 2025, NBER WP 33844](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33844)).

On maternal employment, Sauval et al. estimate a **small negative effect**: the proportion of mothers working for pay was about 1 percentage point lower in the high-cash group (≈2.8–3% relative) ([Sauval et al., *Journal of Public Economics* 236:105159](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047272724000951)). On family processes, Magnuson et al. (2025) report a mixed pattern: the transfer **reduced poverty and increased household income**, and families **spent more on children**—about $68/month more on child-specific items such as books and toys—without spending more on rent or groceries. High-cash mothers also reported more time in stimulating activities (reading, playing). But the study found **no increase in the observed quality of mother–child interactions**, and **no group differences** in self-reported economic hardship or worry, psychological distress, overall well-being, or the quality of romantic and co-parenting relationships ([Magnuson et al. 2025, *Nature Communications*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-62438-x); [JPAL, 2025](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)). In other words, the cash changed what families bought and how much time they reported spending with children, but did not measurably change the *quality* of family life on the survey instruments used.

BFY is a categorical, income-tested programme for low-income mothers of infants, not a universal, individual, or adequate UBI. Its $333/month payment is modest—the study team itself contrasts it with the $1,000/month OpenResearch transfer—and it does not test fiscal affordability, national universality, long-run labour supply, or macroeconomic effects ([JPAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)). The $20/month control arm is not zero, so estimated effects are relative to a nominal transfer. The programme ran through the COVID-19 pandemic, during which expanded government benefits and stimulus payments raised household resources for both arms, plausibly compressing treatment–control differences ([JPAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)). Finally, 76 months of payments is unusually long for a U.S. cash pilot, but it still captures only the first eight years of a child’s life; the long-run educational, health, and economic trajectories that would matter most for UBI policy remain unobserved, and families continued to face structural barriers (discrimination in rental and labour markets, community violence) that cash alone cannot address ([JPAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)).

### Broader U.S. landscape and quasi-universal contrasts

The post-2015 U.S. “guaranteed income” landscape is a fragmented, largely municipal and privately funded ecosystem rather than a national programme. More than 150 cities and counties have launched some form of guaranteed income since Stockton’s 2019 demonstration, but only about 30 have produced evaluation data, and only a minority of those evaluations are independent or peer-reviewed. The Guaranteed Income Pilots Dashboard, a partnership between the Stanford Basic Income Lab and the Center for Guaranteed Income Research at the University of Pennsylvania, visualises evaluations of “30+ guaranteed income pilots across the United States” ([Guaranteed Income Pilots Dashboard](https://guaranteedincome.us/)). California became the first U.S. state to dedicate public funding to local guaranteed-income programmes, appropriating $35 million over five years; the Department of Social Services awarded grants to seven pilot sites serving pregnant individuals and former foster youth, with a subsequent tranche for adults aged 60 and over ([California CDSS](https://www.cdss.ca.gov/inforesources/guaranteed-income-pilot-program)). The pilots share a common design—unconditional monthly cash, usually $500–$1,000, paid for 12–24 months to targeted low-income groups—but they differ in amount, duration, targeting, funding, and evaluation method, which makes pooled conclusions hazardous. Independent evaluations consistently report short-run gains in material wellbeing, food and housing security, and mental health; employment effects are mixed and often null ([UChicago Urban Labs, 2024](https://urbanlabs.uchicago.edu/attachments/5ff88f36218ed7d9bee03d8b2e3f5e87998b51c9/store/71b4d2b6c98f6d7d0e4b8e5ac62db72a0139a748a1dc59ee192309d9e7cf/092024+IEL+GI+Literature+Review.pdf)).

The closest U.S. approximations to a long-run, universal cash transfer are the Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend and the 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit, but neither is a canonical UBI. The Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend is paid annually to all eligible Alaska residents; the fund was valued at $84.4 billion as of 30 September 2025 ([APFC](https://apfc.org/)). Jones and Marinescu (2022) find a negligible extensive-margin labour-market effect: labour-force participation rose by about 0.1 percentage point (95% CI −0.031 to 0.032), or roughly 0.06 percentage point per $1,000 of dividend spending, with a small increase in part-time work ([Jones & Marinescu 2022, *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190299)). This is the strongest U.S. evidence that a universal, permanent, unconditional cash transfer does not produce a large labour-force exit, but it cannot settle the question for a larger, tax-financed UBI. The 2021 expansion of the Child Tax Credit was a national, quasi-universal cash-transfer episode: child poverty fell to 5.2% in 2021 on the Census Bureau’s Supplemental Poverty Measure, down from 9.7% the previous year—a 46% single-year decline—and the credit lifted 5.3 million people, including 2.9 million children, out of poverty ([U.S. Census Bureau, 2022](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2022/09/record-drop-in-child-poverty.html); [Joint Economic Committee](https://www.jec.senate.gov/public/_cache/files/dd209a98-c23b-4b2e-8478-61a55ec2b647/the-expanded-child-tax-credit-dramatically-reduced-child-poverty-in-2021-final-1-.pdf)). The expansion was a one-year measure: monthly payments expired after December 2021, and poverty subsequently rose ([CBPP, 2023](https://www.cbpp.org/press/statements/record-rise-in-poverty-highlights-importance-of-child-tax-credit-health-coverage)). It demonstrates both the feasibility and the political fragility of large-scale cash support.

## Comparative effectiveness: employment and labour supply

The employment evidence from Finland, Canada, and the United States does not support a single “UBI effect” on work. The divergence is better explained by target population, benefit adequacy, duration, macroeconomic context, and COVID-19 than by a universal causal response.

Target population is the most important modifier. Finland targeted unemployed people, for whom the payment replaced unemployment benefits and produced no net income gain while unemployed; the income effect was therefore weak, and the main channel was a reduced participation tax rate, which did not generate aggregate gains. SEED targeted low-income neighbourhood residents, including working and work-seeking people, for whom $500/month could remove material barriers to full-time work. OpenResearch targeted low-income working-age people, for whom $1,000/month represented about a 40% increase in household income, generating a clear income effect and a moderate labour-supply reduction. Benefit adequacy does not show a simple dose-response: the largest payment (OpenResearch) produced the clearest reduction in labour supply, while the smallest (SEED) was associated with a full-time employment gain, but the ordering is confounded by target population and macro context. Duration also matters: Finland and OpenResearch ran two and three years, SEED two years, and Ontario was cancelled after about one year, leaving no causal estimate. Finally, macroeconomic context and COVID-19 differentiate the cases: Finland ran in a strong pre-pandemic economy; SEED spanned pre-COVID and pandemic recovery; OpenResearch ran entirely during the pandemic and recovery.

The effect sizes are not directly comparable. The outcome measures differ (employment days, employment rate, full-time employment, weekly hours), the target populations differ, and the control conditions differ: Finland’s control group received conditional unemployment benefits, SEED’s received no transfer, and OpenResearch’s received $50/month. Cross-country pooling is therefore not warranted. Short-run pilot effects also cannot be extrapolated to long-run national consequences: the OpenResearch finding of no job-quality or human-capital improvement suggests that short-run reductions in labour supply may not translate into better long-run outcomes, but the study cannot observe long-run career trajectories.

## Comparative effectiveness: poverty, material wellbeing, and health

Unconditional cash improved perceived economic security, financial management, and income stability more consistently than it improved hard material outcomes such as food insecurity, housing stability, and debt. Finland’s national experiment and Stockton’s SEED pilot both document better perceived economic security and reduced financial strain; the largest U.S. child-poverty RCT, Baby’s First Years, raised household income but did not reduce economic hardship or worry; and the Campbell systematic review finds food-insecurity improvements in the two studies that measured it, while concluding that the overall evidence base is limited, heterogeneous, and at high risk of bias ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)).

On income adequacy and economic security, Finland’s official results show that basic-income recipients had a more positive perception of their income and economic wellbeing, were more likely to find their financial situation manageable and protected financially, and reported higher life satisfaction and less mental strain than the control group ([Finnish Government](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)). Two caveats qualify this finding: the survey response rate was only 23% (31% treatment, 20% control), and the government itself cautioned that the better wellbeing could not be attributed with certainty to basic-income receipt. Because the Finnish payment partially substituted for existing benefits, the net income gain was smaller than the gross €560/month, which likely muted any material-hardship effect. SEED provides the clearest income-stability signal: the final peer-reviewed evaluation found lower income volatility, greater financial independence and self-sufficiency, and a better ability to weather pandemic-related financial shocks among recipients ([Penn SP2/CGIR, 2023](https://sp2.upenn.edu/news/study-guaranteed-income-improved-peoples-health-during-pandemic/)). Baby’s First Years increased household income by roughly 18% and reduced poverty, but mothers in the high-cash group reported similar levels of economic hardship and worry as the low-cash group ([JPAL, 2025](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)). Ontario’s evidence on income adequacy is partial: the pilot was cancelled before a full evaluation, leaving participant reports rather than a completed official assessment.

On food security, the strongest cross-country evidence comes from the Campbell review, which found that food insecurity was reported in only two studies, both showing improvements (SMD = −0.57, 95% CI −0.65 to −0.49; and SMD = −0.41, 95% CI −0.57 to −0.26); because the study designs differed, the estimates were not pooled ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). The review does not name the two studies in its abstract, so these effect sizes should not be attributed to a specific country without further verification. At the country level, the evidence is thinner. A peer-reviewed study of food and eating practices in the Ontario Basic Income Pilot exists, but the full text was not retrievable here, so specific effect sizes are not reported ([Halpenny et al. 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1467-9566.70261)). SEED recipients spent heavily on food—the largest spending category in the first-year analysis—but no quantified food-security outcome was reported ([New America, 2021](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/exploring-seed-a-guaranteed-income-demonstrations-health-equity-implications/)). Baby’s First Years is the exception in the negative direction: families did not increase groceries spending, and economic hardship and worry were statistically indistinguishable between the high- and low-cash groups ([JPAL, 2025](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)).

Housing effects are weakly documented across the three countries. SEED recipients used payments for rent and bills, and the pilot established a Hold Harmless Fund that provided $35,100 to 13 households to offset losses in housing-voucher and public-housing rental assistance caused by the added income ([New America, 2021](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/exploring-seed-a-guaranteed-income-demonstrations-health-equity-implications/)). Baby’s First Years families did not increase rent spending ([JPAL, 2025](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)), which suggests that a modest monthly transfer does not automatically translate into better housing. Cross-pilot U.S. evidence points in a more positive direction: participants in several pilots reported spending on rent and bills, catching up on debt, and saving, and the Saint Paul People’s Prosperity Pilot reported a 7-percentage-point increase in participants moving to better-quality homes ([Results for America, 2024](https://results4america.org/counties-advancing-guaranteed-income-for-families-children-and-communities/)).

The comparative evidence does not support a strong claim that unconditional cash outperforms in-kind or conditional programs on material wellbeing. The Campbell review concludes that a supplemental guaranteed basic income—provided alongside existing programs—may be effective in alleviating poverty-related outcomes, while warning that wholesale replacement of social assistance could have unintended consequences ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). Baby’s First Years complicates the case further: despite a real income gain, the transfer did not reduce economic hardship or worry, which suggests that a modest, fixed monthly amount may be too small to change material deprivation when families face high child-rearing costs and structural barriers ([JPAL, 2025](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)). None of the three focus-country pilots conducted a head-to-head comparison with an in-kind or conditional alternative, so the “cash versus in-kind” question remains unresolved by this evidence base.

Across the three countries, unconditional cash produced measurable but modest and mechanism-specific health gains—most consistently in mental health—yet the magnitude, durability, and causal certainty differ sharply with design. Finland’s randomised experiment supplies the strongest causal evidence (reduced psychotropic medication and specialised mental-health visits); Ontario’s qualitative evidence shows stress and diet improvements that were reversed by the pilot’s cancellation; SEED shows pandemic-era physical-health and anxiety improvements; Mincome’s long-run hospitalisation claim remains methodologically contested; and Baby’s First Years found no broad maternal mental-health or wellbeing effects despite reducing poverty. The most consistent pathway is reduced financial precarity and stress. In Finland, the authors infer that the income gain “alleviated financial stress, which improved mental health,” because the effect appeared only for mental-health drug use and specialised mental-health care and not for other diagnoses ([FIT/VATT summary](https://verotutkimus.fi/en/cash-transfers/)). Ontario participants described the same mechanism in their own terms: stable income let them “plan better for their futures,” move to better housing, buy healthier food, and stop relying on food charity, all of which lowered stress ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/a-basic-income-can-be-a-strong-investment-in-mental-health-250018)). Baby’s First Years is the critical negative case: the cash “reduced poverty and increased household income” and shifted spending toward children, yet produced no measurable change in maternal psychological distress, wellbeing, or observed mother–child interaction quality; the research team attributes this to the modest amount, its failure to adjust for family circumstances or living costs, and unaddressed structural barriers such as discrimination and community violence ([JPAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)).

Durability differs sharply. Finland offers the only register-based evidence on persistence: psychotropic-medication use began falling six months after randomisation and “remained lower even after the trial ended,” over a three-year follow-up ([FIT/VATT](https://verotkimus.fi/en/cash-transfers/)). Ontario supplies the mirror-image lesson: the gains were real but reversible, and the abrupt cancellation itself caused harm—participants who had signed leases, enrolled in education, or started businesses were “thrust back into poverty,” and the authors describe the termination as “structural violence” ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/a-basic-income-can-be-a-strong-investment-in-mental-health-250018)). This establishes that programme duration and credible expectations are active ingredients in mental-health outcomes. SEED’s effects were measured over 24 months and are confounded by the pandemic labour market, so persistence is unclear ([Penn SP2](https://sp2.upenn.edu/news/study-guaranteed-income-improved-peoples-health-during-pandemic/)). Baby’s First Years, despite following families through the child’s first six years, found no broad durable family-process gains ([JPAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)). The Finnish authors themselves caution that the health impact was “limited” and that targeted mental-health services are “likely to be a much more cost-effective way of treating mental health problems than a change in the benefit system such as the one experimented” ([FIT/VATT](https://verotkimus.fi/en/cash-transfers/)). A UK microsimulation review finds that UBI’s mental-health impact reverses sign depending on assumed labour-supply responses—Full+ UBI increased common mental disorder prevalence by 0.38 percentage points (95% UI 0.13 to 0.69) under base assumptions but reduced it by 0.27 points (95% UI −0.49 to −0.05) under minimal employment impacts ([Thomson et al. 2024, *PLOS Medicine*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10947674/)).

## Fiscal cost and affordability

The pilots reviewed here cannot answer fiscal questions: they were small, short, categorical or partial, and none varied national tax rates, coverage, or macroeconomic conditions. Fiscal cost depends on national coverage, benefit adequacy, the financing instrument, and behavioural response—all outside pilot design. The best available costings converge on a clear conclusion: a canonical UBI at an adequate level would cost roughly as much as or more than entire existing welfare states, and financing it would require tax increases large enough to erode the work-incentive advantages UBI’s advocates claim.

The fiscal evidence points to three linked conclusions. First, universality is the main cost driver: the BC panel’s ~C$51 billion UBI versus roughly C$7.5–17 billion for income-tested alternatives providing similar poverty reduction, and Hoynes and Rothstein’s observation that a UBI directs more to middle-income and childless households, both imply that most UBI spending is not poverty-targeted ([progress.org](https://www.progress.org/wiki/bc-basic-income-panel-final-report/); [Hoynes & Rothstein 2019](https://gspp.berkeley.edu/archived-files/research/pdf/Hoynes-Rothstein-annurev-economics-080218-030237.pdf)). Second, financing is not a secondary detail: the BC panel found that the tax increases needed to fund a UBI would reproduce many of the work disincentives UBI is supposed to remove, and the PBO’s offsets require eliminating tax credits and reducing basic personal amounts—a distributional shift onto middle earners ([progress.org](https://www.progress.org/wiki/bc-basic-income-panel-final-report/); [PBO, 19 February 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)). Third, long-run affordability is sensitive to inflation, earnings growth, and demographic change: the PBO’s cost estimate for 2025 rose from C$90 billion in its 2021 report to C$107 billion in 2025 under the nuclear-family definition, and its behavioural-cost estimates (C$5.0 billion nuclear, C$3.6 billion economic family) show that even the dynamic cost is non-trivial ([PBO, 19 February 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)). None of these dimensions is tested by the Finnish, Ontario, SEED, OpenResearch, or Baby’s First Years pilots.

## Macroeconomic modelling: inflation, output, and distribution

Post-2015 macroeconomic models do not converge on a single UBI verdict; predictions swing sharply with the benefit level and financing regime, and they diverge systematically from pilot evidence because pilots measure short-run, partial-equilibrium responses while models embed general-equilibrium and intergenerational channels.

A UBI’s labour-supply effect is the net of two opposing forces. The **income effect** treats leisure as a normal good: unconditional cash raises non-labour income and reduces work effort. The **substitution effect** works through the benefit phase-out: means-tested transfers impose high effective marginal tax rates on earners, so replacing them with an unconditional payment lowers the tax wedge and encourages work. Post-2015 reviews conclude the net short-run effect is likely moderate—Hoynes and Rothstein summarise the literature as “substitution effects on short-run labor supply are moderate and income effects are small,” while stressing that the major open questions concern longer-run effects ([Hoynes & Rothstein 2019, NBER WP 25538](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25538/w25538.pdf)). At the aggregate level, universal cash also generates **demand stimulus** (higher consumption, especially in non-tradable local services) that can offset household-level labour-supply reductions, while **tax drag** from financing through distortionary consumption or labour-income taxes reduces labour supply and capital accumulation.

The post-2015 macro literature does not treat inflation as a headline modelled outcome; most models solve in real terms and analyse tax-financed UBI. The closest analogue is the consumption-tax channel: Luduvice’s expenditure-neutral UBI raises the consumption tax by 1.3 percentage points, and the $1,000/month variant requires 19.3 percentage points, which mechanically raises consumer prices ([Luduvice 2024 manuscript](https://avdluduvice.github.io/files/Luduvice_UBI_June24_final.pdf)). Daruich and Fernández find their welfare conclusions are “robust to various alternative ways of financing UBI,” implying that the *choice* of funding instrument matters less for welfare than the transfer’s size and the intergenerational channels it triggers ([Daruich & Fernández 2024](http://ungated-research.s3.amazonaws.com/pdf/1647-AER-2024-1-Universal_Basic_Income__A_Dynamic_Assessment.pdf)). Debt financing and its price effects are essentially unmodelled in this literature.

The divergence between models and pilots is instructive rather than contradictory. Finland’s RCT found a small or null aggregate employment effect ([official results](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)); SEED’s small municipal pilot found full-time employment rising from 28% to 40% in the treatment group ([SEED first-year report](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6039d612b17d055cac14070f/t/6050294a1212aa40fdaf773a/1615866187890/SEED_Preliminary+Analysis-SEEDs+First+Year_Final+Report_Individual+Pages+.pdf)); and OpenResearch found a moderate labour-supply effect with a $50/month control arm ([Vivalt et al. 2024](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32719/w32719.pdf)). Models predict a wider range—from Luduvice’s positive participation effect for a modest UBI to employment losses for a generous one—because they incorporate economy-wide tax financing, permanent-transfer expectations, and general-equilibrium price and wage adjustments that short-duration, targeted, partially substituted pilots cannot capture. Hoynes and Rothstein make this explicit: “the ongoing UBI pilot studies will do little to resolve the major outstanding questions” about long-run effects ([Hoynes & Rothstein 2019](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25538/w25538.pdf)).

The Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend and the 1970s NIT experiments are the two quasi-universal, long-run reference points available, and they bracket the model predictions: Alaska shows no aggregate employment loss, while the NIT experiments show moderate labour-supply reductions under generous guarantees ([Jones & Marinescu 2022](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190299); [Munnell 1987](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/conference/30/conf30a.pdf)). A sequential microsimulation–dynamic computable general-equilibrium application to Scotland reports modest output and employment contractions under a tax-financed UBI, consistent with Luduvice’s generous-UBI scenario, though the published article’s exact macroeconomic magnitudes remain provisional pending access to its results section ([Connolly et al. 2024, *Structural Change and Economic Dynamics* 68:259–268](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/309363/1/309363.pdf)).

## Long-term consequences: durability, intergenerational effects, and political risk

The post-2015 pilots cannot establish the long-run consequences of a national UBI. Every focus-country pilot was short (one to three years), small, and tied to a particular political and economic setting; the only focus-country experimental evidence with long-run follow-up, Manitoba’s Mincome, is contested and its measured effects faded after payments stopped; the largest intergenerational RCT, Baby’s First Years, found no child-development impacts by age four despite income gains; and the 2021 U.S. Child Tax Credit expansion shows that a large poverty reduction can be achieved quickly but is not durable once the policy expires.

| Domain | Short-run measured effect (evidence status) | Long-run inferred consequence | Evidence status for long-run inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labour supply and employment | Finland: small/null aggregate; SEED: +12 pp full-time; OpenResearch: moderate reduction, leisure up; Alaska: no employment effect, +1.8 pp part-time (direct) | Permanent national UBI’s effect on labour supply | Modelled/speculative; pilots too short and context-bound |
| Health | Mincome: 8.5% hospitalisation decline, contested; Finland: better self-reported wellbeing and reduced psychotropic use; BFY: null child development at age 4 (direct) | Durable health trajectories | Speculative; Mincome effects faded by 1985 |
| Poverty and material hardship | CTC: 46% child-poverty decline in 2021; BFY: income/poverty gains, no subjective hardship improvement (direct) | Sustained poverty reduction | Speculative; CTC gain reversed after expiration |

Durability of wellbeing and behavioural change is similarly difficult to project forward. In Finland, recipients reported higher life satisfaction, less mental strain, greater trust, and more confidence in the future than the control group, but the survey was conducted just before the experiment ended, had a 23% response rate, and the government explicitly stated that it could not be certain the improvements were due to the basic income itself ([Finnish Government, 2020](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi); [EC ESPN, 2019](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)). Mincome’s fade-out and Baby’s First Years’ null effects on subjective hardship and maternal well-being reinforce the point: short-run wellbeing improvements are real, but their persistence after payments end is unestablished.

Baby’s First Years provides the most rigorous intergenerational evidence, and it is largely null. After the first four years of the intervention, the cash transfers had no statistically significant impact on four preregistered primary outcomes—language, executive function, social-emotional problems, and high-frequency brain activity—or on three secondary outcomes ([Noble et al. 2025, NBER WP 33844](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33844)). Long-run intergenerational effects—school completion, earnings, fertility—therefore remain speculative. The 2021 expansion of the U.S. Child Tax Credit is the largest recent natural experiment in child-poverty reduction, and it demonstrates both the potential and the fragility of cash-based poverty policy. The American Rescue Plan lifted 5.3 million people out of poverty, including 2.9 million children; the credit reduced the Black child-poverty rate by 6.3 percentage points (from 14.5% to 8.1%) and contributed to a 46% decline in child poverty since 2020 ([U.S. Census Bureau, 2022](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2022/09/record-drop-in-child-poverty.html)). The expansion was a one-year measure: it expired at the end of 2021, the credit reverted, and poverty subsequently rose ([CBPP, 2023](https://www.cbpp.org/press/statements/record-rise-in-poverty-highlights-importance-of-child-tax-credit-health-coverage)). It demonstrates that a near-universal cash transfer can reduce child poverty rapidly, but it cannot speak to long-run child development or family stability because the policy was not sustained.

The political record is the strongest evidence in this domain, and it is largely cautionary. Ontario’s pilot was cancelled by the Ford government in July 2018, roughly one year into its three-year timeline, with the last payment in March 2019; a class action filed on behalf of 4,000 participants was certified and seeks $200 million in general damages, and participants reported that the sudden loss of income triggered panic attacks, anxiety, and other mental-health harms ([Cavalluzzo LLP](https://www.cavalluzzo.com/basicincomeclassaction); [The Globe and Mail, 2024](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-ontario-pays-320k-in-legal-fight-over-its-cancellation-of-basic-income/)). Finland’s government did not expand the experiment after 2018 and framed the results as input to social-security reform rather than a path to implementation ([BBC News, April 2018](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-43866700)). British Columbia’s Expert Panel rejected a universal basic income in favour of 65 recommendations to strengthen existing supports, and the provincial government accepted that direction ([BC Gov News, 2021](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2021SDPR0005-000152); [Finances of the Nation, 2021](https://financesofthenation.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/2021-BC-Expert-Panel-Basic-Income-FON-rvd2.pdf)). The United States has moved in the opposite direction: the Stanford Basic Income Lab/Center for Guaranteed Income Research dashboard tracks more than 30 guaranteed-income pilots, and mayoral networks such as Mayors for a Guaranteed Income have been central to the expansion ([Guaranteed Income Pilots Dashboard](https://guaranteedincome.us/)). The pattern implies that national scalability depends less on pilot design than on political durability: pilots that create expectations can be cancelled, and short political cycles can truncate evaluation. A 2026 critical assessment of Canadian pilot-based policy-making argues that the Ontario episode damaged the credibility of evidence-based pilot design; its full text was not accessible here, so its specific arguments are noted rather than independently verified ([Smith-Carrier et al. 2026, *Basic Income Studies* 21(1):115–138](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/bistud/v21y2026i1p115-138n1003.html)).

The automation/AI rationale for UBI is a projection, not a pilot finding. Acemoglu and Restrepo’s framework shows that automation displaces labour through a displacement effect while new tasks reinstate labour through a reinstatement effect; their empirical decomposition attributes slower U.S. employment growth over the last three decades to an acceleration in displacement, a weaker reinstatement effect, and slower productivity growth ([Acemoglu & Restrepo 2019, *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 33(2):3–30](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.33.2.3)). This is a macroeconomic account of task displacement, not evidence that a universal cash transfer is the necessary or optimal response. The pilot evidence on employment is mixed and context-bound. Alaska’s Permanent Fund Dividend—the closest real-world analogue to a national UBI, as a universal and permanent cash transfer—shows no aggregate employment effect but a 1.8 percentage-point increase in part-time work, consistent with a local demand stimulus ([Jones & Marinescu 2022](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190299)). Tech-sector advocacy has linked UBI to automation risk, but the empirical pilots do not test technological unemployment, and the claim that UBI is a necessary response to automation remains a normative projection. The job-guarantee counterpoint reframes the same problem: Pavlina Tcherneva proposes a permanent, federally funded, locally administered public option for jobs at a living wage, supplying voluntary employment on demand rather than cash without work ([Tcherneva 2018, Levy Institute Working Paper 902](https://www.levyinstitute.org/pubs/wp_902.pdf)).

A final long-run risk is that a partial or “UBI-lite” program displaces more effective targeted supports without delivering the simplification and universality that justify UBI. The Campbell systematic review concludes that a supplemental guaranteed basic income—provided alongside existing programs—may be effective and “safer than a wholesale reform of existing social assistance approaches, which could have unintended consequences” ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). The BC Expert Panel reached a similar conclusion, judging it better to build on and improve income assistance than to institute a basic income, and recommending targeted basic incomes and expanded in-kind benefits ([Finances of the Nation, 2021](https://financesofthenation.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/2021-BC-Expert-Panel-Basic-Income-FON-rvd2.pdf)). Kearney and Mogstad argue that a UBI would be extremely expensive, inefficiently targeted, and potentially harmful because it would redistribute resources away from the most needy and lacks a human-capital mechanism, recommending instead pro-work, pro-skills policies such as wage subsidies, the EITC, childcare, and training; the primary text of their Economic Strategy Group memo was not retrievable here, so this position is attributed to the confirmed authorship rather than treated as independently verified evidence ([Brookings / ESG memo](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/universal-basic-income-as-a-policy-response-to-current-challenges/); [BFI confirmation](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/news/magne-mostad-and-melissa-kearney-author-memo-on-universal-basic-income-for-aspen-institute-economic-strategy-group/)).

## Expert assessment and competing frameworks

Expert assessment of UBI is not primarily an empirical disagreement but a normative one that cuts across the three focus countries. The Finnish pilot’s small or null aggregate employment effect is read by supporters as evidence that a modest, tax-funded transfer need not discourage work, and by activation-oriented critics as a failure to deliver the pilot’s stated goal. Fiscal affordability and the question of whether UBI should replace or supplement existing welfare are similarly split.

**Table 7. Expert and ideological positions.** These are interpretive positions, not causal evidence of UBI’s effects.

| Viewpoint | Main argument | Evidence cited | Country context |

| Conditional / pilot-evidence support | Pilots show wellbeing gains; employment effect modest; BI is “essential but insufficient” | [Piketty 2021](https://thomaspiketty.wordpress.com/2021/05/18/from-basic-income-to-inheritance-for-all/) | Cross-national |
| Skeptic: work-conditionality | UBI risks work disincentives; robot tax is counterproductive | [Straubhaar 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/on-the-economics-of-a-universal-basic-income.html) | Germany / EU |

Supporters treat employment as a secondary, even inappropriate, yardstick: Standing argues that “the primary justifications for a basic income are ethical or moral, not instrumental,” framing it as “first and foremost a matter of social justice” and as a source of “republican freedom”—the ability to refuse exploitative work ([Standing 2019](https://www.progressiveeconomyforum.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/PEF_Piloting_Basic_Income_Guy_Standing.pdf)). Piketty likewise calls basic income “an essential but insufficient tool” whose amount is “always extremely modest,” so that “by construction it can only be a partial tool in the fight against inequality” ([Piketty 2021](https://thomaspiketty.wordpress.com/2021/05/18/from-basic-income-to-inheritance-for-all/)). Skeptics invert the framing. Straubhaar’s critique invokes Christopher Pissarides’s conditional support—a universal minimum income is acceptable only “as long as we know how to apply it without taking away incentive to work at the lower end of the market”—and treats work-disincentive risk as the central economic objection ([Straubhaar 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/on-the-economics-of-a-universal-basic-income.html)). Green’s assessment for the BC Expert Panel reaches a more agnostic conclusion: “some of the claims for positive effects from a basic income receive support from the empirical evidence while others do not,” and even where outcomes are positive “a basic income is often not the most direct way to achieve the outcome” ([Green 2021](https://econ.cms.arts.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2021/03/Claims-Made-For-or-About-a-Basic-Income_DGreen1.pdf)).

The affordability dispute turns on financing assumptions. Finnish microsimulation of a partial basic income found that budget neutrality at €450–€750 per month required flat tax rates of 40–50.5%, and explicitly modelled no behavioural response ([Honkanen, Kangas et al., 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). Green’s paper presses the administrative-economy claim—quoting Van Parijs and Vanderborght’s assertion that “the overall administrative cost … can safely be expected to be less for a universal scheme than for a means-tested one”—against two Canadian realities: only 89.1% of census adults filed taxes in 2015, with 7.4% “linked non-filers” and 3.5% absent from the CRA system, and low earners far more likely to be linked non-filers (19.2% vs 7.4%); and the tax system’s annual frequency, which is unresponsive to within-year income shocks ([Green 2021](https://econ.cms.arts.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2021/03/Claims-Made-For-or-About-a-Basic-Income_DGreen1.pdf)). On the “replace or supplement” question, Straubhaar’s canonical definition treats UBI as replacing the welfare state wholesale, while Piketty rejects the substitution framing outright, arguing that basic income, a job guarantee, and “inheritance for all” are “complementary and not substitutable” and should be implemented “in stages” ([Straubhaar 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/on-the-economics-of-a-universal-basic-income.html); [Piketty 2021](https://thomaspiketty.wordpress.com/2021/05/18/from-basic-income-to-inheritance-for-all/)).

The core “sufficiency” divide is whether pilot evidence can support national policy. Standing treats pilots as capable of demonstrating “the strengths or shortcomings of a basic income scheme” and as a way to move from objection to design ([Standing 2019](https://www.progressiveeconomyforum.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/PEF_Piloting_Basic_Income_Guy_Standing.pdf)). Green’s assessment is more demanding: even supported claims do not establish that UBI is the best instrument, and the Finnish modelling was explicitly static, with “any behavioural effects of basic income … not modelled” ([Green 2021](https://econ.cms.arts.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2021/03/Claims-Made-For-or-About-a-Basic-Income_DGreen1.pdf); [Honkanen, Kangas et al., 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). This is the central sufficiency divide: pilots can establish short-run behavioural and wellbeing effects, but not fiscal affordability, universality, or long-run labour supply at national scale.

Country-specific expert traditions shape the debate differently. Finland’s debate is organised around activation and the “welfare wall”: the government’s stated hypotheses were work disincentives and bureaucracy, and the microsimulation literature is explicitly about participation tax rates ([Honkanen, Kangas et al., 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). Canada’s debate is shaped by the guaranteed-annual-income legacy and by the BC panel’s cost-and-claims assessment, which treats UBI as one claim among many rather than a singular reform ([Green 2021](https://econ.cms.arts.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2021/03/Claims-Made-For-or-About-a-Basic-Income_DGreen1.pdf)). The U.S. debate is marked by municipal experimentation, tech-sector advocacy, and AI-era narratives, and by a job-guarantee counter-movement ([Standing 2019](https://www.progressiveeconomyforum.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/PEF_Piloting_Basic_Income_Guy_Standing.pdf); [Tcherneva 2024](https://www.levyinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/wp_1060.pdf); [Acemoglu & Restrepo 2019](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.33.2.3)).

## Policy conclusions and research agenda

The comparative evidence supports a conditional, staged verdict rather than a move to national UBI. No fully universal and unconditional basic income has ever been implemented at scale, so all claims about national UBI rest on approximation from targeted, short-duration pilots ([Hasdell 2020](https://basicincome.stanford.edu/uploads/Umbrella%20Review%20BI_final.pdf)). The Finnish RCT, the Ontario quasi-experiment, the BC Expert Panel, and the U.S. pilots converge on three policy-relevant signals: payment adequacy and duration shape outcomes; universality and community-level effects remain largely untested; and political durability is a first-order design constraint, not an implementation detail. The most defensible course is further well-protected pilots, linked administrative data, and fiscal modelling before any national commitment, with a preference for supplemental rather than full-replacement cash design.

1. **Do not proceed to national UBI on current pilot evidence; invest in further protected pilots and fiscal modelling first.** No fully universal, unconditional BI has been implemented at scale, and the BC panel rejects BI as the main pillar ([Hasdell 2020](https://basicincome.stanford.edu/uploads/Umbrella%20Review%20BI_final.pdf); [BC Panel Executive Summary](https://bcbasicincomepanel.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Executive_Summary_BC_Basic_Income_Panel.pdf)). Caveat: pilots are short and categorical; national effects may differ.
2. **Prefer supplemental cash over full replacement of existing supports.** Supplemental guaranteed basic income improved subjective financial well-being, life satisfaction, and mental distress; full guaranteed basic income did not improve self-rated overall health; the supplemental design is judged “more prudent” ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)).
3. **Set payment levels above social-assistance amounts if wellbeing is the goal.** Guaranteed basic income with amounts larger than social assistance improved life satisfaction and subjective financial well-being; Baby’s First Years’ $333/month produced narrow gains only ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/); [JPAL, Baby’s First Years summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life)). Caveat: adequacy thresholds are undefined and cost trade-offs are not answered by pilots.
4. **Design for long horizons and post-payment follow-up.** Finnish health gains largely faded after the two-year window; Baby’s First Years’ six-year design and OpenResearch’s three-year design show the value of duration ([Salokangas 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml); [JPAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life); [OpenResearch findings](https://www.openresearchlab.org/findings/nber-working-paper-employment)).
5. **Use universal or true saturation designs when the policy question is universal.** Ontario’s Lindsay site was not a true saturation site—eligibility was limited to households already entitled at enrollment—so community-level effects could not be observed ([Mendelson 2021, Maytree transcript](https://maytree.com/publications/critical-lessons-from-ontario-about-how-to-set-up-a-basic-income-experiment/)).
6. **Protect pilots from political transitions through multi-party commitment and pre-registered evaluation.** Ontario’s experiment was cancelled after a 2018 government change; Finland did not extend its pilot; British Columbia rejected full UBI ([Mendelson 2021](https://maytree.com/publications/critical-lessons-from-ontario-about-how-to-set-up-a-basic-income-experiment/); [Salokangas 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml)).
7. **Link evaluations to administrative records and pre-register outcomes.** Finnish register linkage enabled precise health and employment analysis; OpenResearch combined surveys, administrative records, and a mobile app ([Salokangas 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml); [OpenResearch findings](https://www.openresearchlab.org/findings/nber-working-paper-employment)).
8. **Harmonise outcome measures across pilots.** The Campbell review found 176 poverty-related outcome variables across 27 studies and recommends standard validated measures ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)).
9. **For child and family policy, pair long-duration cash with structural supports; do not expect cash alone to transform family processes.** Baby’s First Years reduced poverty and increased spending on children (about $68/month on child-specific items) but found no broad effects on observed parenting quality, psychological distress, or relationship quality; the 2021 Child Tax Credit expansion cut child poverty sharply but was time-limited ([JPAL, Baby’s First Years summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/11-24-25/what-recent-findings-babys-first-years-study-reveals-about-cashs-impact-family-life); [U.S. Census Bureau, 2022](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2022/09/record-drop-in-child-poverty.html)).
10. **Communicate uncertainty.** Report effect sizes with confidence intervals rather than headline nulls or percentage gains, and separate measured pilot effects from modelled projections and normative argument. The Finnish second-year gain equivalent to 6.6 additional days worked is confounded by a concurrent reform that tightened job-search requirements for the control group only; OpenResearch’s $1,000/month transfer reduced labour-market participation with no offsetting productive activity ([Salokangas 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml); [OpenResearch findings](https://www.openresearchlab.org/findings/nber-working-paper-employment)).

The research agenda should resolve the gaps the pilots cannot: long-run and post-payment follow-up using the Baby’s First Years model; universal or true saturation-site designs; linked administrative data on health, education, employment, and tax records; cross-national harmonisation of outcome measures; macro-fiscal modelling of financing and distributional effects; employment-quality and human-capital measures, because OpenResearch found no improvement in job quality and no significant human-capital investment; health-cost offset studies building on the Finnish psychotropic-drug reduction and the Mincome reassessments; political-durability research on institutional designs that survive government transitions; and an explicit typology of basic-income variants in evaluation, since treating guaranteed basic income as a single intervention obscures differences between full, supplemental, and gradual-withdrawal designs ([Rizvi et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)).

If a developed country proceeds, the defensible sequence is further pilots with universal or saturation components, linked administrative data, pre-registered outcomes, multi-party evaluation guarantees, and parallel fiscal modelling; only then consider a national scheme. What should be avoided is replacing targeted supports with a shallow universal payment—the BC panel’s central warning ([BC Panel Executive Summary](https://bcbasicincomepanel.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Executive_Summary_BC_Basic_Income_Panel.pdf))—or treating a short, categorical pilot as a national forecast. The U.S. expanded Child Tax Credit is the strongest near-universal cash-transfer lesson: it lifted 5.3 million people out of poverty, including 2.9 million children, and reduced the Black and Hispanic child-poverty rates by 6.3 percentage points each in 2021, but it was a time-limited tax credit that expired ([U.S. Census Bureau, 2022](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2022/09/record-drop-in-child-poverty.html)). That episode shows the poverty-reduction power of adequate, broad cash support and the political fragility of temporary measures—both central to any future UBI design.
