# Best U.S. relocation cities for a remote San Francisco software engineer earning $120,000

## Executive verdict

After screening for **at least a 30% overall cost-of-living reduction versus San Francisco**, materially cheaper rent, lower healthcare costs, strong home internet, genuinely walkable neighborhoods, substantial green space, and access to a major commercial airport, my top three are:

| Rank | City | Why it makes the cut | Biggest drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **🥇 Pittsburgh, PA** | Best overall balance | About **40.0% lower overall COL** than SF; current average rent about **67% lower**; very walkable East End neighborhoods; extensive urban trails; much lower income-tax structure than California; fiber options | Citywide transit/walkability is only moderate; gray winters; smaller airport |
| **🥈 Minneapolis, MN** | Best quality-of-life/infrastructure choice | About **43.8% lower overall COL**; current rent about **63.7% lower**; citywide Walk Score **71** and Bike Score **83**; exceptional parks; excellent airport; multi-gig fiber | The winter is a real lifestyle change, and Minnesota income taxes are high by non-California standards |
| **🥉 Philadelphia, PA** | Best car-free/big-city choice | About **34.5% lower overall COL**; current rent about **65.2% lower**; Walk Score **75**, Transit Score **67**; excellent in-city hiking; large airport with rail access; highest diversity of the three | Only just clears your 30% COL requirement; healthcare savings are weaker; Philadelphia imposes a substantial wage tax |

The core affordability finding comes from the **C2ER Cost of Living Index 2025 annual average**. San Francisco scores **163.7**, versus **98.3 for Pittsburgh, 92.0 for Minneapolis, and 107.2 for Philadelphia**. That translates to estimated overall consumer-cost reductions of **40.0%, 43.8%, and 34.5%**, respectively. C2ER is particularly relevant to you because its basket is designed around professional/managerial households and uses more than 90,000 prices across roughly 60 consumer items; importantly, it excludes taxes, which I analyze separately below. [[1]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388)

There is one important qualification to your non-negotiables. **All three easily satisfy “overall cost of living at least 30% below SF,” and rent is vastly cheaper, but none has a C2ER healthcare sub-index fully 30% below San Francisco.** Minneapolis comes closest at about **26.8% lower**, Pittsburgh about **23.7% lower**, and Philadelphia about **15.9% lower**. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388) If by “especially rent and healthcare” you meant that *healthcare itself* must also be at least 30% cheaper, rather than simply being materially cheaper, I would change the shortlist; I address that edge case below.

**My personal recommendation for your stated priorities is Pittsburgh**, specifically **Shadyside or Bloomfield**. Minneapolis is the strongest alternative if you can tolerate its climate. Philadelphia is the choice I would make if being able to live a genuinely big-city, car-light life matters more than maximizing your cost savings.

## How I screened the field

I treated the 30% affordability threshold as a hard filter rather than merely looking for places that “feel cheap.” That matters: for example, the same C2ER dataset gives **Chicago 117.6** and **Portland 116.3** against San Francisco's 163.7, making them only about **28.2% and 29.0% cheaper**, respectively. Both are attractive relocation candidates in the abstract, but neither quite satisfies your stated threshold, so I excluded them. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388)

I then cross-checked C2ER with **October 2026 Zillow Rental Manager data**, because a standardized cost-of-living apartment is not necessarily what you would encounter while apartment hunting today. Zillow currently puts average San Francisco rent across property and bedroom types at about **$4,745/month**. Comparable citywide averages are roughly **$1,567 in Pittsburgh, $1,722 in Minneapolis, and $1,650 in Philadelphia**. Those imply current headline rent discounts of approximately **67.0%, 63.7%, and 65.2%**, respectively. [[3]](https://www.zillow.com/rental-manager/market-trends/san-francisco-ca/)

The two rent datasets use different methodologies, but they point in the same direction. C2ER's standardized apartment is **$3,828 in SF, $1,599 in Pittsburgh, $1,453 in Minneapolis, and $2,147 in Philadelphia**, meaning a roughly **58–62% rent discount in Pittsburgh/Minneapolis and 44% in Philadelphia** on that measure. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388) Your actual rent in the most desirable walkable neighborhoods will generally differ from a citywide average, so I would use these figures for relative comparison rather than as a promise of what a particular unit will cost.

For walkability, I deliberately looked beyond metropolitan averages. Pittsburgh's citywide Walk Score is **62**, Minneapolis is **71**, and Philadelphia is **75**. Minneapolis also has a **Bike Score of 83**, while Philadelphia posts **Transit 67 / Bike 67**. [[4]](https://www.walkscore.com/PA/Pittsburgh) More importantly for Pittsburgh, the East End neighborhoods that fit your lifestyle are dramatically more walkable than the city average: **Shadyside scores 91 and Bloomfield 92**. Philadelphia's **Center City West scores 99**. [[5]](https://www.walkscore.com/PA/Pittsburgh/Shadyside)

For internet, I put more weight on **wired fiber availability at residential addresses** than on broad “fastest city” rankings. There is no single current, authoritative, apples-to-apples city reliability score covering all three cities; Ookla's public data are heavily address/network dependent. For a remote engineer, that makes the building itself the meaningful unit of analysis. Minneapolis has Quantum Fiber service with plans from hundreds of Mbps through **multi-gig service up to 8 Gbps in select locations**, with symmetrical speeds on most plans. Philadelphia has Verizon Fios, which Verizon describes as a **100% fiber network available across the Philadelphia area**, subject to address verification. Pittsburgh has Verizon Fios and cable competition; current provider comparisons list multi-gig fiber availability there as well. [[6]](https://www.quantumfiber.com/local/mn/minneapolis)

For whichever city you choose, I would make **“fiber confirmed at the exact apartment address” a lease-signing condition**, rather than assuming that a ZIP code or neighborhood-level availability map guarantees fiber in your building. Verizon and Quantum Fiber themselves explicitly direct prospective customers to check the individual address. [[7]](https://www.verizon.com/home/internet/fios-fastest-internet/faq/)

## Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania — best overall fit

**Why I rank it first:** Pittsburgh gives up relatively little on your non-negotiables while producing very large housing savings, strong access to actual wooded and hilly terrain, and a much more favorable tax environment than California. It does not lead every individual category, but it has the fewest painful compromises.

On C2ER's measure, Pittsburgh's index of **98.3 versus SF's 163.7** makes it approximately **40.0% cheaper overall**. Its healthcare index is **94.9 versus SF's 124.3**, approximately **23.7% lower**, while C2ER's standardized apartment is **$1,599 rather than $3,828**. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388) Zillow's current citywide average rent of about **$1,567** versus San Francisco's **$4,745** reinforces that this is not merely an artifact of the C2ER basket. [[8]](https://www.zillow.com/rental-manager/market-trends/pittsburgh-pa/)

An illustrative way of thinking about the difference is that, holding the C2ER consumption basket constant and ignoring taxes, **$120,000 of SF purchasing power corresponds to only about $72,100 in Pittsburgh**. You would keep the $120,000 remote salary rather than taking that pay cut, so the difference represents substantial potential additional room for saving, travel, retirement contributions, or better housing. That is an index-based approximation rather than a personal budget forecast. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388)

The biggest reason Pittsburgh works for somebody who dislikes driving is that you should **not choose Pittsburgh generically**. Choose the right neighborhood. The city overall has a Walk Score of only **62**, but **Shadyside scores 91, Bloomfield 92, North Oakland 91, and Lower Lawrenceville 86**. [[9]](https://www.walkscore.com/PA/Pittsburgh) My first apartment search would therefore be Shadyside/Bloomfield, rather than a cheaper but more car-dependent outer neighborhood.

Nature is unusually strong for a city at this price point. Pittsburgh maintains **176 parks across nearly 3,800 acres**, and its regional parks account for roughly 1,920 acres. Frick Park alone is **644 acres**, with walking and mountain-bike trails of varying difficulty. [[10]](https://www.pittsburghpa.gov/Recreation-Events/CitiParks) Unlike Minneapolis, which excels at lakes, river trails, and cycling, Pittsburgh's hills and wooded ravines make the local experience feel more like actual hiking. That is a qualitative judgment, but it is why I give Pittsburgh the edge for your explicit hiking preference.

Remote-work connectivity is good enough that I would have no hesitation moving there **provided the apartment passes an address-level fiber check**. Current Pennsylvania provider data show Verizon Fios multi-gig service in Pittsburgh, alongside Xfinity cable, while Verizon's fiber service uses a fiber-optic network and requires address verification. [[11]](https://www.allconnect.com/local/pa) Multiple wired options are also useful for a remote engineer because they leave open the possibility of choosing a building with fiber and keeping cellular/cable as a backup rather than being captive to one slow DSL connection.

Pittsburgh International Airport is substantially smaller than MSP or PHL but is still a meaningful commercial airport: it handled **9.84 million passengers in 2025**, and the airport authority says it serves **60+ nonstop destinations**. [[12]](https://flypittsburgh.com/news-article/pittsburgh-international-airport-sees-9-84m-passengers-in-2025/) For your “airport within about 90 minutes” requirement, central Pittsburgh is therefore an easy pass; the trade-off is fewer nonstop choices than you would get in Minneapolis or Philadelphia.

Taxes are a major Pittsburgh advantage relative to remaining in California, although Pennsylvania is not tax-free. Pennsylvania's personal income tax is a flat **3.07%**, and Pittsburgh residents also owe a **3% Earned Income Tax on wages**. [[13]](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/revenue/resources/tax-types-and-information/personal-income-tax) The simple headline combination is therefore about **6.07%**, although you should not interpret that as a precisely calculated effective tax rate because the state and local tax bases and rules are not identical.

The climate is a compromise, but in my view a manageable one. Pittsburgh has four real seasons; NOAA's 1991–2020 normals put a typical early-February day around a **37°F high and 21°F low**, and summer normals reach the low 80s. [[14]](https://www.weather.gov/pbz/groundhogday) The part that SF residents tend to underestimate is not merely cold: **Pittsburgh can be gray, damp, and overcast for long stretches in winter**. If lack of winter sun affects your mood, I would treat that as a serious negative rather than a footnote.

On diversity, Census QuickFacts shows a city that is approximately **22.3% Black alone, 6.2% Asian alone, and 62.7% White alone** in its race categories. [[15]](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/pittsburghcitypennsylvania) It is meaningfully diverse, but Philadelphia is clearly stronger if living in a highly heterogeneous large city is important to you.

There is one additional remote-work trade-off coming from SF that has nothing to do with money: Pittsburgh is on Eastern Time. A literal **9 a.m.–5 p.m. Pacific schedule becomes noon–8 p.m. locally**. If your team operates asynchronously that may be fantastic—you get your mornings back. If you are chained to West Coast meeting hours, it can make weeknight social life noticeably harder.

**My Pittsburgh neighborhood choice:** **Shadyside first, Bloomfield second.** They are the best evidence-backed combination here of 90+ walkability, nearby everyday services, and proximity to Pittsburgh's major urban green spaces. [[16]](https://www.walkscore.com/PA/Pittsburgh/Shadyside)

## Minneapolis, Minnesota — best infrastructure and outdoor urban lifestyle

Minneapolis is statistically the strongest city on several of your hard criteria. If winter and state income tax were not part of the equation, I could just as easily rank it first.

Its **92.0 C2ER index** is the lowest of the three finalists, approximately **43.8% below San Francisco**. The standardized C2ER apartment is **$1,453**, about **62.0% below SF**, and its healthcare index of **91.0** is approximately **26.8% below SF**, the best healthcare result among my top three. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388) Zillow's late-October 2026 citywide average rent is around **$1,722**, still roughly **63.7% below San Francisco's $4,745**. [[17]](https://www.zillow.com/rental-manager/market-trends/minneapolis-mn/)

On the same rough C2ER purchasing-power calculation, SF's $120,000 lifestyle maps to only around **$67,400 in Minneapolis before taxes**. Again, that is not a salary recommendation—it illustrates how large the consumer-cost gap is if you keep earning $120,000 remotely. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388)

This is the easiest of the three places to recommend without saying “but only live in these four blocks.” Minneapolis as a whole has a **Walk Score of 71 and Bike Score of 83**, and Walk Score identifies neighborhoods including Lyn-Lake, Downtown West, and Lowry Hill East among its most walkable areas. [[18]](https://www.walkscore.com/MN/Minneapolis) For your priorities, I would focus on **Lyn-Lake/Lowry Hill East or the neighborhoods around the Chain of Lakes**, rather than choosing a far-out suburb simply because the apartment is cheaper.

The park system is arguably Minneapolis's standout advantage. The Minneapolis Park and Recreation Board oversees about **7,059 acres of parkland and water, 22 lakes and 102 miles of Grand Rounds biking/walking paths**. [[19]](https://www.minneapolisparks.org/parks-destinations/trails-parkways/pedestrian_and_bike_trail_distances/) The **Chain of Lakes Regional Park alone is roughly 1,555 acres with 14.81 miles of pedestrian trails and 15.27 miles of bike trails**. [[20]](https://www.minneapolisparks.org/parks-destinations/parks-lakes/minneapolis_chain_of_lakes_regional_park/) The National Park Service's Mississippi National River and Recreation Area runs **72 miles through the urban region**, providing hiking, cycling, birding, boating and river access; within Minneapolis, the Winchell Trail offers a roughly **five-mile hiking-only route** along the Mississippi gorge. [[21]](https://www.nps.gov/miss/)

So Minneapolis is exceptional if “nature” means **daily access to water, running, cycling, cross-country recreation and urban trails**. Pittsburgh wins if your mental picture is steep wooded hiking. Minneapolis is comparatively flat.

Internet is a particular strength. Quantum Fiber currently advertises **500 Mbps through multi-gig plans in Minneapolis**, with speeds up to **8 Gbps in select areas** and symmetrical upload/download speeds on most plans. [[22]](https://www.quantumfiber.com/local/mn/minneapolis) For a software engineer regularly pushing containers, downloading large repos, working over VPN or doing video calls while uploading data, the availability of symmetrical fiber is more meaningful than a generic citywide download-speed ranking. Address verification remains essential. [[23]](https://www.quantumfiber.com/local/mn/minneapolis/internet-deals)

The airport is the best of these three for the combination of scale and car-free access. **MSP handled 36.07 million passengers in 2025**. [[24]](https://www.mspairport.com/about-msp) Metro Transit's Blue Line runs between downtown and the airport every 15 minutes, with **downtown-to-airport travel under 30 minutes** according to Metro Transit. [[25]](https://www.metrotransit.org/airport) That comfortably beats your 90-minute requirement without requiring you to own a car.

Taxes are why Minneapolis falls behind Pittsburgh for a $120,000 earner. Minnesota is progressive rather than flat. For tax year 2026, single-filer rates are **5.35%, 6.80%, 7.85%, and 9.85%**, with the **7.85% bracket beginning at $109,431 of Minnesota taxable income** under the state's announced 2026 thresholds. [[26]](https://www.revenue.state.mn.us/press-release/2025-12-16/minnesota-income-tax-brackets-standard-deduction-and-dependent-exemption) Your gross salary alone does not determine your marginal bracket because deductions affect taxable income, but this is unmistakably a relatively high-tax state. You would probably save on state income tax versus your California situation, but the improvement is less dramatic than moving to a low-tax state.

And then there is winter. This is the one factor I would not rationalize away. NOAA's official climate-normal system uses 1991–2020 observations to represent typical conditions, and Minneapolis/St. Paul normally receives substantial snow through the winter season. [[27]](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals) January alone averages about **11 inches of snowfall**, February another **9.5 inches**, March **8.2 inches**, and April still averages about **3.5 inches** at MSP. [[28]](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&endDate=9996-12-31&format=pdf&startDate=0001-01-01&stations=USW00014922) Beyond snowfall, prolonged subfreezing weather and very short winter days are a genuine adaptation for someone moving from SF.

The counterargument is that Minneapolis is built around that reality better than many cities with milder winters: trails and parks are not simply summer amenities, and the park system specifically maintains parts of its pedestrian/bike network in winter. [[29]](https://www.minneapolisparks.org/parks-destinations/parks-lakes/cedar_lake_park/) Whether that sounds appealing or miserable is personal, but I would visit for a week in January before signing a year lease.

Census QuickFacts reports Minneapolis at roughly **18.8% Black alone, 5.3% Asian alone, 9.9% two or more races, and 60.3% White alone**. [[30]](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/minneapoliscityminnesota) It is a diverse city, though Philadelphia has the broadest racial mix of the three finalists.

A remote SF schedule is somewhat easier here than in Pennsylvania. Minneapolis is on Central Time, so a strict **9–5 Pacific workday becomes 11 a.m.–7 p.m. local**, leaving you usable mornings while preserving more evening than the Pennsylvania options.

**My Minneapolis neighborhood target:** **Lyn-Lake or Lowry Hill East**, leaning toward the latter if easy access to the lakes matters more than being directly downtown. The citywide walk/bike numbers are strong enough that you do not have to choose between “walkable” and “affordable” as sharply as you do in many lower-cost U.S. metros. [[31]](https://www.walkscore.com/MN/Minneapolis)

## Philadelphia, Pennsylvania — best if you really do not want a car

Philadelphia is the least cheap of my three finalists on the broad cost index, but it is the most convincing choice if “I really don't want to be stuck in a car” is the priority you care about most.

C2ER places Philadelphia at **107.2 versus San Francisco's 163.7**, about **34.5% lower overall**—enough to clear your threshold, but with less margin than Pittsburgh or Minneapolis. Its standardized apartment is **$2,147**, around **43.9% below SF**, while the healthcare index is **104.5 versus SF's 124.3**, only about **15.9% lower**. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388)

Interestingly, current rental-market data look even better: Zillow's October 2026 citywide average is approximately **$1,650**, versus $4,745 in SF, a roughly **65.2% nominal difference**. [[32]](https://www.zillow.com/rental-manager/market-trends/philadelphia-pa/) The discrepancy with C2ER is a good reminder that these measures use different apartment inventories and methodologies; I would trust their shared conclusion—Philadelphia housing is dramatically cheaper than SF—more than any one precise percentage.

The C2ER purchasing-power equivalent works out to about **$78,600 in Philadelphia for $120,000 of SF consumer costs**, before taxes. That is the least dramatic improvement among my finalists, but it remains substantial. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388)

Philadelphia decisively wins the walkability contest. Its citywide scores are **Walk 75, Transit 67 and Bike 67**, making it the strongest all-around non-car city of these three. [[33]](https://www.walkscore.com/PA/Philadelphia) **Center City West has a Walk Score of 99.** [[34]](https://www.walkscore.com/score/loc/lat%3D39.950818/lng%3D-75.168439/) In practical terms, this is the option where selling your car rather than merely “using it less” is most realistic.

Philadelphia also disproves the idea that a dense East Coast city cannot meet your nature requirement. Philadelphia Parks & Recreation manages **more than 10,200 acres of public land and waterways**. [[35]](https://www.phila.gov/departments/philadelphia-parks-recreation/) **Wissahickon Valley Park has roughly 1,800 acres of woodlands, meadows and streams and more than 50 miles of scenic trails**, while the broader Philadelphia parks system has well over 100 miles of trails. [[36]](https://www.phila.gov/2019-10-29-friends-of-wissahickon-group-celebrates-95-years-of-activism/) For somebody who wants urban life during the week but a wooded trail without organizing a full road trip, that is unusually good.

Internet is likewise straightforward. Verizon says its **100% fiber-optic Fios network is available across the Philadelphia area**, subject to an individual address check. [[37]](https://www.verizon.com/home/internet/near-me/philadelphia-pennsylvania/) Current provider data show multi-gig Fios and cable options in Philadelphia, so finding a building suitable for full-time software work should not be difficult so long as you verify the unit before committing. [[38]](https://www.allconnect.com/local/pa/philadelphia/zip-19151)

Airport access is a major advantage. Philadelphia International Airport is connected directly to Center City by SEPTA's Airport Regional Rail Line, which the airport says operates **every 30 minutes**. [[39]](https://www.phl.org/faqs) That makes your 90-minute requirement a non-issue even without owning a car.

The tax picture is more complicated. Pennsylvania charges the same **3.07% state personal income tax** as in Pittsburgh, but a Philadelphia resident also pays a **3.735% Wage/Earnings Tax** as of July 1, 2026. [[40]](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/revenue/resources/tax-types-and-information/personal-income-tax) A simple addition gives a nominal headline of about **6.805%** across the two rates, although, again, the exact taxable bases and credits matter. Philadelphia therefore does not give you as large a tax advantage over California as Pennsylvania's low state rate initially suggests.

Weather is, on balance, the easiest of these three for a Californian to adapt to, though summer humidity may be the bigger surprise. Official 1991–2020 NWS normals for Philadelphia show a **January normal high of 41.3°F** and a **July normal high of 87.8°F with a 69.6°F normal low**. [[41]](https://www.weather.gov/phi/clinormalsKPHL) Winters are much milder than Minneapolis and somewhat milder than Pittsburgh; summers are hotter and significantly more humid than SF.

Philadelphia is also my clear diversity winner. Census QuickFacts gives selected race shares of approximately **39.4% Black alone, 35.1% White alone, and 8.0% Asian alone**, a substantially more mixed racial composition than either Pittsburgh or Minneapolis on those Census categories. [[42]](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/philadelphiacitypennsylvania) Of the three, it is the closest replacement for San Francisco in the sense of being part of a dense, globally connected, culturally varied urban environment—while still costing much less.

The main hidden lifestyle downside for you is again time zone. A Pacific-hours job can mean working **noon to 8 p.m. Eastern**. Philadelphia has more evening amenities than Pittsburgh, but that does not help much when your last recurring team meeting ends at 7:30 p.m.

**My Philadelphia neighborhood strategy:** choose **Center City West** if car-free living is paramount, and use transit to reach the larger trail systems. If nature eventually outranks maximum downtown walkability, I would shift the apartment search northwest toward neighborhoods closer to Wissahickon, but I would verify walkability and fiber building by building rather than assuming all of northwest Philadelphia works equally well.

## Taxes, healthcare, and the relocation details I would not ignore

Your California tax situation deserves more attention than simply comparing posted state rates. The California Franchise Tax Board states that once someone is genuinely a **California nonresident and performs all services outside California, those wages are generally not California-source income**. [[50]](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/personal/residency-status/part-year-and-nonresident.html) In other words, the fact that your employer remains in California does not by itself make your post-move remote salary California-taxable; where the services are actually performed matters. FTB separately confirms that wages for services physically performed in California can create California-source income. [[51]](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/about-ftb/newsroom/covid-19/help-with-covid-19.html)

The major software-engineer trap is **equity compensation**. California explicitly warns that deferred or equity-based compensation can remain California-source after a move. For restricted stock, for example, FTB describes allocating compensation according to California versus non-California workdays over the relevant vesting period; its published example makes 70% of restricted-stock compensation California-taxable when 70% of the underlying workdays occurred in California. [[52]](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/personal/residency-status/part-year-and-nonresident.html) If you have unvested RSUs, options or substantial deferred comp, the tax savings from moving are real but the first few post-move tax returns can be considerably more complicated than “I left California, therefore California gets zero.”

That makes the three destination tax regimes look roughly like this: Pittsburgh gives you Pennsylvania's **3.07%** state rate plus Pittsburgh's **3% resident EIT**; Philadelphia gives you 3.07% plus a **3.735% resident wage tax**; Minnesota uses progressive rates from **5.35% to 9.85%**. [[53]](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/revenue/resources/tax-types-and-information/personal-income-tax) For a $120,000 salaried employee, Pittsburgh therefore has the most attractive simple rate structure of my finalists; Philadelphia gives back some of Pennsylvania's low-state-tax advantage through its city tax; Minneapolis is substantially higher-tax than either Pennsylvania option.

Healthcare deserves a similarly careful reading. C2ER's healthcare category is a **consumer-price component of its cost-of-living basket**, not a guarantee that your employer-sponsored insurance premium will drop by the same percentage. C2ER's broader methodology measures price differences in a standardized basket of goods and services and explicitly separates healthcare as one of its six major categories. [[1]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388) Your actual premium may largely follow your employer's benefits plan rather than your ZIP code, while deductibles, provider prices and out-of-pocket costs may respond more directly to the local market.

If your original requirement literally meant **“healthcare must itself be ≥30% cheaper than San Francisco,”** my top three fail that one test. The best alternative I found is **Columbus, Ohio**: C2ER puts Columbus at **93.0 overall versus SF's 163.7**—about 43% cheaper—and its healthcare index at **80.8 versus SF's 124.3**, approximately **35% lower**. Its standardized apartment is about **$1,468**. [[2]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388) The reason Columbus does not replace one of my top three is transportation: the city as a whole is much more car-oriented, even though pockets such as **Short North have a Walk Score of 95 and German Village 90**. [[54]](https://www.walkscore.com/OH/Columbus/Short_North) In other words, Columbus satisfies the strict healthcare interpretation better, but at a meaningful cost to your “I don't want to be stuck in a car” requirement.

My final ranking therefore reflects what I think you actually asked for: **a ≥30% reduction in total cost of living, with rent and healthcare scrutinized particularly closely, rather than a requirement that every individual spending category fall by 30%.**

For your specific profile—**$120,000 remote salary, leaving SF primarily because of cost, strong dislike of car dependence, technical need for dependable broadband, and desire for real nature access**—I would apartment-hunt in this order:

**Pittsburgh first:** Shadyside and Bloomfield. The savings are enormous, the walkability in those neighborhoods is 90+, nature is unusually accessible, taxes are comparatively favorable, and winter is much easier than Minnesota. [[55]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388)

**Minneapolis second:** Lyn-Lake/Lowry Hill East and the Chain-of-Lakes side of town. It is actually the best statistical performer on broad affordability, parks, biking, broadband and airport connectivity; I rank it second solely because its winter and Minnesota's income-tax rates are sufficiently large quality-of-life/financial trade-offs that they should not be hidden inside a spreadsheet. [[56]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388)

**Philadelphia third:** Center City West or another transit-rich central neighborhood. Choose it over the first two when **“I want to function without a car and still have the amenities of a major city”** outweighs maximizing cost reduction. It clears your 30% overall threshold, crushes SF on rent, has the best transit/walkability and diversity, and gives you excellent urban nature—but it offers the smallest overall COL cushion, the weakest healthcare-cost improvement, and a significant city wage tax. [[57]](https://www.grpva.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GRP_MediaKit_2026_web.pdf?t=1781886388)

On the evidence, **Pittsburgh is the best “save a lot without giving up your lifestyle” move; Minneapolis is the best “build a higher-quality daily life with excellent infrastructure” move; Philadelphia is the best “keep a real big-city, car-free lifestyle for far less than SF” move.**
