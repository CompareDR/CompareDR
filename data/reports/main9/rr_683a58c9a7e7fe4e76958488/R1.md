# A Biologically Plausible Engram–Predictive Coding Spiking Network for Classification and Associative Memory

## Executive summary

This report proposes a unified **Engram–Predictive Coding Spiking Network (EPC-SNN)** in which the same neural system performs perceptual inference, classification, episodic memory formation, pattern completion, and systems-level consolidation. The central hypothesis is that **predictive coding and engram dynamics are complementary rather than competing descriptions of neural computation**: predictive coding determines *what is unexpected and therefore worth learning*, while engram mechanisms determine *where and how those experiences are rapidly allocated, stabilized, and subsequently reinstated*. This synthesis is motivated by converging work showing that cortical prediction can be described through reciprocal prediction/error signaling, that hippocampal recurrent circuitry can support associative completion, that neuronal excitability biases engram allocation, and that hippocampal–cortical replay contributes to consolidation. [[1]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10195184/)

The proposed network has two coupled learning systems. A **slow cortex-like hierarchy** contains spiking representation/prediction neurons and explicit positive and negative prediction-error populations, with error gain controlled by a precision signal. A **fast hippocampus-like module** contains a dentate-gyrus-like sparse expansion layer, a recurrent CA3-like attractor network in which sparse engrams are rapidly allocated, and a CA1-like comparator/interface population. This division follows the computational logic of complementary learning systems: hippocampal circuitry learns individual episodes rapidly while neocortical weights change more slowly and integrate structure across experiences. [[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7624455/)

Engram formation is triggered not simply by stimulus presentation, but by **precision-weighted prediction error**. Neurons with temporarily elevated intrinsic excitability have a greater probability of entering the engram, matching experimental evidence that relative excitability and CREB-linked mechanisms bias memory allocation. Recurrent synapses among allocated CA3-like neurons then undergo rapid local Hebbian/STDP plasticity, producing an attractor that can later complete a stored representation from degraded or partial cues. [[3]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17446403/)

Learning is deliberately restricted to mechanisms that have plausible biological analogues: conductance-based leaky-integrate-and-fire neurons, sparse firing, lateral inhibition, spike-timing-dependent eligibility traces, local Hebbian prediction-error learning, homeostatic plasticity, and neuromodulatory third-factor gating. STDP has strong experimental foundations, voltage-dependent STDP models reproduce a broad range of observed plasticity phenomena, and dopamine-modulated eligibility traces provide a concrete mechanism for delayed global reinforcement signals. [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9852584/)

The model should first be evaluated on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, followed by CIFAR-10 as a scaling challenge. **No numerical results below are presented as already obtained.** The tables under “Expected results” specify falsifiable hypotheses and acceptance criteria. A useful empirical anchor is Diehl and Cook's unsupervised, STDP-driven SNN, which obtained approximately 95% MNIST classification accuracy using conductance-based neurons, lateral inhibition, adaptive thresholds, and local plasticity. [[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26941637/)

The strongest prediction of the EPC-SNN is not necessarily a higher clean-data classification score than conventional networks. Its scientific value would instead be demonstrated by a particular **phenotype of behavior**: comparable clean classification, substantially superior partial-cue and occlusion robustness, one/few-shot episodic recall, sparse memory allocation, recoverable attractors, prediction-error-sensitive memory updating, and a progressive reduction in hippocampal dependence after offline replay. These properties directly connect the machine-learning evaluation to experimentally measurable neuroscience phenomena. [[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26982728/)

A review of the literature suggests that the proposal is genuinely integrative rather than a relabeling of an established model. Recurrent predictive-coding models have already demonstrated associative-memory behavior and biologically plausible recurrent covariance learning; predictive-coding accounts have explicitly linked hippocampal prediction with memory; and spiking/STDP networks have demonstrated classification. However, I did not find a peer-reviewed model that simultaneously combines **explicit cellular engram allocation, spiking hierarchical prediction/error populations, precision weighting, CA3-like attractor completion, hippocampal–cortical consolidation, and supervised classification in one experimentally testable network**. That conclusion is an inference from the currently available literature rather than proof of absence. [[7]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7305946/)

## Scientific rationale and theoretical synthesis

### Why engrams and predictive coding fit together

The modern experimental engram concept is considerably more specific than the generic claim that “memories are stored in neurons.” Optogenetic experiments showed that hippocampal neuronal ensembles activated during fear learning can later drive memory expression when artificially reactivated, establishing that appropriately tagged ensembles can be sufficient for recall. Subsequent work showed that neurons with elevated CREB or intrinsic excitability are preferentially recruited, that perturbing selected ensembles can selectively alter memories, and that synapse-specific plasticity can preserve the identity of different memories even when their cellular ensembles overlap. [[8]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22441246/)

Engrams therefore imply at least four computational operations:

- **Allocation:** only a sparse subset of eligible cells should become strongly associated with an episode.

- **Association:** synapses within and between allocated ensembles should change so that the episode can later be reconstructed.

- **Completion:** partial cues should reinstate much of the original population state.

- **Consolidation:** rapidly acquired episodic information should gradually influence distributed cortical representations.

The first two have direct experimental analogues in excitability-biased recruitment and synapse-specific plasticity; the third has strong support from CA3 manipulations; and the fourth is supported by systems-consolidation and hippocampal–cortical replay experiments. [[9]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25102562/)

Predictive coding supplies a complementary computational problem: how hierarchical neural systems infer hidden causes of sensory input. In the Rao–Ballard formulation, higher cortical levels generate predictions of activity in lower levels, and the residual discrepancy is transmitted upward. Friston subsequently formalized related dynamics in terms of minimizing variational free energy or precision-weighted prediction errors. Canonical-microcircuit proposals map ascending prediction errors preferentially to superficial populations and descending predictions to deeper cortical populations, although the precise biological mapping remains an active empirical question rather than an established one-to-one correspondence. [[10]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10195184/)

Importantly, prediction-error-like signals are not merely theoretical. Auditory-cortex experiments have identified neurons responding selectively to particular violations of learned sensorimotor expectations, and recent mouse experiments identified a thalamocortical disinhibitory circuit required for generating sensory prediction-error responses. Such findings support the general existence of circuit-level mismatch computations, while not establishing that every cortical neuron implements a specific textbook predictive-coding variable. [[11]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37699716/)

A direct theoretical bridge already exists. Barron, Auksztulewicz, and Friston proposed that hippocampal–neocortical interactions can alternate between prediction and memory modes by changing the gain, or precision, assigned to neocortical prediction errors; in their framework, hippocampally driven recall can even produce internally generated or “fictive” prediction-error-like teaching signals in the absence of sensory input. More recently, Tang and colleagues demonstrated recurrent predictive-coding networks capable of associative memory using recurrent connections intended to capture an important aspect of CA3 architecture. [[12]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7305946/)

The EPC-SNN takes the next conceptual step:

$$
\boxed{\text{Engram allocation}  \approx  \text{rapid storage of high-precision residual information}}
$$

while

$$
\boxed{\text{predictive cortical learning}  \approx  \text{slow assimilation of recurrently experienced residual structure}.}
$$

An expected stimulus is already well explained by the cortical generative model and therefore produces small error. A surprising or novel stimulus produces larger precision-weighted error and temporarily opens a plasticity gate. The hippocampal network rapidly stores the resulting state as a sparse attractor. During subsequent replay, that attractor becomes an internally generated training sample for the cortical hierarchy.

This provides a computational answer to an important question that engram theory alone does not solve: **why should a particular episode be strongly encoded now rather than treated as another repetition of already known structure?** Prediction error provides the novelty/surprise signal; excitability-dependent competition provides cellular allocation.

### Sparse coding, pattern separation, and attractor completion

Sparse coding is attractive for both sides of the theory. Olshausen and Field showed that optimizing sparse representations of natural images can generate localized, oriented receptive-field-like basis functions, demonstrating that sparse unsupervised coding can capture important statistics of sensory input. [[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8637596/)

Within hippocampal computation, dentate gyrus and CA3 are often assigned complementary roles in pattern separation and associative completion. Physiological recordings support decorrelation of similar inputs in DG/CA3 circuitry, while CA3-specific NMDA-receptor disruption produces particularly strong impairments when animals must retrieve learned spatial information from incomplete rather than complete cues. [[14]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17303747/)

The EPC-SNN therefore does **not** make the hippocampal representation simply another dense hidden layer. It first expands high-level cortical activity into a sparse DG-like representation and then compresses/selects it into a sparse CA3 ensemble. This separation between a cortical semantic code and a hippocampal episodic code is central to the architecture.

### Fast and slow memory systems

The classic complementary-learning-systems argument predicts that a fast-learning hippocampal system can encode new episodes without catastrophically modifying the structured knowledge already stored in cortex, whereas gradual interleaved cortical learning extracts statistical regularities over many experiences. [[15]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7624455/)

The relevant physiology has moved beyond the purely theoretical level. Hippocampal and cortical representations can replay related experience during sleep, and experimentally manipulating the temporal coordination of hippocampal sharp-wave ripples, cortical delta events, and spindles can alter subsequent memory and cortical reorganization. Systems-engram experiments likewise suggest that cortical engram populations can exist early but become progressively capable of supporting natural recall over time. [[16]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17173043/)

The model therefore uses:

$$
{ \eta }_{H} \gg { \eta }_{C},
$$

where ${ \eta }_{H}$ is hippocampal learning rate and ${ \eta }_{C}$ is cortical learning rate, combined with offline replay rather than direct copying of synaptic matrices.

## Proposed network architecture

### Functional organization

The proposed system contains five functional components.

| Component | Computational role | Biological analogy | Dominant plasticity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sensory encoding | Converts pixels/features into spikes | retina/LGN/sensory afferents | fixed or slow normalization |
| Cortical predictive hierarchy | Learns latent causes and top-down predictions | hierarchical sensory cortex | slow error-gated STDP/Hebbian plasticity |
| Precision circuit | Controls gain of prediction-error populations | attention/neuromodulatory + inhibitory gain control | slow adaptation |
| Episodic attractor module | Rapid allocation, storage, pattern completion | DG–CA3–CA1-like circuit | fast STDP, excitability-dependent allocation |
| Classification readout | Maps semantic/episodic state onto class decision | downstream decision/action circuitry | reward/label-modulated eligibility traces |

The cortical mapping is intentionally functional rather than asserting that particular laminae correspond uniquely to abstract variables. Canonical predictive-coding microcircuit models provide a useful anatomical scaffold, but the specific neuronal realization remains unsettled. [[17]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23177956/)

### Episodic versus semantic representations

Let ${c}^{(L)}$ denote the top cortical latent representation and $h$ the CA3 population.

The cortex should learn approximately category- or feature-level regularities:

$$
{c}^{(L)}({x}_{a}) \approx {c}^{(L)}({x}_{b})
$$

for two different exemplars ${x}_{a},{x}_{b}$ of the same statistical structure.

The hippocampal module instead receives a sparsified conjunctive code:

$$
h({x}_{a}) \not\approx h({x}_{b})
$$

unless the two experiences are intentionally linked or encoded within an overlapping excitability window.

This distinction permits the classifier to generalize through cortical representations while preserving exemplar-specific memory in CA3.

Experiments showing that experiences acquired close together can share neuronal ensembles motivate adding a slowly decaying excitability trace. [[18]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251287/)

## Mathematical formulation and local learning rules

### Spiking neuron dynamics

For computational tractability, the main implementation should use conductance-based leaky integrate-and-fire neurons:

$$
{C}_{m}\frac{d{V}_{i}}{dt}= - {g}_{L}({V}_{i} - {E}_{L}) - {g}_{i}^{E}({V}_{i} - {E}_{E}) - {g}_{i}^{I}({V}_{i} - {E}_{I})+{I}_{i}^{bias}+{I}_{i}^{mod}.
$$

A spike is emitted when

$$
{V}_{i}(t) \geq {V}_{th},
$$

after which

$$
{V}_{i} \leftarrow {V}_{reset}
$$

for an absolute refractory period ${ \tau }_{ref}$.

Synaptic conductances obey

$$
{ \tau }_{E}{\dot{g}}_{i}^{E}= - {g}_{i}^{E}+\sum_{j}^{​} {{w}_{ij}^{E}}{s}_{j}(t)
$$

and

$$
{ \tau }_{I}{\dot{g}}_{i}^{I}= - {g}_{i}^{I}+\sum_{k}^{​} {{w}_{ik}^{I}}{s}_{k}(t),
$$

where

$$
{s}_{j}(t)=\sum_{f}^{​} { \delta }(t - {t}_{j}^{f}).
$$

Filtered spike trains define the continuously varying population variables used by predictive inference:

$$
{ \tau }_{r}{\dot{r}}_{i}= - {r}_{i}+{s}_{i}(t).
$$

This maintains an explicitly spike-based implementation while allowing prediction errors to be interpreted on neuronal timescales rather than between mathematically instantaneous spikes. Balanced spiking networks have previously been shown capable of implementing predictive representations of dynamical variables, supporting the feasibility of expressing predictive computations through LIF populations. [[19]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24244113/)

### Hierarchical predictive objective

For cortical level $ \ell $, let

$$
{\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}}_{ \ell  - 1}={f}_{ \ell }\left( {W}_{ \ell }{\boldsymbol{r}}_{ \ell } \right)
$$

be the top-down prediction of the lower-level representation.

The residual is

$$
{\boldsymbol{ \epsilon }}_{ \ell  - 1}={\boldsymbol{r}}_{ \ell  - 1} - {\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}}_{ \ell  - 1}.
$$

Introduce a diagonal or locally structured precision matrix

$$
{ \Pi }_{ \ell  - 1}=diag({ \pi }_{ \ell  - 1,1}, \ldots ,{ \pi }_{ \ell  - 1,n}).
$$

The instantaneous predictive objective is

$$
{\mathcal{F}}_{PC}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ \ell =1}^{L} {{\boldsymbol{ \epsilon }}_{ \ell  - 1}^{T}}{ \Pi }_{ \ell  - 1}{\boldsymbol{ \epsilon }}_{ \ell  - 1}+{ \lambda }_{s}\sum_{ \ell }^{​} { \parallel }{\boldsymbol{r}}_{ \ell }{ \parallel }_{1}.
$$

The final term implements sparse coding.

Precision-weighted error is

$$
{\boldsymbol{ \xi }}_{ \ell }={ \Pi }_{ \ell }{\boldsymbol{ \epsilon }}_{ \ell }.
$$

Precision can therefore change inference without changing the sensory discrepancy itself. This follows the distinction between prediction error and the confidence/gain assigned to that error in predictive-processing models. Experimental work manipulating attention and prediction is compatible with such gain weighting, while acetylcholine has been shown to influence auditory mismatch responses, making neuromodulation of precision a biologically testable rather than purely mathematical assumption. [[20]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22047964/)

### Predictive inference dynamics

An idealized rate-level inference rule is

$$
{ \tau }_{c}{\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}}_{ \ell }= - {\boldsymbol{r}}_{ \ell }+ \phi \left[ {B}_{ \ell }{\boldsymbol{ \xi }}_{ \ell  - 1} - {\boldsymbol{ \xi }}_{ \ell } - { \lambda }_{s}+{U}_{ \ell }\boldsymbol{h} - {I}_{ \ell }^{inh} \right].
$$

Here:

- ${B}_{ \ell }$ transports lower-level error upward;

- $ - {\boldsymbol{ \xi }}_{ \ell }$ represents unexplained error at the current level;

- ${U}_{ \ell }\boldsymbol{h}$ allows hippocampal retrieval to bias cortical inference;

- ${I}_{ \ell }^{inh}$ implements competition/sparsity.

In the analytically simplest predictive-coding derivation one often obtains a transpose ${W}_{ \ell }^{T}$, but exact weight symmetry is neurobiologically problematic. The simulation should therefore contain distinct ${B}_{ \ell }$ and ${W}_{ \ell }$ matrices and examine whether reciprocal local plasticity produces useful alignment. Predictive-coding networks can approximate efficient supervised credit assignment using local Hebbian plasticity under appropriate conditions, but this should be treated as a computational result rather than proof that cortex implements backpropagation. [[21]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28333583/)

### Local cortical synaptic learning

At the population level, the generative connection can use the local predictive update

$$
 \Delta {W}_{ \ell ,ij}={ \eta }_{C} { \xi }_{ \ell  - 1,i} {r}_{ \ell ,j}.
$$

The update depends only on a presynaptic representation ${r}_{ \ell ,j}$, the postsynaptic/local error ${ \xi }_{ \ell  - 1,i}$, and the current synaptic strength.

A spike-level implementation uses eligibility traces. Define presynaptic and postsynaptic traces:

$$
{ \tau }_{+}{\dot{x}}_{i}= - {x}_{i}+{s}_{i}^{pre},
$$

$$
{ \tau }_{ - }{\dot{y}}_{j}= - {y}_{j}+{s}_{j}^{post}.
$$

An STDP eligibility variable becomes

$$
{\dot{e}}_{ij}= - \frac{{e}_{ij}}{{ \tau }_{e}}+{A}_{+}{x}_{i}{s}_{j} - {A}_{ - }{y}_{j}{s}_{i}.
$$

Actual plasticity is then

$$
\boxed{{\dot{w}}_{ij}= \eta  {m}_{j}(t) {e}_{ij}(t)}
$$

where ${m}_{j}(t)$ is a third factor.

For cortical predictive synapses,

$$
{m}_{j}(t) \approx { \xi }_{j}(t).
$$

For supervised readout synapses,

$$
m(t) \approx R(t) - \bar{R}(t),
$$

with $R$ a class/reward signal.

For hippocampal encoding,

$$
m(t) \approx {g}_{novelty}(t) {g}_{allocation}(t).
$$

Three-factor plasticity of this general type is supported conceptually by dopamine-modulated STDP and eligibility-trace models. [[22]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17220510/)

### Engram allocation

Each hippocampal candidate neuron $i$ has a dynamic excitability variable ${q}_{i}$:

$$
{ \tau }_{q}{\dot{q}}_{i}= - ({q}_{i} - {q}_{0})+{ \kappa }_{q}{r}_{i}+{ \sigma }_{q}{ \eta }_{i}(t).
$$

During a novel episode, its allocation score is

$$
{a}_{i}= \alpha {I}_{i}^{DG}+ \beta {q}_{i}+ \gamma { \zeta }_{i},
$$

where ${ \zeta }_{i}$ represents small stochastic variability.

Only the top fraction ${ \rho }_{H}$ becomes strongly plastic:

$$
{z}_{i}^{engram}=\left\{ \begin{matrix}
1, & {a}_{i}\text{ among top }{ \rho }_{H} \\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{matrix} \right.
$$

The crucial biological term is $ \beta {q}_{i}$. CREB manipulations and direct excitability manipulations both indicate that comparatively excitable neurons are preferentially recruited into a memory trace. [[23]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17446403/)

A sensible starting range is

$$
{ \rho }_{H} \in [0.02,0.10],
$$

with sparsity treated as an experimentally varied parameter rather than hard-coded as a presumed biological constant.

### Prediction-error-dependent encoding gate

Define global novelty as

$$
S(t)=\sum_{ \ell }^{​} {\frac{1}{{N}_{ \ell }}} \parallel {\boldsymbol{ \xi }}_{ \ell }(t){ \parallel }_{1}.
$$

The plasticity gate is

$$
{g}_{novelty}= \sigma \left[ { \alpha }_{S}(S - { \theta }_{S}) \right].
$$

Thus an episode can strongly activate sensory neurons while producing little new episodic storage if it is accurately predicted.

This mechanism yields a clear distinction:

$$
\text{sensory activity} \neq \text{memory-writing signal}.
$$

That separation is also consistent with human evidence that mnemonic prediction errors can destabilize ongoing hippocampal representations and promote memory updating rather than simply producing a generic increase in encoding. [[24]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2117625118)

### CA3 recurrent engram learning

Rapid recurrent plasticity is

$$
 \Delta {J}_{ij}^{H}={ \eta }_{H}{g}_{novelty}{z}_{i}^{engram}{z}_{j}^{engram} - { \eta }_{dep}{D}_{ij},
$$

with

$$
{ \eta }_{H} \gg { \eta }_{C}.
$$

A spike-timing implementation substitutes the pair product with the STDP eligibility term.

Homeostatic normalization prevents runaway excitation:

$$
{w}_{ij} \leftarrow \frac{{w}_{ij}}{ \epsilon +\sum_{j}^{​} {|}{w}_{ij}|}{W}_{budget}.
$$

At retrieval, a partial DG cue initializes the state:

$$
\boldsymbol{h}(0)={\boldsymbol{h}}_{cue}.
$$

The recurrent dynamics then follow approximately

$$
{ \tau }_{H}\dot{\boldsymbol{h}}= - \boldsymbol{h}+ \phi \left( {J}_{H}\boldsymbol{h}+{W}_{DG \rightarrow H}\boldsymbol{d} - {I}_{inh}^{H} \right).
$$

A successful recall satisfies

$$
\boldsymbol{h}(t \rightarrow  \infty ) \rightarrow {\boldsymbol{h}}^{ \mu },
$$

where ${\boldsymbol{h}}^{ \mu }$ is the stored attractor for memory $ \mu $.

CA3-specific NMDA-receptor disruption particularly impairs associative recall under partial-cue conditions, giving this mechanism a direct experimental counterpart. [[25]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12040087/)

### Supervised classification readout

The core representational hierarchy is trained without labels. Labels are restricted to the final decision pathway.

Let class neuron $k$ receive

$$
{I}_{k}^{out}=\sum_{i}^{​} {{W}_{ki}^{C}}{r}_{i}^{(L)}+{ \lambda }_{H}\sum_{j}^{​} {{W}_{kj}^{H}}{h}_{j}.
$$

Strong lateral inhibition implements a winner-take-all decision.

During labeled training, the correct output receives a teacher/reward burst. Eligibility-trace plasticity gives

$$
 \Delta {W}_{ki}={ \eta }_{R} \delta {R}_{k}{e}_{ki},
$$

where

$$
 \delta {R}_{k}=\left\{ \begin{matrix}
+1, & k=y \\
 - { \beta }_{R}, & k \neq y\text{ and incorrectly active} \\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{matrix} \right.
$$

This confines explicit supervision to downstream decision synapses rather than propagating a class error through the full hierarchy.

### Hippocampal–cortical consolidation

During an offline phase, stored attractors are reactivated:

$$
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{ \mu } \rightarrow U{\boldsymbol{h}}^{ \mu } \rightarrow {\boldsymbol{r}}_{L}^{ \mu } \rightarrow {\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}}_{L - 1}^{ \mu } \rightarrow  \cdots .
$$

These internally generated states drive local prediction errors:

$$
{ \epsilon }_{ \ell }^{replay}={r}_{ \ell }^{reinstated} - {\hat{r}}_{ \ell }.
$$

Cortical synapses undergo the same local rule used in waking learning:

$$
 \Delta {W}_{ \ell }={ \eta }_{replay}{ \xi }_{ \ell  - 1}^{replay}{r}_{ \ell }^{T}.
$$

No separate “copy hippocampal weights to cortex” operation is required.

This mechanism follows two convergent ideas: hippocampal replay can provide cortical training events, and a predictive-coding account can interpret internally reinstated activity as a teaching signal for a generative model. [[26]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17173043/)

### Complete online algorithm

for each input episode x:

    encode x into sensory spikes

    initialize cortical latent populations

    for t = 1 ... T_inference:
        generate top-down predictions at every cortical level
        compute positive and negative prediction-error spikes
        precision-weight the error populations
        update cortical latent populations by local recurrent dynamics

    compute novelty S from precision-weighted errors

    update slow cortical predictive synapses using local
    error × presynaptic-activity / STDP eligibility rules

    if S > encoding threshold:
        project high-level cortical state through DG-like sparse layer
        rank hippocampal neurons by sensory drive + intrinsic excitability
        allocate sparse CA3 engram
        rapidly strengthen eligible recurrent synapses
        bind cortical state to CA3 engram

    if a class label is available:
        generate classification response
        deliver class-dependent reinforcement signal
        update only readout eligibility traces


during offline consolidation:

    sample hippocampal attractor memories
    reactivate each attractor in an SWR-like burst
    reinstate the associated high-level cortical representation
    propagate predictions downward
    compute local cortical prediction errors
    update cortical weights slowly
    interleave recent and older attractors

## Simulation plan and experimental protocol

### Proof-of-concept network

The first simulation should emphasize mechanistic interpretability rather than maximizing benchmark accuracy.

| Element | MNIST/Fashion-MNIST starting configuration |
| --- | --- |
| Input neurons | 784 |
| Cortical C1 representation | 1,024 excitatory + 256 inhibitory |
| C1 signed-error populations | 2 × 784 |
| Cortical C2 representation | 512 excitatory + 128 inhibitory |
| C2 signed-error populations | 2 × 1,024 |
| Cortical C3 representation | 256 excitatory + 64 inhibitory |
| C3 signed-error populations | 2 × 512 |
| DG-like expansion | 1,024–2,048 neurons |
| CA3 recurrent population | 1,024 neurons |
| CA1/interface population | 256–512 neurons |
| Output | 10 competing neurons |
| Approximate total | 7,000–10,000 neurons |

The exact numbers are engineering choices, not proposed anatomical cell counts.

For biological plausibility, C1–C2 connections should preferably be **locally connected receptive fields without exact convolutional weight sharing**. Weight sharing is computationally convenient but has no straightforward synapse-by-synapse cortical analogue.

These should be treated as priors for systematic search, not fitted post hoc to obtain a preferred conclusion.

Conductance-based LIF neurons, adaptive thresholds, lateral inhibition, and STDP have already been jointly demonstrated in an unsupervised MNIST SNN, providing an important feasibility benchmark for this scale of experiment. [[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26941637/)

### Input coding

Three encodings should be compared.

**Rate coding** is the initial control: pixel intensity ${x}_{i} \in [0,1]$ produces Poisson spikes with

$$
{ \lambda }_{i}={ \lambda }_{min}+{x}_{i}({ \lambda }_{max} - { \lambda }_{min}),
$$

with ${ \lambda }_{max} \approx 100$ Hz.

**Latency coding** uses

$$
{t}_{i}={t}_{max} - {x}_{i}({t}_{max} - {t}_{min}),
$$

so strong inputs spike earlier.

**Hybrid population coding** uses a small group of neurons with overlapping intensity tuning.

Rate coding is easiest for model validation; latency coding is preferable as a later test of whether the network can exploit temporal information instead of relying predominantly on spike counts.

### Training schedule

The experimental sequence should separate four kinds of learning.

| Phase | Labels? | Hippocampal plasticity | Cortical plasticity | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Predictive pretraining | No | low/off | on | learn sensory statistics |
| Episodic exposure | No | fast/on | slow/on | create engrams |
| Readout learning | Yes | usually frozen | frozen or slow | classification |
| Offline consolidation | No external input | replay | on | hippocampal → cortical assimilation |

This separation is essential for interpretation. If all synapses and labels are simultaneously optimized, one cannot tell whether classification is being solved by predictive learning, episodic memory, or supervised shortcuts.

### Datasets

The recommended progression is:

**MNIST** for mechanistic debugging and direct comparison with established STDP-SNN work. Diehl and Cook provide a strong biologically motivated reference point at approximately 95% test accuracy. [[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26941637/)

**Fashion-MNIST** should follow because the same input dimensionality permits reuse of the architecture while increasing within-class visual variability.

**CIFAR-10** should be regarded as the main scalability challenge. For CIFAR-10, the architecture should use several retinotopic, locally connected spiking feature populations rather than flattening 3,072 input channels into one fully connected layer.

An event-camera dataset can subsequently test whether the temporal machinery is functionally important rather than cosmetic.

### Core experiments

#### Clean classification

Train predictive representations without class labels, freeze or strongly slow the hierarchy, and train only the final readout.

Primary metric:

$$
Accuracy=\frac{{N}_{correct}}{{N}_{test}}.
$$

Secondary metrics should include class-balanced accuracy, confusion matrices, spikes per inference, and decision latency.

#### Noise robustness

Evaluate independently with Gaussian noise,

$$
{x}^{′}=clip(x+\mathcal{N}(0,{ \sigma }^{2})),
$$

salt-and-pepper noise, random pixel deletion, and structured occlusion.

Recommended severities are

$$
 \sigma  \in {0,0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4}
$$

for normalized pixels and block occlusion of approximately

$$
10 \% ,25 \% ,40 \% ,60 \% .
$$

Report the area under the accuracy-versus-corruption curve rather than selecting only one favorable corruption level.

#### Pattern completion

Store an exemplar as an episodic engram, mask progressively larger fractions of its input, and quantify recovery.

Population similarity:

$$
{S}_{recall}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{retrieved} \cdot {\boldsymbol{h}}_{stored}}{ \parallel {\boldsymbol{h}}_{retrieved} \parallel  \parallel {\boldsymbol{h}}_{stored} \parallel }.
$$

For sparse binary engram membership, also report Jaccard similarity:

$$
J(A,B)=\frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}.
$$

Cue completeness should span 20–100%.

A particularly important comparison is *full cue versus partial cue* because CA3 manipulation experiments predict a disproportionately large deficit for partial-cue retrieval. [[25]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12040087/)

#### Episodic one-shot memory

Expose the hippocampal module to a previously unseen exemplar once.

Test:

- exact retrieval;

- retrieval after occlusion;

- retrieval after sensory noise;

- reconstruction of the high-level cortical representation;

- classification before and after retrieval.

This distinguishes rapid episodic memorization from ordinary multi-epoch representation learning.

#### Consolidation

Immediately after encoding, measure:

$$
P(\text{correct recall}∣\text{hippocampus intact})
$$

and

$$
P(\text{correct recall}∣\text{CA3 silenced}).
$$

Then perform progressively more replay cycles and repeat the virtual lesion.

Define a hippocampal-dependence index:

$$
{D}_{H}={A}_{intact} - {A}_{CA3 lesion}.
$$

The critical prediction is

$$
\frac{d{D}_{H}}{d{N}_{replay}}<0
$$

for sufficiently consolidated memories.

This mirrors the qualitative systems-consolidation prediction that cortical engrams become more competent with time/replay while hippocampal support changes. [[27]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28386011/)

#### Memory allocation

Measure the probability that a neuron joins a new engram as a function of its pre-training excitability:

$$
P({z}_{i}^{engram}=1∣{q}_{i}).
$$

The model should produce

$$
\frac{ \partial P}{ \partial {q}_{i}}>0.
$$

Then artificially increase or decrease ${q}_{i}$ in a random subset before learning.

This directly reproduces the logic of experimental memory-allocation manipulations. [[23]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17446403/)

#### Temporal memory linking

Present two episodes at delay $ \Delta t$.

Measure engram overlap

$$
O( \Delta t)=\frac{|{E}_{1} \cap {E}_{2}|}{\sqrt{|{E}_{1}||{E}_{2}|}}.
$$

The model predicts approximately

$$
O( \Delta t) \propto {e}^{ -  \Delta t/{ \tau }_{q}}
$$

above chance overlap, provided the first episode raises subsequent excitability.

Experiments showing increased overlap between memories formed close together motivate this test. [[28]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251287/)

### Essential ablations

At minimum, compare:

| Model | Predictive coding | Precision | Sparse allocation | CA3 recurrence | Consolidation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feedforward SNN | — | — | — | — | — |
| STDP-SNN baseline | — | — | partial | — | — |
| PC-only SNN | ✓ | ✓ | — | — | — |
| Engram-only SNN | — | — | ✓ | ✓ | optional |
| EPC without precision | ✓ | — | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| EPC without attractor recurrence | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — | ✓ |
| EPC without replay | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — |
| **Full EPC-SNN** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

The ablation study is more scientifically important than showing only full-model classification accuracy.

### Statistical protocol

Use at least 10 independent seeds on MNIST/Fashion-MNIST and at least 5 for computationally expensive CIFAR experiments.

For each primary comparison report:

$$
\text{mean}, 95 \% \text{ confidence interval},  \Delta \text{performance},
$$

together with a paired test across matched random seeds.

For corruption curves, compare areas under the curves. For multiple ablations, control family-wise comparisons, for example with Holm correction. Report all initialized seeds and all failed runs rather than discarding unstable network states.

For memory experiments, the unit of analysis should be both network seed and memory item, avoiding a misleading result based on thousands of memories generated by a single trained network.

### Quantitative acceptance criteria

A defensible success criterion would not require beating backpropagation on clean images. Instead, the following could be preregistered:

| Criterion | Proposed falsifiable threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Clean-data cost of biological mechanisms | full EPC accuracy no more than ~2–3 percentage points below matched PC-only model |
| Partial-cue advantage | statistically significant improvement over PC-only and recurrence-ablated EPC |
| Memory completion | monotonic degradation with cue removal, but significantly shallower than feedforward baseline |
| Precision benefit | larger corruption-AUC with precision than precision-ablated model |
| Sparse allocation | stable engram fraction within predefined 2–10% range without runaway recruitment |
| Excitability allocation | positive relation between pre-encoding ${q}_{i}$ and engram membership |
| Consolidation | hippocampal-lesion deficit decreases with replay cycles |
| Novelty gating | novel/mismatching stimuli induce more hippocampal plasticity than equally strong but predicted stimuli |

Failure of several of these predictions would undermine the proposed unification even if classification accuracy remained high.

### Why robustness should improve

A feedforward classifier computes roughly

$$
\hat{y}=f({x}_{corrupt}).
$$

The EPC-SNN instead performs iterative inference:

$$
{x}_{corrupt} \rightarrow  \epsilon  \rightarrow c \rightarrow \hat{x} \rightarrow { \epsilon }^{′} \rightarrow  \cdots ,
$$

with hippocampal retrieval adding

$$
{x}_{corrupt} \rightarrow {h}_{partial} \rightarrow {h}_{attractor} \rightarrow {c}_{reinstated}.
$$

Therefore the final class decision can depend on the **completed latent cause**, rather than directly on the damaged sensory pattern.

The prediction is strongest for structured occlusion and missing information. Gaussian noise can often be handled by statistical denoising alone, whereas missing components are precisely where an associative attractor is expected to add information. CA3 pattern-completion experiments make this distinction especially important. [[25]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12040087/)

### Expected error dynamics

For a familiar clean image:

$$
 \parallel  \xi (t) \parallel  \downarrow 
$$

rapidly over inference.

For an unexpected image:

$$
 \parallel  \xi (0){ \parallel }_{novel}> \parallel  \xi (0){ \parallel }_{familiar}.
$$

After learning/consolidation:

$$
 \parallel  \xi { \parallel }_{same episode} \downarrow .
$$

Thus learning should be visible not just as better classification but as reduced residual cortical activity.

The general expectation that predicted inputs can evoke reduced responses is central to predictive-coding theory and is consistent with experimental observations of expectation-dependent sensory suppression, although empirical results across paradigms are more heterogeneous than the simplest theory implies. [[31]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10195184/)

### Attractor analysis

A genuine engram attractor should be established dynamically rather than inferred merely from a decoder.

For memory $ \mu $, define basin size:

$$
{B}_{ \mu }=P\left( \boldsymbol{h}(T)={\boldsymbol{h}}^{ \mu }∣\boldsymbol{h}(0) \sim {\mathcal{C}}_{ \mu } \right),
$$

where ${\mathcal{C}}_{ \mu }$ contains corrupted versions of the memory.

Plot

$$
{B}_{ \mu }(p)
$$

against corruption fraction $p$.

A recurrent-ablation control should show a dramatic reduction in basin size.

A second analysis should estimate a state-space trajectory using PCA or another fixed projection:

$$
\boldsymbol{h}(0) \rightarrow \boldsymbol{h}(1) \rightarrow  \cdots  \rightarrow {\boldsymbol{h}}^{ \mu }.
$$

The critical observation would be convergence of different partial cues toward the same learned population state.

### Representational analysis

Cortex and hippocampus should show measurably different geometry.

For cortical C3:

$$
\text{similarity within class}>\text{similarity across class}.
$$

For CA3:

$$
\text{similarity between different exemplars} \approx \text{low},
$$

except for linked memories.

Following consolidation, cortical within-class clustering should increase while exemplar-specific CA3 states remain separable.

This provides a direct operational distinction between **semantic compression** and **episodic separation** rather than simply naming one layer “cortex” and another “hippocampus.”

## Biological plausibility and testable neuroscience predictions

### Where the model is strongly grounded

**Spiking communication** is explicit rather than obtained by converting a trained rate network afterward.

**Synaptic learning is local.** STDP-like changes require pre/post activity, local voltage/error state, and optionally a third modulatory factor. Timing-dependent synaptic modification has been observed experimentally, and voltage-dependent models can account for several nonlinear properties of plasticity. [[32]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9852584/)

**Engram allocation depends on excitability.** Increasing CREB or neuronal excitability can bias cells toward memory-trace recruitment, directly motivating the model's ${q}_{i}$ allocation variable. [[23]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17446403/)

**Engram representations are sparse and associative.** Direct manipulation of learning-activated ensembles can affect memory expression, and synaptic changes can distinguish memories even when ensembles overlap. [[33]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22441246/)

**CA3 recurrence is functionally consequential for incomplete-cue retrieval.** This is one of the most direct biological constraints on the proposed attractor mechanism. [[25]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12040087/)

**Fast–slow learning and replay have experimental support.** Hippocampal/cortical co-replay and causal manipulation of hippocampal–cortical oscillatory coordination support the inclusion of a separate offline consolidation regime. [[34]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17173043/)

### Where plausibility is more speculative

The identity of dedicated cortical prediction and prediction-error neurons is less settled than the existence of mismatch-sensitive responses. Stimulus-specific prediction-error neurons have been identified experimentally, and specific thalamocortical circuitry can be necessary for sensory mismatch signals, but those findings do not uniquely validate one canonical predictive-coding circuit implementation. [[11]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37699716/)

Likewise, treating a CA1-like population specifically as a predictive comparator is a **modeling hypothesis**, not an assertion that CA1 neurons universally encode a scalar prediction error.

Precision is represented here as modulation of neuronal gain. This is consistent with predictive-coding formulations and attention experiments, but the mapping of mathematical precision onto particular neuromodulators, interneurons, oscillations, or dendritic mechanisms is unlikely to be one-to-one. [[35]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22047964/)

### Prediction: surprise should determine engram recruitment beyond sensory activity

Consider two stimuli with matched sensory intensity.

One is highly expected:

$$
{S}_{expected} \approx 0.
$$

The other violates a learned prediction:

$$
{S}_{unexpected} \gg 0.
$$

The model predicts

$$
P(\text{new engram}∣\text{unexpected})>P(\text{new engram}∣\text{expected}),
$$

even when total sensory firing is matched.

This is more specific than simply predicting greater hippocampal activity for novelty. It predicts that **precision-weighted cortical mismatch should forecast which cells enter a new episodic engram**.

### Prediction: manipulating precision should change memory allocation

Holding raw sensory discrepancy constant,

$$
 \epsilon =\text{constant},
$$

but increasing precision

$$
 \Pi  \uparrow 
$$

gives

$$
 \xi = \Pi  \epsilon  \uparrow 
$$

and should increase hippocampal encoding probability.

Therefore an experimental manipulation that selectively alters mismatch gain should change engram recruitment even if mean sensory responses remain comparable.

Attention experiments and cholinergic modulation of auditory mismatch responses make such a prediction experimentally approachable, although the exact neuromodulator implementing precision remains open. [[20]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22047964/)

### Prediction: excitability and surprise should interact multiplicatively

Engram recruitment probability should approximately satisfy

$$
{P}_{i} \sim  \sigma \left(  \alpha {q}_{i}+ \beta S+ \gamma {q}_{i}S \right).
$$

The critical term is

$$
 \gamma >0.
$$

Thus high-excitability cells should receive the strongest recruitment advantage during high-surprise episodes.

At low prediction error, artificially increasing excitability should have less effect on memory allocation than during high prediction error.

This interaction is a new prediction emerging from combining predictive coding with excitability-based engram allocation. The excitability component itself is grounded in allocation experiments. [[23]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17446403/)

### Prediction: CA3 perturbation should selectively affect incomplete cues

The model predicts:

$$
 \Delta {A}_{lesion}(\text{partial cue}) \gg  \Delta {A}_{lesion}(\text{full cue}).
$$

A full cue can drive the cortical hierarchy sufficiently strongly to yield correct classification even with impaired CA3 recurrence. A highly occluded cue depends more heavily on attractor completion.

This closely parallels the phenotype of CA3 NMDA-receptor disruption in partial-cue spatial recall. [[25]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12040087/)

### Prediction: recently reactivated memories become temporarily easier to link

Because successful recall transiently increases the engram's excitability variable,

$$
{q}_{i}({t}_{recall}) \uparrow ,
$$

a second memory encoded soon afterward is more likely to recruit overlapping cells.

The prediction agrees qualitatively with evidence that engram excitability can transiently increase after reactivation and that nearby-in-time memories can occupy overlapping neuronal ensembles. [[36]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30551997/)

The EPC-SNN adds a further prediction: the effect should be strongest when the second event also generates substantial prediction error.

### Prediction: replay should transform cortical prediction errors before eliminating hippocampal dependence

During early replay,

$$
 \parallel { \epsilon }_{cortex}{ \parallel }_{replay}
$$

should initially be large because the cortex poorly predicts the reinstated hippocampal state.

Across consolidation,

$$
 \parallel { \epsilon }_{cortex}{ \parallel }_{replay} \downarrow .
$$

Only after this decline should virtual CA3 inactivation have progressively less influence on classification/retrieval.

Thus the model predicts a temporal order:

$$
\text{hippocampal replay} \rightarrow \text{cortical prediction-error reduction} \rightarrow \text{cortical engram stabilization} \rightarrow \text{reduced hippocampal dependence}.
$$

Systems-engram and hippocampal–cortical coupling experiments provide biological motivation for this sequence, while the prediction-error intermediate is specific to the present synthesis. [[27]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28386011/)

### Prediction: sensory prediction-error cells and mnemonic engram cells should become coupled during updating

During ordinary familiar perception:

$$
\text{prediction error low} \rightarrow \text{little CA3 modification}.
$$

During violated expectation:

$$
\text{prediction error high} \rightarrow \text{high engram plasticity}.
$$

The model therefore predicts increased short-latency functional coupling between cortical mismatch populations and hippocampal engram neurons during memory updating.

Human work already suggests that mnemonic prediction errors disrupt sustained hippocampal representations and can bias memory toward updating; the EPC-SNN predicts a cellular circuit mechanism linking that phenomenon to engram allocation. [[24]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2117625118)

### Prediction: artificial engram activation should generate cortical teaching signals without external sensory input

Optogenetic-like activation of an established CA3 attractor should:

$$
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{ \mu } \rightarrow {\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}}_{L}^{ \mu } \rightarrow {\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}}_{L - 1}^{ \mu } \rightarrow  \cdots 
$$

and produce structured cortical activity corresponding to the remembered stimulus.

If a sensory layer is clamped silent, the model still predicts internally generated cortical mismatch/teaching activity as the memory is reconstructed.

This operationalizes the “fictive prediction error” concept proposed in predictive-coding accounts of memory. [[37]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7305946/)

### Model-to-biology correspondence

| Model variable | Proposed biological correlate | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| spikes ${s}_{i}(t)$ | action potentials | high |
| ${q}_{i}$ | intrinsic excitability / CREB-related allocation state | moderately high [[23]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17446403/) |
| recurrent ${J}_{H}$ | CA3 recurrent associative synapses | high at architectural level [[25]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12040087/) |
| prediction error $ \epsilon $ | mismatch-sensitive neural population | moderate [[11]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37699716/) |
| precision $ \Pi $ | gain/attention/neuromodulatory control | moderate–speculative [[20]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22047964/) |
| novelty gate $S$ | distributed mismatch + neuromodulatory signal | speculative |
| replay | hippocampal SWR-associated reinstatement | moderately high [[34]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17173043/) |
| slow cortical learning | systems consolidation | moderate [[38]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28386011/) |
| supervised class reinforcement | reward/teacher-dependent decision learning | functional analogy, not direct cortical classification mechanism |

The table makes explicit that “biologically plausible” should not be interpreted as “biologically established.” The model is deliberately strongest where experimental mechanisms are known and explicitly marks its speculative mappings.

## Limitations, interpretation, and future work

The largest conceptual limitation is that **image classification is not a natural analogue of rodent contextual fear engrams**. Engram evidence is strongest for episodic/contextual memory paradigms, whereas MNIST and CIFAR labels are artificial experimental constructs. The benchmark should therefore be treated as a controlled computational assay, not evidence that biological hippocampus performs handwritten-digit classification. The purpose of classification is to test whether the proposed mechanisms can support useful inference while retaining their predicted mnemonic phenotype. [[39]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22441246/)

A second limitation is that predictive coding itself is a broad family of models rather than a uniquely established cortical algorithm. Rao–Ballard-style hierarchical residual coding, Friston-style precision-weighted inference, and canonical-microcircuit interpretations overlap but are not identical theories. Experimental mismatch responses are consistent with predictive processing but can sometimes admit alternative explanations such as adaptation, expectation-dependent gain, or other forms of contextual modulation. [[40]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10195184/)

A third limitation concerns **weight transport**. Mathematical predictive-coding derivations are often simplest when feedforward and feedback influences have related weights. Requiring exact synaptic symmetry would be biologically unattractive. The primary EPC-SNN implementation should consequently use independently instantiated ascending and descending synapses and directly measure whether useful alignment emerges. Whittington and Bogacz showed that local predictive-coding learning can approximate efficient credit assignment under particular conditions, but this does not eliminate the need to test those conditions in a fully spiking recurrent implementation. [[21]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28333583/)

A fourth limitation is the abstraction of dendrites. Real pyramidal neurons can segregate basal, apical, and somatic signals, potentially offering a considerably more compelling substrate for combining bottom-up drive, top-down prediction, and local plasticity than a point-neuron LIF model. The point-neuron version should therefore be viewed as the **minimum falsifiable model**, not the final biological realization. Tang and colleagues explicitly note dendritic implementations as one route toward biologically plausible prediction-error representations in recurrent predictive memory networks. [[30]](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1010719)

A fifth limitation is the simplification of neuromodulation. Dopamine, acetylcholine, noradrenaline, and other modulatory systems cannot responsibly be collapsed into a single scalar named “surprise.” The initial model should use an abstract third factor $m(t)$, then split it in future versions into distinct variables for expected reward, uncertainty, arousal, and sensory precision. Dopamine-modulated eligibility traces demonstrate that such three-factor learning is computationally plausible, while acetylcholine-related mismatch results suggest that not all neuromodulatory influences should be interpreted as reward. [[41]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17220510/)

A sixth limitation is capacity. Sparse recurrent attractors inevitably face interference as more memories are embedded. The model should therefore report memory capacity explicitly:

$$
 \alpha =\frac{{P}_{stored}}{{N}_{CA3}},
$$

alongside retrieval success rather than demonstrating only a small hand-selected set of memories. Consolidation should free or weaken old hippocampal traces only in a later extension; the first implementation should avoid adding ad hoc deletion mechanisms before the basic model has been tested.

A seventh issue is representational drift and consolidation timescale. Biological systems consolidate over periods vastly longer than a practical simulation. A few hundred replay events cannot literally represent days of biological time. Simulation “sleep” must therefore be interpreted as **accelerated algorithmic time**, and conclusions should concern ordering and dependency of mechanisms rather than absolute biological durations. Systems consolidation and replay provide the biological rationale, but temporal equivalence should not be claimed. [[42]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28386011/)

The most informative next-generation model would use multi-compartment pyramidal neurons, placing bottom-up sensory/error drive predominantly on basal compartments and top-down predictions on apical dendrites, with local dendritic coincidence controlling plasticity. It should also replace static images with temporal sequences so that predictive coding predicts not only missing spatial components but the future. This would connect more naturally to hippocampal sequence processing and to experimentally observed predictive neural activity.

A second extension should explicitly model **memory reconsolidation**. When a stored attractor is reactivated but encounters a large mismatch,

$$
 \parallel  \xi  \parallel >{ \theta }_{update},
$$

the corresponding engram should become temporarily plastic rather than automatically strengthened. The prediction-error literature suggests precisely such a distinction between preserving an expected memory and updating a violated one. [[24]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2117625118)

A third extension should examine **memory linking versus pattern separation** as a controlled tradeoff. The excitability timescale ${ \tau }_{q}$ determines whether two nearby experiences recruit overlapping ensembles, whereas DG inhibition determines how aggressively similar sensory inputs are decorrelated. The model predicts an interaction:

$$
\text{Engram overlap}=f(\text{input similarity}, \Delta t,{ \tau }_{q},\text{prediction error},\text{DG inhibition}).
$$

That multidimensional relationship is more biologically meaningful than using a fixed overlap coefficient. Experimental work on temporally linked memories and competitive engram allocation provides direct constraints for this analysis. [[18]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251287/)

Finally, success should be defined by **convergent validity** rather than benchmark leadership. A convincing EPC-SNN would simultaneously show local spike-based learning, sparse excitability-biased engram allocation, stimulus-specific prediction errors, CA3-like pattern completion, precision-sensitive learning, strong corrupted-input classification, replay-dependent cortical consolidation, and a gradual shift from episodic toward semantic representations. Any model can be given neuroscience-inspired names after it works; the scientific test here is whether perturbing the proposed mechanisms produces the same qualitative dissociations that motivated them in the first place. The existing literature provides unusually strong constraints for that strategy—from optogenetic engram activation and excitability-dependent allocation to partial-cue CA3 deficits, stimulus-specific prediction-error cells, and causal hippocampal–cortical consolidation effects. [[43]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22441246/)
