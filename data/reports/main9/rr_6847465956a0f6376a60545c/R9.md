# Comparative Research Report: French (1789), Russian (1917), and Iranian (1979) Revolutions

**Current date assumed:** October 10, 2026

## Executive Summary

The French, Russian, and Iranian Revolutions differed sharply in ideology, leadership, social base, and institutional outcome, yet they share a common revolutionary logic: each emerged when a state facing accumulated social grievances lost the capacity to manage fiscal, military, ideological, and legitimacy crises through existing institutions. The strongest explanatory framework is therefore **state-centered but not state-only**. State breakdown created openings; class structures, mobilizing networks, ideology, war, and counter-revolutionary conflict determined who seized those openings and what kind of regime followed.

My concrete assessment is that **Skocpol’s state-centered model remains the most persuasive baseline theory**, especially for France and Russia, but it must be expanded for Iran and future scenarios to include **urban networks, religious or cultural authority, digital mobilization, and climate-economic legitimacy crises**. Revolutions are not produced by poverty or ideology alone. They occur when socioeconomic distress becomes politically organized, when coercive institutions fracture or become overstretched, and when revolutionary actors can plausibly claim to embody national salvation.

## 1. Methodological and Source Note

This report prioritizes comparative historical scholarship and institutionally grounded sources. Theda Skocpol’s *States and Social Revolutions* is treated as the core theoretical source because it directly compares France, Russia, and China and explains social revolutions through state breakdown and agrarian class structures rather than ideology alone ([Skocpol, 1979](https://archive.org/details/statessocialrevo0000skoc)). Skocpol’s later article on Iran is especially important because it shows how the Iranian case forced modification of her earlier model by adding culture, religion, and urban mobilization to the explanation ([Skocpol, 1982](https://www.civilresistance.info/biblio-item/1982/rentier-state-and-shia-islam-iranian-revolution)). For contemporary projections, this report uses recent 2022–2026 sources on protest dynamics, youth mobilization, climate stress, and political risk.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: A Seven-Factor Model of Revolutionary Dynamics

A useful comparative framework must distinguish **protest**, **revolutionary situation**, and **revolutionary outcome**.

### 2.1 Core proposition

A social revolution becomes possible when seven factors converge:

1. **Structural socioeconomic strain**  
   Inequality, fiscal crisis, inflation, food or fuel scarcity, peasant distress, unemployment, or austerity create grievances.

2. **State crisis and coercive weakness**  
   The regime cannot solve its fiscal, military, administrative, or legitimacy problems within existing institutions.

3. **Elite fracture**  
   Nobles, bureaucrats, military commanders, clerics, merchants, or technocrats defect, hesitate, or divide.

4. **Mass mobilization networks**  
   Peasant communes, soviets, mosques, labor unions, political clubs, bazaar networks, or digital platforms convert grievances into collective action.

5. **Ideological framing**  
   Revolutionary ideology translates suffering into a claim about justice, sovereignty, religion, class, nation, or dignity.

6. **International pressure**  
   War, sanctions, foreign debt, geopolitical competition, invasion, or dependency intensify domestic breakdown.

7. **Counter-revolution and consolidation**  
   Civil war, foreign invasion, royalist resistance, class enemies, or rival parties justify emergency rule, purges, and centralized state-building.

Skocpol’s key insight is that social revolution required the simultaneous occurrence of **state breakdown and lower-class rebellion**, not simply ideology or class consciousness ([Skocpol, 1979](https://archive.org/details/statessocialrevo0000skoc)). However, Iran shows that “lower-class rebellion” need not be primarily peasant-based; urban religious, bazaar, student, and clerical networks can perform an equivalent mobilizing function ([Skocpol, 1982](https://www.civilresistance.info/biblio-item/1982/rentier-state-and-shia-islam-iranian-revolution)).

## 4. Socioeconomic Preconditions

### 4.1 France: fiscal crisis, agrarian strain, and estate inequality

The French Revolution emerged from the contradictions of absolutism. The monarchy claimed centralized authority but depended on a privileged nobility, tax exemptions, and fragmented corporate rights that limited reform. Fiscal crisis, food scarcity, and resentment against feudal dues created explosive conditions. Skocpol treats Bourbon France as a case where old-regime state breakdown interacted with agrarian class structures and peasant uprisings ([Skocpol, 1979](https://archive.org/details/statessocialrevo0000skoc)).

The peasantry mattered because the collapse of central authority enabled local attacks on seigneurial privileges. Urban unrest and elite constitutional politics alone could not have destroyed the old order so thoroughly. The rural revolt turned fiscal-political crisis into social revolution.

### 4.2 Russia: underdeveloped great-power competition

Imperial Russia was an “underdeveloped great power”: militarily ambitious but socially and administratively fragile. The state faced the demands of modern warfare without a fully modern economy or legitimate representative institutions. Skocpol’s framework emphasizes how international military competition strained old-regime states, weakening elite and military loyalty and opening space for revolution ([Skocpol, 1979](https://archive.org/details/statessocialrevo0000skoc)).

World War I was decisive. It intensified food shortages, military collapse, inflation, and delegitimation of the Romanov monarchy. The Russian case shows that war can transform discontent into state breakdown when soldiers, workers, and peasants no longer believe the regime can defend national survival.

### 4.3 Iran: modernization without political inclusion

Iran diverges from the classic agrarian model. The revolution was not primarily a peasant uprising against landlordism; it was an urban, religious, and bazaar-centered revolution against an authoritarian rentier monarchy. Skocpol later argued that Iran forced her to deepen the role of culture and ideas in revolutionary explanation, treating the Shah’s regime as a vulnerable rentier-absolutist state ([Skocpol, 1982](https://www.civilresistance.info/biblio-item/1982/rentier-state-and-shia-islam-iranian-revolution)).

Oil wealth gave the Shah resources to modernize without broad consent, but it also weakened reciprocal ties between state and society. Rapid modernization, repression, inequality, and perceived Western dependency alienated clerics, merchants, students, and sections of the urban poor. Shi’a clerical institutions had exceptional mobilizing reach through mosques, welfare, education, Islamic courts, and popular religious networks, reaching poor Iranians, artisans, workers, merchants, the unemployed, and rural populations ([Skocpol, 1982](https://padletuploads.blob.core.windows.net/prod/194861778/d08dc007c572145549af095594d39aac/Skocpol.pdf)).

## 5. Ideological Frameworks

### 5.1 France: sovereignty, republican virtue, and Jacobin centralism

French revolutionary ideology began with constitutional reform and rights but radicalized under war, factional struggle, and counter-revolution. Jacobinism evolved from a political club into a label associated after 1793 with Robespierre, the Reign of Terror, centralized republican authority, and an indivisible sovereign state exercising power over civil society ([Wikipedia contributors, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobin_(politics))).

The Jacobins’ ideological importance lay in merging democratic sovereignty with emergency state power. They argued that liberty could require coercion against enemies of liberty. This logic became institutionalized through the Committee of Public Safety, Revolutionary Tribunal, Law of Suspects, and levée en masse.

### 5.2 Russia: Marxism-Leninism and class dictatorship

Russian revolutionary ideology was shaped by Marxism, but Leninism altered Marxism by emphasizing disciplined party leadership, seizure of state power, and dictatorship of the proletariat. Bolshevik ideology justified emergency violence as class struggle. Later revolutionary terror literature notes that Lenin and Trotsky treated mass terror as a necessary weapon during revolutionary dictatorship ([Wikipedia contributors, 2026b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_terror)).

The Bolsheviks also consciously learned from the French Revolution. Early twentieth-century Bolshevism and Jacobinism were often linked; Russian revolutionary leaders invoked the French precedent while trying to avoid what they saw as its mistakes ([Wikipedia contributors, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobin_(politics))).

### 5.3 Iran: Shi’a Islam, anti-imperialism, and clerical sovereignty

Iran’s revolutionary ideology fused Shi’a symbolism, anti-imperial nationalism, social justice, and opposition to Western-backed monarchy. Khomeini initially functioned as a unifying figure for diverse opposition forces, but the post-revolutionary order consolidated around **velayat-e faqih**, the guardianship of the Islamic jurist. The new constitution created a hybrid theocratic-republican system and strengthened institutions such as the Revolutionary Council, Islamic Republican Party, and Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps ([Iran 1400 Project, n.d.](https://iran1400.org/content/political-parties-and-public-trust/)).

Iran therefore shows that ideology is not merely “false consciousness” or propaganda. It can provide organization, moral legitimacy, rituals of sacrifice, and institutional blueprints.

## 6. Revolutionary Leadership Structures

| Revolution | Leadership pattern | Organizational strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| France | Initially plural: National Assembly, Girondins, Jacobins, sans-culottes | Jacobin clubs and Convention committees spread ideology and emergency rule | Factional purges; Terror alienated allies |
| Russia | Dual power, then Bolshevik party centralization | Highly disciplined party, soviets, Red Army, Cheka | One-party coercion and suppression of pluralism |
| Iran | Broad anti-Shah coalition unified by Khomeini | Clerical networks, mosques, bazaars, revolutionary committees, IRGC | Rapid exclusion of secular, leftist, nationalist rivals |

In France, leadership radicalized through crisis. Military setbacks in 1792–1793 intensified factional conflict inside the Convention, with Girondins and Jacobin Montagnards each claiming that only they could save the republic ([George Mason University, n.d.](https://revolution.chnm.org/exhibits/show/liberty--equality--fraternity/war-terror-and-resistance)).

In Russia, the Bolsheviks succeeded because they combined ideology with organizational discipline and strategic timing. The provisional government failed to end the war or resolve land demands; the Bolsheviks offered “peace, land, and bread” and built coercive institutions after taking power.

In Iran, leadership was paradoxical: socially broad but symbolically concentrated. Khomeini’s authority allowed many factions to project their hopes onto one figure. After victory, however, this pluralism narrowed quickly as clerical institutions and revolutionary bodies consolidated power ([Iran 1400 Project, n.d.](https://iran1400.org/content/political-parties-and-public-trust/)).

## 7. International Influences

### 7.1 France: war as radicalizer

The French Revolution became more radical under foreign threat. European monarchies feared revolutionary contagion, while French revolutionaries interpreted foreign pressure as proof of aristocratic conspiracy. France’s war against Austria and Prussia, the First Coalition, and domestic revolts helped justify emergency government. Revolutionary leaders defended the Terror as necessary against foreign invasion, domestic rebellion, and treason ([Wikipedia contributors, 2026c](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Terror)).

### 7.2 Russia: world war and imperial collapse

Russia’s revolution cannot be separated from World War I. War destroyed the credibility of the monarchy, militarized society, and armed millions of peasants in uniform. International conflict accelerated internal class conflict.

### 7.3 Iran: dependency, oil, and geopolitical legitimacy

Iran’s revolution was deeply shaped by the Shah’s association with Western power, especially the United States. Oil wealth empowered the monarchy but also reinforced the image of a state detached from society. Anti-imperialism gave religious and secular opposition groups a shared language.

## 8. Counter-Revolutionary Responses and Revolutionary Violence

### 8.1 France: Terror as state-building

The French revolutionary state extended emergency rule into the provinces through **representatives on mission**, often granted “unlimited powers” to mobilize resources, enforce laws, and suppress rebellion. These powers were often abused and became instruments of Terror ([George Mason University, n.d.](https://revolution.chnm.org/exhibits/show/liberty--equality--fraternity/war-terror-and-resistance)).

The Terror produced massive repression. By Robespierre’s fall in July 1794, **16,594 official death sentences** had been issued across France, including **2,639 in Paris**, while an estimated **10,000–20,000** people were executed without trial or died awaiting trial ([Wikipedia contributors, 2026c](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Terror)).

### 8.2 Russia: Red Terror and civil war

The Bolsheviks faced civil war, White armies, foreign intervention, sabotage, and economic collapse. In September 1918, the Red Terror became official state policy. It used mass detention and execution against alleged “class enemies” and laid foundations for later Soviet repression ([Blakemore, 2020](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/red-terror-set-macabre-course-soviet-union)).

National Geographic reports that tens of thousands, and possibly more than a million, people were branded class enemies, detained, or executed during the Red Terror period; it also notes that the Cheka became a precursor to later Soviet security institutions ([Blakemore, 2020](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/red-terror-set-macabre-course-soviet-union)).

### 8.3 Iran: revolutionary pluralism and repression

Iran followed a common post-revolutionary narrowing pattern. Khomeini was initially viewed as a unifying anti-Shah figure, but the new regime moved swiftly and systematically to eliminate rival political groups ([Wikipedia contributors, 2026d](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_repression_in_the_Islamic_Republic_of_Iran)). Secular, leftist, nationalist, and other dissenting groups were suppressed, while conservative social policies and restrictions on civil liberties defined the new regime’s boundaries ([Iran 1400 Project, n.d.](https://iran1400.org/content/political-parties-and-public-trust/)).

## 9. Institutional Transformations

### 9.1 France

France destroyed feudal privilege, monarchy, and corporate estate society. It created new principles of citizenship, secular law, national sovereignty, mass conscription, and centralized administration. Yet the revolutionary process also produced instability: constitutional monarchy, republic, Terror, Directory, and eventually Napoleonic authoritarianism.

### 9.2 Russia

Russia moved from monarchy to provisional government to Bolshevik one-party rule. The institutional transformation included soviets, the Communist Party-state, secret police, Red Army, planned economy, and later the USSR. The revolution created one of the twentieth century’s most influential state models.

### 9.3 Iran

Iran replaced monarchy with a hybrid theocratic-republican state. Its institutions combined elections with unelected clerical oversight. The Revolutionary Council, Islamic Republican Party, IRGC, and doctrine of velayat-e faqih consolidated clerical authority ([Iran 1400 Project, n.d.](https://iran1400.org/content/political-parties-and-public-trust/)).

## 10. Long-Term Societal Impacts

| Impact area | France | Russia | Iran |
|---|---|---|---|
| Political culture | Citizenship, republicanism, secular nationalism | Class politics, party-state, socialist internationalism | Political Islam, martyrdom politics, clerical sovereignty |
| State power | Centralized bureaucratic state | Highly centralized party-state | Security-theocratic state |
| Social hierarchy | Feudal privilege abolished | Aristocracy and bourgeois property attacked; new party elite emerged | Monarchy abolished; clerical-security elite emerged |
| International legacy | Inspired liberal, nationalist, socialist movements | Inspired communist revolutions and Cold War politics | Inspired Islamist movements and reshaped Middle East geopolitics |
| Repression legacy | Terror, Thermidor, Bonapartism | Red Terror, purges, gulag foundations | Revolutionary courts, executions, surveillance, morality enforcement |

The three revolutions show a repeated pattern: **revolutions democratize political imagination before narrowing political power**. They destroy old hierarchies, but new revolutionary states often consolidate through coercion.

## 11. Application to Future Revolutionary Scenarios

### 11.1 Protest is increasing, but revolution remains rare

Recent protest data show a world with high grievance intensity but uneven revolutionary potential. Economic grievances—especially inflation, food, fuel, and cost-of-living pressures—drove antigovernment protests to a new high in 2022, appearing in nearly every region ([Carothers & Feldman, 2022](https://carnegieendowment.org/russia-eurasia/posts/2022/12/economic-anger-dominated-global-protests-in-2022)). Yet about half of 2022 economic protests lasted a week or less, and fewer than one in six major antigovernment protests produced significant policy change or leadership change ([Carothers & Feldman, 2022](https://carnegieendowment.org/russia-eurasia/posts/2022/12/economic-anger-dominated-global-protests-in-2022)).

This confirms the core theoretical point: **economic anger can trigger protests, but it does not automatically create revolutionary capacity**.

### 11.2 High-risk conditions in 2026

Current political-risk analysis identifies a world entering sustained turbulence. Coface reports that global political risk reached a historic level of **41.1%**, driven by armed conflicts and violent internal unrest ([Coface, 2026](https://www.coface.com/news-economy-and-insights/3-political-and-social-risks-to-watch-out-for-in-2026)). Iran is especially high-risk: Coface ranks it as the second most politically and socially fragile country in the world in 2025, at **86%**, with severe repression no longer fully containing protests rooted in accumulated economic, social, and political crises ([Coface, 2026](https://www.coface.com/news-economy-and-insights/3-political-and-social-risks-to-watch-out-for-in-2026)).

However, my assessment is that **Iran is more likely to experience recurring revolutionary crises than immediate regime collapse**, unless three conditions converge: elite/security defections, coordination among diverse opposition currents, and a credible institutional alternative. External war may actually stabilize the regime temporarily by reviving national-security narratives and suppressing dissent.

### 11.3 Gen Z, digital networks, and new revolutionary forms

Youth-led movements are a major future variable. Harvard Kennedy School notes that nonviolent campaigns with extensive youth participation are more than twice as likely to succeed as campaigns with limited youth participation, although youth movements also face more intense repression ([Harvard Kennedy School, 2026](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/faculty-research/policy-topics/advocacy-social-movements/gen-z-protesting-corruption-worldwide-will)). Gen Z protests tend to be decentralized, digitally organized, and focused on corruption, inequality, unemployment, and elite impunity rather than formal ideology alone ([Harvard Kennedy School, 2026](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/faculty-research/policy-topics/advocacy-social-movements/gen-z-protesting-corruption-worldwide-will)).

A 2026 study of Gen Z digital activism across Asia finds common patterns: hybrid online-offline tactics, symbolic resistance, decentralized leadership, and mobilization against corruption, inequality, authoritarian governance, and censorship ([Subedi et al., 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-08897-z_reference.pdf)). But it also finds that outcomes vary by regime type and coercive capacity: high repression can suppress mobilization temporarily, though it may radicalize discourse over time ([Subedi et al., 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-08897-z_reference.pdf)).

### 11.4 Climate legitimacy crises

Future revolutions may be less agrarian in the eighteenth-century sense but still material and ecological. Climate stress becomes politically explosive when experienced as water insecurity, price increases, agricultural precarity, unsafe infrastructure, and unequal protection. Environmental crisis becomes revolutionary when citizens interpret it as governance failure rather than natural misfortune ([Heinrich Böll Stiftung, 2026](https://www.boell.de/en/2026/04/18/sharing-gen-z-struggle)).

## 12. Conclusion

The French, Russian, and Iranian Revolutions demonstrate that revolutions are not spontaneous eruptions of anger. They are **structured collapses of authority** in which socioeconomic grievances, ideological visions, organizational networks, international pressures, and counter-revolutionary conflict converge.

France and Russia most strongly confirm Skocpol’s classic model: old-regime state breakdown, international pressure, agrarian structures, and lower-class rebellion interacted to destroy monarchies. Iran modifies the model: revolutionary capacity can come not from peasant revolt but from urban religious networks, bazaar alliances, and moral-cultural authority.

The most valid general conclusion is this: **future revolutions are most likely where economic and climate stress delegitimize the state, youth and civil society can mobilize across class lines, and coercive institutions either fracture or become too costly to sustain**. Protest waves alone are insufficient. A revolution requires not only people in the streets, but a state that can no longer rule in the old way and a coalition capable of claiming power in a new one.
