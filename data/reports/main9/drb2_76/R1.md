# International Organizations and Decolonization: Empire, International Scrutiny, and the Remaking of World Order

## Scope, method, and source exclusion

This report treats international organizations neither as straightforward engines of liberation nor simply as instruments of empire. Its central argument is that twentieth-century decolonization developed through a **reciprocal transformation**: imperial states helped construct international institutions in ways that protected substantial colonial discretion, yet the procedures, vocabularies, and forums created by those institutions could be appropriated by colonized peoples, anticolonial activists, and newly independent states. Over time, those actors changed both the meaning of international oversight and the organizations exercising it.

The research is limited to primary materials and scholarship available by early 2022. In accordance with the research instructions, **Eva-Maria Muschik, “Special issue introduction: Towards a global history of international organizations and decolonization,” and every URL specified for that article are excluded from the evidentiary base: they are not relied upon, quoted, or cited here.** The attached research brief explicitly requires that exclusion.  Interpretive sources used below therefore emphasize scholarship published before 2022, including Susan Pedersen, Mark Mazower, Michele Louro, Fredrik Petersson, Meredith Terretta, and earlier contemporary scholarship, alongside League and UN primary documents.

The most useful way to understand the story is as a sequence of institutional shifts. The League of Nations mandates system internationalized aspects of imperial administration without dismantling empire; the United Nations initially inherited much of that compromise but gradually became a more hostile environment for colonial rule; and the arrival of scores of postcolonial states subsequently transformed the UN itself, broadening international politics from a predominantly European and great-power system into one increasingly preoccupied with racial hierarchy, sovereignty, development, economic redistribution, and the North–South distribution of power.

## League mandates, imperial preservation, and international scrutiny

The League of Nations mandate system emerged from the post-First World War settlement. Article 22 of the Covenant declared that former German colonies and Ottoman territories whose inhabitants were deemed “not yet able to stand by themselves” should be administered as a “sacred trust of civilisation.” Administration was entrusted to supposedly more “advanced” states acting as Mandatory Powers “on behalf of the League.” Territories were differentiated into what became A, B, and C mandates according to purported stages of political and social “development.” A mandates, mainly former Ottoman Arab territories, were described as sufficiently advanced for provisional recognition as independent nations subject to advice and assistance; B mandates in Africa permitted much more direct mandatory administration; C mandates, including South West Africa and some Pacific islands, could effectively be administered as integral portions of the Mandatory Power’s territory, though formally subject to mandate obligations. [[1]](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/about/league-of-nations/covenant)

This arrangement was therefore an **internationalization of empire, not decolonization in the later sense**. The victorious Allies did not return Germany’s overseas colonies or generally recognize colonial populations as immediately sovereign. Instead, Britain, France, Belgium, South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, and Japan acquired administrative authority over territories taken from defeated powers. Contemporary and later critics could plausibly see the mandates as annexation under a new legal vocabulary. Pedersen’s reconstruction emphasizes precisely this tension: imperial powers accepted a language of tutelage and international obligation while retaining the practical machinery of colonial rule. [[2]](https://academic.oup.com/book/6410)

The system also reproduced a hierarchical conception of international society. Article 22 divided peoples according to presumed capacity for self-government and entrusted their political futures to “advanced nations.” Such concepts were closely connected to the racialized “civilizing mission” that had justified nineteenth-century empire. Even humanitarian restrictions—against forced abuses, arms and liquor trafficking, or religious discrimination—were embedded within a political order in which colonized populations had no equal voice in deciding the basic terms of administration. [[3]](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/about/league-of-nations/covenant)

Yet the mandate regime contained an institutional innovation that distinguished it from ordinary colonial sovereignty. Mandatory governments were required to submit annual reports to the League Council, while the **Permanent Mandates Commission** examined those reports and advised the Council on compliance. The very idea that an imperial government had to explain its administration to an international body weakened the proposition that colonial rule was exclusively a domestic matter. Pedersen argues that this was the mandates system’s most consequential novelty: not that Geneva normally dictated colonial policy—it did not—but that colonial governance became exposed to sustained international discussion and publicity. [[4]](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/about/league-of-nations/covenant)

The petitions procedure widened that opening. The Covenant itself had not provided a general right of petition, and the Mandatory Powers resisted anything resembling judicial review. Nonetheless, from 1923 the League Council permitted petitions concerning mandate administration to reach the Permanent Mandates Commission through prescribed procedures. Petitioners were not treated as litigants equal to the Mandatory Power, and admissibility rules sharply limited challenges to the mandate system itself. Even so, petitioning allowed nationalists, local leaders, religious communities, and other organizations to place grievances into international circulation. Pedersen finds that petitions were particularly important in places such as Syria, Palestine, and Samoa: they rarely produced immediate redress, but they could generate publicity, connect local politics to Geneva, and force mandatory governments to justify their conduct in an international setting. [[5]](https://academic.oup.com/book/6410/chapter-abstract/150182885)

This exposed a central contradiction. Mandatory Powers remained militarily and administratively dominant, and international supervision was cautious. France, for example, faced international criticism after its repression of the Syrian revolt and bombardment of Damascus but did not lose its mandate. The Commission repeatedly avoided presenting itself as a court with coercive jurisdiction over imperial governments. Nevertheless, its insistence that Mandatory Powers did **not possess ordinary sovereignty** over mandate territories established an important legal and political distinction between mandate administration and outright annexation. [[6]](https://academic.oup.com/book/6410/chapter-abstract/150191602)

The League thus simultaneously stabilized and destabilized imperial legitimacy. It stabilized empire by recognizing European and settler-colonial administration and by translating racial hierarchy into an internationally sanctioned language of tutelage. But it destabilized the old claim that empire was a matter solely for the imperial sovereign. International reporting, petitions, commissions, publicity, and the language of trusteeship made colonial governance discussable before an international audience. In Pedersen’s formulation, the system’s historical importance lay less in compelling colonial reform than in subjecting imperial rule to a level of scrutiny that helped make alternative political futures imaginable. [[7]](https://academic.oup.com/book/6410)

### The League Against Imperialism as an alternative internationalism

Anticolonial activists did not confine themselves to working through Geneva. One of the most significant interwar attempts to construct a rival international arena was the **League Against Imperialism and for National Independence**, formally founded at the International Congress against Colonial Oppression and Imperialism in Brussels in February 1927. The initiative was associated closely with German communist organizer Willi Münzenberg and received Comintern support, but its significance lay in its effort to bring communists together with nationalists, socialists, trade unionists, Black internationalists, pacifists, and colonial activists who did not share a single ideology. Petersson describes Berlin, where its international secretariat operated from 1927 until the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, as a central hub of interwar anti-imperialist networking. [[8]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1369801X.2013.776222)

The Brussels Congress brought together more than 170 participants associated with organizations across Asia, Africa, Europe, the Americas, and the Caribbean. Prominent participants included Jawaharlal Nehru, Indonesian nationalist Mohammad Hatta, African National Congress leader Josiah Gumede, Algerian nationalist Messali Hadj, and representatives of Chinese, African, Caribbean, Latin American, and European movements. Nehru’s own 1927 report to the Indian National Congress stressed the importance of the meeting for linking struggles that previously had often been conducted within the boundaries of particular empires. [[9]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/comrades-against-imperialism/real-league-of-nations/66B1A6FEC713B579B243C87C33F55C99)

The LAI differed fundamentally from the official League of Nations. Geneva’s system presumed a hierarchy between governing and “dependent” peoples; the LAI asserted that colonial peoples themselves were political actors capable of coordinating their liberation. Its campaigns publicized colonial repression, supported national independence movements, organized meetings and publications, connected activists across imperial boundaries, and tried to link colonial liberation to labor and anti-capitalist politics. Its importance was consequently less that it possessed material power—it did not—than that it served as an infrastructure for **transnational political socialization**. [[10]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1369801X.2013.776222)

The organization also contained serious contradictions. Communist sponsorship provided money, organization, and international connections, but Comintern priorities could conflict with the strategies of noncommunist nationalist movements. The rupture became especially sharp after the Communist International’s turn toward a more sectarian “class against class” line. At the LAI’s Frankfurt Congress in 1929, growing attacks on “bourgeois nationalist” collaborators weakened the united-front model. Nehru and other nationalists became increasingly distant, and the LAI’s capacity to act as a broad coalition declined. The Nazi takeover in Germany destroyed its Berlin base in 1933; the organization shifted westward and ultimately disappeared in 1937. [[11]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/comrades-against-imperialism/nehru-and-the-interwar-world/6431E868E9B6E02AF2E63F616EFF4302)

Its longer-term influence should therefore not be exaggerated into an organizational genealogy in which the LAI directly “caused” postwar decolonization. Its more defensible legacy was relational and intellectual. It provided formative encounters among future postcolonial leaders, helped normalize the proposition that colonial struggles were interconnected rather than merely local, and encouraged activists such as Nehru to combine nationalism with internationalism. Historical work on Nehru shows that Brussels broadened his conception of Indian independence into a global struggle against empire; later Afro-Asian cooperation, including Bandung, drew on a much wider set of experiences, but memories of the interwar anti-imperialist milieu remained politically significant. [[12]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/comrades-against-imperialism/real-league-of-nations/66B1A6FEC713B579B243C87C33F55C99)

The contrast between the two “Leagues” is therefore revealing without being absolute. The League of Nations institutionalized a regulated version of imperial trusteeship, while the League Against Imperialism challenged imperialism’s legitimacy from outside official diplomacy. Yet both contributed, in radically different ways, to an increasingly internationalized colonial question: Geneva made imperial governments answerable to international procedures, while Brussels and Berlin helped colonial activists construct transnational constituencies capable of contesting the assumptions on which those procedures rested.

## The United Nations, trusteeship, and the conversion of colonialism into an international question

The United Nations did **not** begin in 1945 as an unequivocally anticolonial organization. Its Charter was the product of wartime coalition politics in which Britain and France remained major colonial empires and the United States itself administered overseas dependencies. Mark Mazower’s history of the UN’s ideological origins cautions against reading the later anticolonial United Nations backward into 1945: the organization inherited important assumptions from the imperial international order even as the Charter contained principles that could later be mobilized against empire. [[13]](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691157955/no-enchanted-palace)

The ambiguity appears directly in the Charter. Article 1(2) made respect for the “equal rights and self-determination of peoples” a UN purpose, and Chapter XII stated that trust territories should progress toward **“self-government or independence.”** But Chapter XI, applying much more broadly to Non-Self-Governing Territories, required administering states only to “develop self-government,” take account of political aspirations, and transmit technical information. It did not explicitly prescribe independence for all colonies. Thus two different formulations—one explicitly including independence, the other stopping at self-government—coexisted in the founding document. [[14]](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/full-text)

That compromise mattered because the **International Trusteeship System** did not automatically encompass the entire colonial world. Under Articles 75–77, trusteeship could cover former League mandates, territories detached from enemy states after the Second World War, and territories voluntarily placed under trusteeship by the states responsible for them. Existing colonial possessions did not automatically become trust territories. [[15]](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/full-text)

Even so, the UN trusteeship arrangements strengthened international oversight relative to the League. The Trusteeship Council could examine administering authorities’ reports, accept and examine petitions, and conduct periodic visiting missions. The Charter also stated more explicitly that advancement toward self-government **or independence**, taking account of the freely expressed wishes of the peoples concerned, was a basic objective. Eleven territories eventually entered the system, and all ultimately achieved self-government or independence, with Palau—the last remaining trust territory—becoming independent in 1994. [[16]](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/trusteeship-council)

The more consequential struggle, however, developed around **Chapter XI**, because it applied beyond the relatively small category of trust territories. Article 73 required administering Members to regard inhabitants’ interests as “paramount,” promote political, economic, social, and educational advancement, develop self-government, and transmit regularly to the Secretary-General statistical and technical information concerning economic, social, and educational conditions. [[17]](https://www.un.org/about-us/un-charter/chapter-11)

Colonial governments initially tended to read Article 73(e) narrowly: information transmission did not, in their view, authorize international supervision comparable to trusteeship. Anti-colonial and non-administering states pressed a much broader interpretation. U.S. diplomatic records from the late 1940s explicitly describe a cleavage between colonial powers and states including India, China, the Philippines, Arab governments, and the Soviet Union over whether the UN possessed broader supervisory authority regarding non-self-governing territories. [[18]](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v01/d178)

The General Assembly became decisive because its procedures allowed these states to transform a seemingly technical reporting obligation into an arena of political accountability. In December 1946, the Assembly created an ad hoc committee to examine information transmitted under Article 73(e). The arrangement evolved into the **Special Committee on Information Transmitted under Article 73(e)**, subsequently known as the **Committee on Information from Non-Self-Governing Territories**. Its membership was deliberately balanced between administering and non-administering states. Although its formal remit initially emphasized economic, social, and educational data, repeated Assembly debates pushed the institution toward questions that were unmistakably political: what constituted self-government, who had authority to determine when a territory ceased to be non-self-governing, and whether political and constitutional information should also be supplied. [[19]](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/node/644)

This procedural struggle illustrates how international institutions could acquire functions that their founders had not fully specified. The Charter did not itself create a standing committee to evaluate Article 73(e) reports; the General Assembly did so. Anti-colonial delegations then used repeated resolutions, committee debates, and reporting demands to narrow the administering powers’ claim that colonial affairs fell within domestic jurisdiction. By 1946 eight administering governments had listed 72 territories they considered non-self-governing; subsequent disputes over reporting and removal from that list became political contests over who had authority to certify that self-government had genuinely been attained. [[20]](https://www.un.org/en/node/69650)

The Assembly’s **Fourth Committee**, responsible for trusteeship and later decolonization questions, became an especially important forum because colonized peoples’ claims could be amplified by governments already possessing UN membership. This altered the political geometry of colonial disputes. Anti-colonial nationalist movements remained indispensable on the ground, but they could increasingly obtain diplomatic backing from India, the Philippines, Arab states, and later African and Asian governments. A U.S. policy assessment from 1952–54 already observed that most states outside Western Europe, the older Commonwealth dominions, and the United States tended to support “self-determination” and “independence,” and that colonial questions were spreading beyond the Fourth Committee into human rights, economic, and other Assembly debates. [[21]](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v03/d808)

The emerging **Arab-Asian group** was important in this transition. By the late 1940s and early 1950s, Arab and Asian delegations increasingly coordinated votes and resolutions on Indonesia, former Italian colonies, racial discrimination, and self-determination. This cooperation preceded Bandung and demonstrated that postcolonial states were not merely passive additions to an existing organization: they learned to use General Assembly procedures collectively, and in doing so helped redefine decolonization as an international rather than exclusively imperial question. [[22]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/with-a-minimum-of-bitterness-decolonization-the-right-to-selfdetermination-and-the-arabasian-group/E0F73E3F4F1FE011BCC6999C3CEAC9A5)

Membership changes greatly strengthened that tendency. The United Nations had 51 founding members in 1945. The 1955 admissions package raised membership to 76; Ghana and Malaya entered in 1957; and the great African independence wave brought membership to 99 in 1960 alone. Seventeen new African and other formerly dependent states entered that year, dramatically increasing the Assembly’s anti-colonial constituency. [[23]](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/growth-in-un-membership)

### Resolution 1514 and the delegitimation of colonial rule

The turning point came on **14 December 1960**, when the General Assembly adopted Resolution 1514 (XV), the **Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples**, by 89 votes to none, with nine abstentions. Its language went significantly beyond the cautious compromises of 1945. [[24]](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/662085?ln=en)

The declaration proclaimed the necessity of bringing colonialism “in all its forms and manifestations” to a speedy and unconditional end. It declared alien subjugation, domination, and exploitation contrary to human rights and the Charter; affirmed that **all peoples have the right to self-determination** and may freely determine their political status and pursue economic, social, and cultural development; and rejected inadequate political, economic, social, or educational “preparedness” as a pretext for delaying independence. It also called for immediate steps to transfer powers to the peoples of trust and non-self-governing territories without conditions or reservations, while simultaneously protecting national unity and territorial integrity. [[25]](https://mandates.un.org/mandate/A/RES/1514%28XV%29)

Its importance was simultaneously normative, political, and institutional. Normatively, it reversed the presumption underlying the old “civilizing” language: colonial peoples no longer had to prove their readiness for freedom; colonial rule now had to justify its continued existence against a declared right of self-determination. Politically, the resolution crystallized an Assembly majority in which newly independent states could use sovereign equality and one-state-one-vote procedures to place colonial powers on the defensive. Institutionally, Resolution 1514 led to the creation in 1961 of the **Special Committee on the Situation with regard to the Implementation of the Declaration**, commonly called the Special Committee on Decolonization or C-24, which began work in 1962. When the older Committee on Information was dissolved in 1963, its information-related functions passed into the new decolonization machinery. [[26]](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/node/644)

Resolution 1514 nevertheless did not mechanically produce independence. Colonial withdrawals resulted from nationalist movements, wars, metropolitan political calculations, changing economic interests, Cold War pressures, and international diplomacy in varying combinations. Nor did the UN possess a general coercive mechanism capable of compelling every colonial government to withdraw. Portugal, Southern Rhodesia, South West Africa/Namibia, and other cases demonstrated the limits of declaratory politics. The significance of the UN was instead that it changed the **legitimating environment**: after 1960, permanent colonial rule became progressively harder to defend as a normal institution of international society. The General Assembly could not abolish empire by resolution, but it helped establish a presumption that legitimate sovereignty belonged to formerly colonized peoples rather than to metropolitan powers. [[27]](https://legal.un.org/repertory/art73/english/rep_supp3_vol3_art73.pdf)

## Decolonization remakes the United Nations and the wider international system

Decolonization did not merely add new flags to an unchanged United Nations. It transformed the membership, agenda, political language, and institutional priorities of the postwar order. UN membership expanded from 51 founding states in 1945 to 99 in 1960, 115 by 1964, and continued rising as African, Caribbean, Pacific, and other territories became independent. By 1980 membership had more than tripled from the founding total. [[28]](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/growth-in-un-membership)

This mattered particularly in the General Assembly, where each state possessed one vote. The Security Council retained its great-power hierarchy through permanent membership and the veto, while financial institutions such as the IMF and World Bank remained governed through weighted voting. But in universal or near-universal bodies based on sovereign equality, the numerical growth of postcolonial states gave coalitions from Asia, Africa, the Middle East, Latin America, and the Caribbean unprecedented agenda-setting capacity. Decolonization therefore produced a striking institutional asymmetry: the political weight of developing countries increased dramatically in the General Assembly even though economic and military power remained concentrated elsewhere. [[29]](https://academic.oup.com/book/35127/chapter-abstract/299279119)

The resulting shift should **not** be described as a simple replacement of an East–West Cold War by a North–South conflict. East–West rivalry remained fundamental—to Korea, Vietnam, nuclear strategy, European security, and many crises in the developing world. Rather, decolonization **superimposed and increasingly institutionalized a North–South axis** concerned with colonialism, racism, development, commodity dependence, trade terms, technology, control of natural resources, and disparities of wealth. Those questions could cut across bloc politics. India, Yugoslavia, Egypt, Indonesia, Ghana, and other states sought room for political agency that was not reducible to either Washington or Moscow, while developing countries increasingly argued that juridical sovereignty was incomplete without greater economic autonomy. [[30]](https://www.un.org/sg/en/content/former-secretary-general/statements/2005-04-22/secretary-generals-address-the-asia-african-summit)

The **Bandung Conference of April 1955** was a major landmark in this process. Twenty-nine Asian and African governments met in Indonesia and endorsed self-determination, opposition to colonialism and racial discrimination, peaceful coexistence, sovereign equality, economic cooperation, and greater consultation among Asian and African states. Bandung was not yet the Non-Aligned Movement, and participating governments differed considerably in ideology and Cold War alignment, but it provided a durable language of Afro-Asian political agency. [[31]](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/860963/files/1955-E.pdf)

The **Non-Aligned Movement (NAM)** emerged formally at the Belgrade summit of 1961. “Non-alignment” did not mean political neutrality in every dispute, nor equidistance from the superpowers. Its core objectives were greater autonomy from great-power blocs, respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, opposition to foreign interference, support for anticolonial liberation, peaceful coexistence, and a stronger voice for states outside the dominant alliance systems. The movement thereby attempted to convert the numerical growth of postcolonial states into coordinated diplomatic influence. [[32]](https://unterm.un.org/unterm2/es/view/UNHQ/b9380c26-022c-49a6-ab4d-e132d8f7c75f)

The **Group of 77** gave this coalition-building a more explicitly economic institutional form. It was created on 15 June 1964 when 77 developing countries signed the “Joint Declaration of the Seventy-Seven Developing Countries” at the conclusion of the first United Nations Conference on Trade and Development. The group sought to coordinate developing countries’ economic positions and increase their collective negotiating power within the UN system. Over time it established institutional presences around UNCTAD, UNESCO, FAO, UNIDO, UNEP, and the IMF/World Bank framework. [[33]](https://www.g77.org/doc/)

This development was inseparable from the perceived economic incompleteness of political decolonization. Many newly independent governments inherited economies heavily dependent on a narrow range of commodity exports, foreign-owned enterprises, imported manufactures, outside capital, and trade relationships formed under colonial rule. They argued that formal sovereignty did not automatically eliminate structural dependence. Political decolonization therefore generated demands for what contemporaries often termed **economic decolonization**. [[34]](https://doi.org/10.1093%2Fdh%2Fdhz043)

Those demands culminated in the campaign for a **New International Economic Order (NIEO)**. At Algeria’s initiative on behalf of the Non-Aligned countries, the General Assembly convened its Sixth Special Session in 1974 on raw materials and development. On 1 May it adopted without a vote Resolutions 3201 (S-VI), the *Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order*, and 3202, its Programme of Action. Ninety-four states associated with the Non-Aligned group had sponsored the initial drafts. [[35]](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/ga_3201/ga_3201.html)

The NIEO agenda sought a substantial redistribution of authority in the international economy. Its principles included permanent sovereignty over natural resources; greater control over transnational corporations; improved and more stable commodity prices; better market access for developing countries; preferential and non-reciprocal trade treatment; more development finance and technology transfer; reform of monetary arrangements; stronger producer cooperation; and greater developing-country participation in global economic decision-making. The underlying claim was that sovereign equality in international law was compromised when former colonies remained structurally subordinate in trade, finance, production, and technology. [[36]](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/ga_3201/ga_3201.html)

The NIEO also exposes the limits of the postcolonial majority’s institutional power. Developing states could pass major declaratory resolutions in the General Assembly, but they could not by themselves compel industrialized states to redistribute wealth, technology, or control of financial institutions. The oil shock and OPEC’s influence briefly strengthened hopes that commodity producer power might shift bargaining leverage, but economic recession, debt crises, divisions among developing countries, resistance from major industrial powers, and the rise of market-oriented policies in the 1980s sharply weakened the project. The contrast between General Assembly voting strength and the distribution of material economic power became one of the defining contradictions of North–South politics. [[34]](https://doi.org/10.1093%2Fdh%2Fdhz043)

Decolonization also changed specialized institutions and ideas of development. Newly independent governments demanded technical assistance, economic planning expertise, education, public health programs, industrialization support, and representation within international agencies. The expansion of development activity sometimes empowered postcolonial governments, but it could also reproduce hierarchy in new forms: international experts and donor institutions increasingly became involved in the administration and planning of sovereign states. Historians have therefore described a paradox in which sovereignty became almost universal precisely while international organizations acquired new roles in shaping the internal capacities of the new states. [[37]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/abs/managing-the-world-the-united-nations-decolonization-and-the-strange-triumph-of-state-sovereignty-in-the-1950s-and-1960s/EC3FA990F5E7E7A017DDF035592D093A)

### Peacekeeping and the management of postcolonial crises

Peacekeeping provides a particularly clear example of institutional adaptation. The UN Charter did not use the term “peacekeeping,” and the practice developed pragmatically. Early observer missions appeared in Palestine in 1948 and India–Pakistan in 1949, but the **Suez Crisis of 1956** produced the first armed United Nations Emergency Force. Its creation was deeply entangled with decolonization: Egypt’s nationalization of the Suez Canal triggered the Anglo-French-Israeli attack, and the UN operation helped supervise the cessation of hostilities and withdrawal of foreign forces. UNEF operated with the consent of the host state rather than as an enforcement action under Article 42. [[38]](https://peacekeeping.un.org/en/our-history)

The relationship became even more pronounced in the **Congo crisis after independence in 1960**. Belgian troops remained or returned amid military disorder, Katanga seceded with significant external involvement, and Cold War powers competed for influence. The United Nations Operation in the Congo (ONUC) became the organization’s first large-scale peacekeeping operation, reaching nearly 20,000 military personnel. Its mandate evolved from facilitating Belgian withdrawal and assisting law and order to preserving Congo’s territorial integrity, preventing civil war, and removing foreign military personnel and mercenaries; under Security Council Resolution 161 it could employ force as a last resort. [[39]](https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/en/content/repertoire/peacekeeping-missions)

ONUC demonstrated both the possibilities and dangers of the expanded UN role. It offered a newly independent state an alternative—however imperfect—to direct intervention by former colonial powers or superpower occupation. At the same time, decisions concerning Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba, Katanga, the use of force, and the Secretary-General’s authority generated profound controversy over whether the UN was defending Congolese sovereignty or constraining it. Scholarship on the Congo consequently treats the operation as a formative struggle over the meaning of international organization in a decolonizing world rather than as a neutral technical exercise. [[40]](https://academic.oup.com/ejil/article/31/2/601/5909398)

Peacekeeping thus belongs within the history of decolonization for a structural reason. The collapse of empires created new sovereign states while simultaneously generating border disputes, secessionist conflicts, foreign military interventions, and crises involving weak state institutions. UN peacekeeping, mediation, technical assistance, and later state-support functions developed partly as responses to those circumstances. The organization increasingly became involved not merely in preventing wars among established powers but in managing the difficult political transitions produced by the dissolution of empire. [[41]](https://academic.oup.com/ejil/article/31/2/601/5909398)

## Overall interpretation

The relationship between international organizations and decolonization was therefore **constitutively ambiguous**.

The League of Nations mandates system preserved imperial authority while altering its legal form. Its language of civilization was hierarchical and its machinery left Mandatory Powers overwhelmingly dominant. Yet by making them report to Geneva, subjecting them to a Permanent Mandates Commission, and eventually permitting petitions, the League weakened the old premise that colonial sovereignty required no international justification. The unintended result was a political vocabulary and procedural repertoire that anticolonial actors could exploit. [[42]](https://academic.oup.com/book/6410/chapter-abstract/150182885)

The League Against Imperialism represented a different kind of internationalization. It lacked the League of Nations’ legal authority but connected activists who rejected the legitimacy of imperial tutelage itself. Its internal conflicts—especially between communist organizational priorities and nationalist strategies—prevented it from becoming a unified global anticolonial movement. Nonetheless, it helped make anticolonial politics transnational and offered a formative setting for figures who would later shape postcolonial internationalism. [[43]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1369801X.2013.776222)

The United Nations initially reproduced the same basic compromise in more advanced form. Trusteeship explicitly recognized independence as a possible objective and created more substantial oversight mechanisms, but most colonies remained outside the Trusteeship System, under the weaker provisions of Chapter XI. The decisive transformation came not simply from what the founders wrote in San Francisco but from **what later member states did with the Charter**. India, the Philippines, Arab states, Afro-Asian coalitions, and eventually a rapidly growing postcolonial majority enlarged the General Assembly’s interpretation of international responsibility for dependent territories. The Committee on Information, Fourth Committee, trusteeship machinery, and finally the C-24 converted colonial administration from a largely metropolitan prerogative into an object of routine international argument. [[44]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03086534.2017.1332133)

Resolution 1514 in 1960 marked the normative culmination of that change. Whereas the mandate system had asked which “advanced” state should govern supposedly unready peoples, the Declaration on Decolonization treated colonial domination itself as anomalous and proclaimed self-determination as a universal right. That was a fundamental reversal of international legitimacy even though the UN did not itself create the anticolonial movements that made independence possible. [[45]](https://mandates.un.org/mandate/A/RES/1514%28XV%29)

Finally, decolonization transformed the international organizations through which it had partly been pursued. Newly independent states changed General Assembly majorities, formed the Non-Aligned Movement and Group of 77, introduced a durable North–South dimension into international politics, and demanded that sovereign equality extend into the economic sphere through the NIEO. At the same time, postcolonial crises stimulated innovations such as large-scale peacekeeping and international technical assistance. The result was not the disappearance of hierarchy but its reconfiguration: the legal universality of sovereign states coexisted with Security Council privilege, unequal economic power, dependence on international finance, and continued disputes over external intervention. [[46]](https://www.g77.org/doc/)

The most historically useful conclusion, therefore, is neither that international organizations “gave” colonized peoples independence nor that they were merely disguises for imperial domination. **They were contested institutions whose rules were initially written within an imperial world but were progressively appropriated and rewritten—politically, procedurally, and normatively—by actors produced by the dismantling of that world.** Decolonization changed who could speak internationally, what counted as a legitimate subject of international concern, and what governments could plausibly claim sovereignty meant. In doing so, it remade the League’s institutional legacy, transformed the United Nations, and shifted twentieth-century international politics toward questions of racial equality, political self-determination, economic sovereignty, development, and the unresolved distribution of power between North and South.
