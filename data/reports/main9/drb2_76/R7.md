# From Imperial Assemblage to Global South: The Contradictory Role of International Organizations in 20th-Century Decolonization

## The League of Nations: An Imperial Assemblage and the Dawn of Anti-Colonial Resistance

The period spanning the two World Wars witnessed the creation of the first major international organization dedicated to maintaining peace and security—the League of Nations. Its role in the process of decolonization is one of profound historical contradiction. Far from being an anti-imperial body, the League was fundamentally an instrument designed to manage and legitimize the redistribution of former German and Ottoman colonies among the Allied powers. This system, known as the Mandates System, represented a sophisticated form of imperial administration repackaged under a veneer of international supervision and idealistic rhetoric. Yet, this very structure contained the seeds of its own subversion. The transparency required by its committees and the sacred language of its charter provided a platform for both international scrutiny and the mobilization of a nascent global anti-colonial movement. Understanding this duality is essential to grasping the complex, symbiotic, yet often antagonistic relationship between international institutions and the forces they sought to govern.

The institutional foundation for the League's involvement in colonial affairs was Article 22 of its Covenant [[111](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-185531/), [112](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/829695)]. This article addressed the status of territories formerly belonging to the Central Powers, stipulating that their administration should be guided by the principle that "the well-being and development of such peoples form a sacred trust of civilization" [[106](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/emergence-of-international-society-in-the-1920s/imperial-internationalism/A1BC6F0777464760F7128B62D6832BF7)]. To implement this principle, mandatory powers—primarily Britain and France—were tasked with exercising "tutelage" over these territories on behalf of the League [[113](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919Parisv13/ch10subch1), [114](https://legal-tools.org/doc/106a5f/pdf)]. This system was not intended to lead immediately to independence but rather to guide the inhabitants along a path toward self-government, a process that could take many years. The League itself categorized mandates into three classes based on the perceived level of development of the territory and its inhabitants [[13](https://www.academia.edu/105229639/The_British_mandate_in_Iraq_1920_1932_Considered_history_of_formation_existence_and_liquidation_the_British_mandate_of_The_League_of_Nations_in_territory_of_Iraq_1920_1932_)]. Class A mandates, which included the former Ottoman provinces of Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Palestine, were considered the most advanced [[14](https://www.britannica.com/topic/mandate-League-of-Nations)]. While nominally destined for independence, these territories were placed under the control of mandatory powers who retained significant administrative authority [[13](https://www.academia.edu/105229639/The_British_mandate_in_Iraq_1920_1932_Considered_history_of_formation_existence_and_liquidation_the_British_mandate_of_The_League_of_Nations_in_territory_of_Iraq_1920_1932_)]. For instance, Britain established an Arab monarchy in Iraq under Fayṣal in 1921, but nationalist opposition led to a revolt against British rule in 1920, highlighting the tensions inherent in this arrangement [[12](https://tcf.org/content/report/beyond-bad-borders-how-nationalism-imperialism-and-power-politics-shaped-the-modern-middle-east/), [46](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iraq/British-occupation-and-the-mandatory-regime), [60](https://www.washingtoninstitute.org/media/3546)]. Classes B and C mandates were treated more like traditional colonies, with less emphasis on immediate self-government [[13](https://www.academia.edu/105229639/The_British_mandate_in_Iraq_1920_1932_Considered_history_of_formation_existence_and_liquidation_the_British_mandate_of_The_League_of_Nations_in_territory_of_Iraq_1920_1932_)].

This framework has been described by scholars as an "Imperial Assemblage," a term that captures its dual nature as both an engine of imperial administration and a nascent public sphere for colonial governance [[74](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-journal-of-law-in-context/article/league-of-nations-as-an-imperial-assemblage-coloniality-indirect-rule-and-the-actualization-of-international-law/959F65CD60C90A78C8229DD22D5F9B7D?)]. On one hand, the system was a clear extension of pre-war ideas about proper colonial management, effectively repackaging empire under a new international label [[75](https://manela.scholars.harvard.edu/file_url/138), [76](https://explaininghistory.org/2025/05/31/mandates-or-empire-repackaged-susan-pedersen-and-the-complex-legacy-of-the-mandates-system/)]. It legitimized foreign rule by embedding it within an international legal structure, providing mandatory powers with a mandate to govern territories whose peoples had not yet attained a full measure of self-government [[64](https://decol.guam.gov/sites/default/files/giha-mona-sd-study_part-i-digital_0.pdf), [91](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/revisiting-the-origins-of-human-rights/from-league-of-nations-mandates-to-decolonization-a-brief-history-of-rights/CFD4BF06D045D655F3AE0732BDE24524)]. The very existence of the mandate system was predicated on the idea that these populations were not ready for sovereignty, thereby justifying prolonged foreign tutelage [[106](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/emergence-of-international-society-in-the-1920s/imperial-internationalism/A1BC6F0777464760F7128B62D6832BF7)]. However, the system also created novel mechanisms for international oversight that were not present in the unmediated colonial relationships of previous centuries. The most important of these was the Permanent Mandates Commission (PMC). The PMC was responsible for reviewing the annual administrative reports submitted by each mandatory power, offering a forum where the conduct of colonial administration could be scrutinized by representatives of League members [[90](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/united-nations-and-the-question-of-palestine/interwar-period/EA727938F11DF8FA2E975D9EB1456669)]. Although the PMC's powers were limited and its effectiveness often constrained by the political realities of the time, it introduced a "level of international diplomacy, publicity, and 'talk'" that had unintended consequences [[90](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/united-nations-and-the-question-of-palestine/interwar-period/EA727938F11DF8FA2E975D9EB1456669)]. Mandatory powers became increasingly concerned with their international reputation and the perception of their administration, creating a space where colonial issues could be debated publicly and criticized internationally. This dynamic forced administrators to justify their policies before a global audience, however sympathetic or critical that audience might be.

Furthermore, the idealistic language embedded in the Covenant's Articles 22 and 23 provided powerful ideological tools that anti-colonial nationalists could seize upon and amplify [[76](https://explaininghistory.org/2025/05/31/mandates-or-empire-repackaged-susan-pedersen-and-the-complex-legacy-of-the-mandates-system/)]. The pledge to ensure the well-being and development of the mandated peoples, to take due account of their political aspirations, and to develop self-government offered a moral framework against which colonial rule could be judged [[95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapter_XI_of_the_United_Nations_Charter), [106](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/emergence-of-international-society-in-the-1920s/imperial-internationalism/A1BC6F0777464760F7128B62D6832BF7)]. In practice, this often meant that mandatory powers used the language of the mandate to legitimate their actions while resisting substantive changes that would threaten their control. For example, in Syria, the French high commission in Beirut exercised limited imperial power and lacked the popular consent or economic wherewithal to fully consolidate its local authority, yet it continued to administer the territory under the guise of its mandate [[15](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4284208)]. Similarly, in Iraq, nationalist demands for independence were met with British offers of a monarchy, all while Britain framed its presence as a necessary step in guiding Iraq toward eventual self-rule [[46](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iraq/British-occupation-and-the-mandatory-regime)]. The gap between the rhetoric of the "sacred trust" and the reality of colonial administration became a central point of contention, fueling anti-colonial resistance across the globe. The very vocabulary of the mandates system thus became a double-edged sword, providing both the justification for continued rule and the ideological basis for demands for change.

While the League of Nations institutionalized a new form of empire, it simultaneously served as a catalyst for organized international opposition. The most significant manifestation of this resistance was the League Against Imperialism and Colonial Oppression (LAI). Founded at a major congress in Brussels in February 1927, the LAI represented a concerted effort to forge a global front against colonialism, operating entirely outside the League's framework [[5](https://revolutionsnewsstand.com/2025/10/28/the-brussels-congress-against-imperialism-by-manuel-gomez-from-the-communist-vol-6-nos-3-may-1927/), [6](https://thetricontinental.org/nuestra-america/voices-of-1927/)]. Initiated by a wing of the Communist International, the LAI brought together 174 delegates representing 134 organizations from 34 countries [[4](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3204036/download), [8](https://www.academia.edu/3087708/Hub_of_the_Anti_Imperialist_Movement_The_League_against_Imperialism_and_Berlin_1927_1933)]. Crucially, out of the 175 delegates, 107 were from 37 countries under colonial rule, underscoring the movement's grassroots, anti-colonial character [[2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_Against_Imperialism_and_Colonial_Oppression)]. The congress aimed explicitly to create a "mass anti-imperialist movement" on a world scale, moving beyond national or regional struggles to build transnational solidarity among oppressed peoples, trade unionists, and progressive intellectuals [[2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_Against_Imperialism_and_Colonial_Oppression), [6](https://thetricontinental.org/nuestra-america/voices-of-1927/)]. Figures like Jawaharlal Nehru traveled in the service of the League, highlighting its importance as a hub for anti-colonial activists from Asia, Africa, and Europe [[63](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/comrades-against-imperialism/making-of-the-league-against-imperialism-1927/BF29DA267B98D29F047D120B7EA36860), [110](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/comrades-against-imperialism/real-league-of-nations/66B1A6FEC713B579B243C87C33F55C99)]. Berlin was established as the league's operational hub, facilitating coordination and communication among its diverse network of member organizations [[8](https://www.academia.edu/3087708/Hub_of_the_Anti_Imperialist_Movement_The_League_against_Imperialism_and_Berlin_1927_1933)]. The LAI's strategy was not to seek reform within the existing international order but to overthrow it, viewing the League of Nations itself as an instrument of imperialism. By organizing globally and using the very international fora opened by the post-war settlement to criticize the League's members, the LAI demonstrated the growing strength and sophistication of the anti-colonial struggle. It proved that even as the League attempted to formalize and legitimize the colonial status quo, a powerful, organized, and ideologically driven counter-force was rapidly taking shape, laying the intellectual and organizational groundwork for the wave of decolonization that would follow World War II. The relationship between the League and the LAI was therefore one of fundamental antagonism, with the latter emerging as a direct and forceful challenge to the legitimacy of the former's imperial architecture.

## The United Nations: From Trusteeship to the Political Frontline of Decolonization

Following the devastation of World War II, the architects of the new international order recognized the urgent need to address the issue of colonialism, which had been a source of instability and conflict throughout the interwar period. The United Nations, born from the ashes of the League of Nations, inherited this complex legacy. Its approach evolved significantly, moving from a narrowly focused administrative mechanism to a broad-based political and legal campaign against colonial rule. While the Trusteeship System was designed as a direct successor to the League's Mandates System, intended to provide closer international supervision over territories moving toward independence, it ultimately proved less central to the overall decolonization process than the political activism of the UN General Assembly. It was the General Assembly, now populated by a new generation of anti-colonial states, that transformed the UN into the principal arena for the dismantling of the colonial empires, culminating in landmark resolutions that enshrined the right to self-determination as a cornerstone of international law.

The International Trusteeship System was formally established by Chapters XI and XII of the UN Charter [[57](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07075332.2025.2471772), [61](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e563?p=emailAGM/Ejv7bMB02&d=/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e563)]. Its framers viewed it as an improvement on the League's model, designed to correct its perceived defects by creating a more robust framework for international oversight [[79](https://repository.law.umich.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=8653&context=mlr)]. The system was created to act as a government over non-self-governing territories that were being colonized, ensuring that administering powers fulfilled their duties [[17](https://www.facebook.com/UNVisitorsCentre/posts/between-1945-and-1994-the-trusteeship-council-one-of-the-six-main-organs-of-the-/1350224367150090/)]. Unlike the Mandates System, which had allowed for a degree of ambiguity, the Trusteeship Council was given the specific task of executing a closely defined system of operations to promote the advancement of the inhabitants of Trust Territories and their progressive development [[16](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/trusteeship-council), [21](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/trusteeship-council)]. The Council itself was structured differently from the League's Permanent Mandates Commission; it consisted of the five permanent members of the Security Council (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States) plus elected members, giving it a different balance of power and a potentially stronger enforcement capacity [[18](https://www.dagdok.org/w/dd/en/un-system/trusteeship-council)]. The system came into effect after the League of Nations ceased to exist, and the remaining League mandates in the Pacific became the initial UN trust territories [[81](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_trust_territories)]. There were eleven trust territories under the administration of the UN Trusteeship Council, including Western Samoa, Tanganyika, Rwanda-Urundi, and various Cameroons territories [[80](https://research.un.org/en/docs/tc/territories)].

Despite these refinements, the Trusteeship System faced inherent limitations. Chapter XI of the Charter, which dealt with "Declaration Regarding Non-Self-Governing Territories," was criticized for being "soft," vague, and somewhat profiled as a weak mechanism compared to other provisions of the Charter [[116](https://access.archive-ouverte.unige.ch/access/metadata/1e633402-806d-40ba-97f1-9598e84d2ba7/download)]. Its effectiveness relied heavily on the voluntary cooperation of the administering powers. Article 73 of Chapter XI, in particular, imposed a duty on member states administering non-self-governing territories to transmit regular information to the UN Secretary-General about conditions in those territories [[93](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/chapter-11), [94](https://legal.un.org/repertory/art73.shtml), [115](https://www.facebook.com/groups/656202015320922/posts/1508986326709149/)]. This information was supposed to cover political, economic, social, and educational advancements, allowing the international community to monitor progress toward self-government [[48](https://polsci.institute/international-relations/un-approach-self-determination-policies-resolutions/)]. However, the obligation rested on the administering powers themselves, creating a potential conflict of interest. The UN's role was primarily supervisory and consultative, lacking strong enforcement powers to compel compliance or intervene if an administering power was deemed to be failing its obligations [[49](https://jura.ku.dk/jurabog/pdf/juridiske-monografier/ross_constitution_of_the_united_nations_1950.pdf)]. Consequently, while the Trusteeship System successfully managed the transition to independence for its eleven designated territories, its scope was narrow, focusing only on specific lands rather than addressing the broader colonial question affecting hundreds of millions of people worldwide [[82](https://digitalcommons.pace.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1411&context=pilr)]. Its work was completed when the last trust territory achieved independence, leading the Trusteeship Council to suspend its work on November 1, 1994 [[82](https://digitalcommons.pace.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1411&context=pilr)].

The true engine of decolonization within the UN framework, however, was not the Trusteeship Council but the General Assembly. With the admission of dozens of newly independent and anti-colonial states after 1945, the GA's composition shifted dramatically, altering its agenda and power dynamics. These new members, many of whom were non-aligned, brought with them a fierce determination to see an end to colonial rule. They quickly realized that the GA, with its principle of sovereign equality of all members, was the most powerful platform to advance their cause. The primary mechanism for action was the implementation of Chapter XI. Administering powers were required to submit detailed reports on the political aspirations of the peoples in their territories [[95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapter_XI_of_the_United_Nations_Charter)]. These reports were then reviewed by a Special Committee on Decolonization, commonly known as the Special Committee or C-24 [[109](https://digitallibrary.un.org/nanna/record/187838/files/A_RES_32_43-EN.pdf?withWatermark=0&withMetadata=0&version=1&registerDownload=1)]. This committee became a crucial forum for exposing colonial abuses and putting pressure on administering powers. It was granted the authority to investigate complaints and make recommendations, transforming the issue from a bilateral matter between an administers and the UN into a matter of international concern [[31](https://transcripts.un.org/en/asset/k1s/k1sjz1in6d)]. The committee's proceedings provided a stage for representatives of colonial peoples to voice their grievances directly to the international community, turning abstract principles into concrete political debates.

The culmination of this political offensive was the adoption of the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples. Adopted unanimously by the General Assembly on December 14, 1960, as Resolution 1514 (XV), this document marked a pivotal moment in the history of international relations [[28](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/dicc/dicc.html), [50](https://valdaiclub.com/a/highlights/the-1960-un-declaration-on-the-granting), [102](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/United_Nations_General_Assembly_Resolution_1514)]. The vote was 89 in favor, none opposed, with nine abstentions, including Australia, Belgium, France, and the United Kingdom [[100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_on_the_Granting_of_Independence_to_Colonial_Countries_and_Peoples)]. The declaration went far beyond the descriptive and supervisory language of Chapter XI. It boldly declared that "the continuation of colonialism is a violation of the principles of the Charter" and that "all peoples have the right to self-determination" [[53](https://www.facebook.com/groups/326953933989277/posts/32552359754355277/)]. Most significantly, it asserted that "colonialism in all its forms and manifestations must be brought to a speedy and unconditional end" [[104](https://kennedyhumanrights.org/our-voices/modern-decolonization-the-role-of-the-un-and-the-responsibility-of-all-of-us/)]. This single resolution transformed the principle of self-determination from a theoretical ideal discussed in commissions into a binding norm of international law and a central tenet of the UN's mission [[85](https://brill.com/view/journals/nord/91/2/article-p189_001.xml?language=en)]. Its legal weight was immense, later being cited by the International Court of Justice in its advisory opinion on the Western Sahara, confirming that the application of Resolution 1514 was not subject to debate [[68](https://law.justia.com/cases/foreign/international/1975-icj-rep-12.html)]. The declaration empowered liberation movements around the world, providing them with a powerful legal and moral argument against their colonial rulers. It signaled a decisive victory for the anti-colonial bloc within the UN and cemented the organization's role not merely as a supervisor of the colonial transition, but as a primary agent in its political and legal dismantling. The UN had moved decisively from being a manager of empire to becoming its most vocal and influential critic.

## The Post-Colonial Transformation: Reshaping Global Governance and the Rise of the Global South

The rapid wave of decolonization that swept across Asia and Africa after 1945 did not simply add new members to the roster of the United Nations; it fundamentally reconfigured the institution's power dynamics, agenda, and identity. The influx of dozens of newly independent states, many of which were located in the Global South, injected a new set of priorities and perspectives into the corridors of global governance. This demographic and ideological shift precipitated a profound transformation, replacing the dominant East-West axis of Cold War politics with a new and equally potent North-South divide. In response to their shared economic vulnerabilities and political marginalization within the existing international order, these nations began to organize collectively, forging powerful blocs that would champion a new agenda focused on economic justice and equitable development. This collective action, most notably through the Group of 77, gave rise to the call for a New International Economic Order, marking a significant evolution from the political struggle for independence to the economic struggle for sovereignty and prosperity.

The most immediate and dramatic impact of decolonization was the alteration of the UN's internal balance of power. The number of member states swelled, with nearly a third of the current membership joining since the UN's birth in 1945 [[30](https://www.facebook.com/UNGeneva/posts/since-the-birth-of-the-united-nations-in-1945-nearly-13-of-the-worlds-population/1405791768250789/)]. This expansion diluted the influence of the traditional Western European colonial powers and amplified the voice of nations that had previously been denied a seat at the table. These new members, often united by their recent experiences of colonial rule and their desire for genuine sovereignty, formed voting blocs that could decisively shape the outcomes of debates in the General Assembly. Their primary focus shifted from securing independence to shaping the rules of the international system that would govern their newly sovereign states. This led to a new geopolitical alignment, with the binary logic of the Cold War receding as a secondary concern compared to the pressing issues of economic development, resource control, and international equity. The North-South divide—the economic chasm between the wealthy industrialized nations of the Global North and the poorer developing nations of the Global South—became the central axis of negotiation and conflict within the UN system.

In response to their collective challenges, developing nations forged powerful political and economic alliances. The Non-Aligned Movement (NAM), which emerged during the Cold War, provided a platform for states to pursue an independent foreign policy, avoiding entanglement in the military and ideological conflicts between the United States and the Soviet Union [[92](https://diplowiki.com/g77-global-south-coordination)]. While NAM was primarily a political coalition, the economic dimension of the new international order soon demanded a more focused institutional response. This led to the formation of the Group of 77 (G77) on June 15, 1964, when seventy-seven developing countries issued the "Joint Declaration of the Seventy-Seven Developing Countries" at the first session of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) in Geneva [[42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_77), [43](https://www.g77.org/doc/)]. The group's original name was retained despite its growth to 134 member countries, as a symbol of its historical roots and shared purpose [[43](https://www.g77.org/doc/)]. The G77 was founded to provide a unified forum for developing nations to articulate and advance their collective economic interests, enhance their joint negotiating capacity on international economic issues, and promote South-South cooperation for development [[92](https://diplowiki.com/g77-global-south-coordination)]. The organization developed a permanent institutional structure, establishing liaison offices in key UN cities like Geneva, Nairobi, and Paris to coordinate positions and advocate for their common agenda [[43](https://www.g77.org/doc/)].

The centerpiece of the G77's agenda was the promotion of a New International Economic Order (NIEO). This was not a minor adjustment to the existing system but a radical proposal for its fundamental restructuring. The NIEO sought to address the deep-seated inequalities that persisted between the developed North and the developing South, arguing that the post-war economic architecture still perpetuated patterns of dependency and exploitation [[38](https://investmentpolicy.unctad.org/international-investment-agreements/treaty-files/2775/download)]. The demand for a NIEO was formally articulated and endorsed by the UN General Assembly in May 1974 with the adoption of the "Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order" (Resolution 3201 (S-VI)) [[37](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/218450?ln=en), [41](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09614524.2025.2534149)]. This resolution called for a new international economic order based on principles of equity, sovereign equality, interdependence, common interest, and cooperation among all states [[38](https://investmentpolicy.unctad.org/international-investment-agreements/treaty-files/2775/download)]. The program outlined a comprehensive agenda for change, demanding that the rights of developing countries be respected and their economies be integrated into the global system on fair terms. Key objectives included ending foreign domination, including colonial and neocolonial exploitation, through the exercise of permanent sovereignty over natural resources by developing countries [[39](http://www.un-documents.net/s6r3202.htm)]. It also called for measures to reverse the stagnation or decline in the real prices of commodities exported by developing nations, aiming to link export prices more fairly with import prices from developed nations [[39](http://www.un-documents.net/s6r3202.htm)]. Furthermore, the NIEO agenda demanded urgent reforms of the international monetary system to prevent inflation from being transferred to developing countries and to ensure their adequate participation in decision-making bodies like the proposed Council of Governors of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) [[39](http://www.un-documents.net/s6r3202.htm)]. Other key demands included promoting the industrialization of developing countries by encouraging investment and technology transfer, establishing an international code of conduct for transnational corporations to prevent interference in host countries' affairs, and increasing financial transfers and considering debt cancellation for the most affected nations [[39](http://www.un-documents.net/s6r3202.htm)]. The push for the NIEO represented a profound transformation in the goals of the post-colonial world, shifting the focus from political liberation to the pursuit of economic justice and true economic sovereignty.

Decolonization also compelled the United Nations to expand its functions beyond political advocacy and into the volatile and dangerous realm of peace and security. The abrupt end of colonial rule in many regions often led to state collapse, civil wars, and secessionist conflicts, creating situations that threatened international peace and security. The UN was drawn into these crises, pioneering a new type of peacekeeping operation—one that was far more complex and interventionist than the traditional missions of observer and buffer zone monitoring. The quintessential example of this new role was the United Nations Operation in the Congo (ONUC), launched in 1960 following the country's independence from Belgium [[44](https://www.walterdorn.net/40-intelligence-and-peacekeeping-the-un-operation-in-the-congo-1960-64)]. When the Belgians deployed troops to protect their citizens and a secessionist crisis erupted in Katanga, the Congolese government appealed to the UN for assistance [[44](https://www.walterdorn.net/40-intelligence-and-peacekeeping-the-un-operation-in-the-congo-1960-64)]. The Security Council authorized ONUC, which grew to include 93,000 personnel from 34 states, making it one of the largest and most controversial peacekeeping operations in UN history [[44](https://www.walterdorn.net/40-intelligence-and-peacekeeping-the-un-operation-in-the-congo-1960-64), [45](https://www.manchesterhive.com/display/9781526137906/9781526137906.00009.xml)].

The experience of ONUC starkly illustrated the evolving nature and immense challenges of UN peacekeeping in a post-colonial context. Initially conceived with a traditional peacekeeping mandate focused on overseeing the withdrawal of Belgian forces and assisting the civilian administration, ONUC's role expanded dramatically after the murder of Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba in February 1961 [[44](https://www.walterdorn.net/40-intelligence-and-peacekeeping-the-un-operation-in-the-congo-1960-64)]. Subsequent Security Council resolutions, such as Resolution 161, broadened the mandate to include "all appropriate measures to prevent the occurrence of civil war...including...the use of force, if necessary, in the last resort" [[44](https://www.walterdorn.net/40-intelligence-and-peacekeeping-the-un-operation-in-the-congo-1960-64)]. This marked a departure from classic peacekeeping principles of impartiality and consent, thrusting the UN into a more active enforcement role. The complexity of the Congo crisis, with its intricate web of ethnic, political, and mercenary factions, exposed the UN's intelligence and operational shortcomings. Fearing accusations of violating national sovereignty and compromising its public image, the civilian leadership was initially reluctant to embrace covert intelligence methods [[44](https://www.walterdorn.net/40-intelligence-and-peacekeeping-the-un-operation-in-the-congo-1960-64)]. However, the necessity of gathering information about sensitive political and security matters eventually led to the establishment of the Military Information Branch (MIB), the UN's first dedicated intelligence body [[44](https://www.walterdorn.net/40-intelligence-and-peacekeeping-the-un-operation-in-the-congo-1960-64)]. The MIB's late start and persistent limitations, including inadequate training and poor procedures for disseminating intelligence, hampered the operation and contributed to tragic incidents like the ambush and massacre of a Ghanaian garrison at Port Francqui in April 1961 [[44](https://www.walterdorn.net/40-intelligence-and-peacekeeping-the-un-operation-in-the-congo-1960-64)]. The Congo crisis thus demonstrated that decolonization did not automatically produce stable nation-states. Instead, it created fragile new polities that required sophisticated and often militarized UN intervention. The experience forced the UN to confront the difficult balance between upholding its principles and responding effectively to complex emergencies, setting a precedent for future large-scale, multidimensional peacekeeping missions in the decades that followed.

## Synthesis of a Contradictory Legacy

The 20th-century relationship between international organizations and decolonization was defined by a persistent and productive tension. These bodies, conceived in the image of the very states that wielded imperial power, were simultaneously instruments of imperial administration and platforms for anti-colonial struggle. The League of Nations, in its foundational period, exemplified this paradox. It was constructed as an "Imperial Assemblage," a sophisticated apparatus to manage the spoils of war by channeling former colonies into a new system of mandates that extended, rather than ended, imperial rule under a thin veil of international supervision [[74](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-journal-of-law-in-context/article/league-of-nations-as-an-imperial-assemblage-coloniality-indirect-rule-and-the-actualization-of-international-law/959F65CD60C90A78C8229DD22D5F9B7D?), [76](https://explaininghistory.org/2025/05/31/mandates-or-empire-repackaged-susan-pedersen-and-the-complex-legacy-of-the-mandates-system/)]. Yet, the transparency built into its Permanent Mandates Commission and the idealistic language of its Covenant created openings that anti-colonial actors exploited [[90](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/united-nations-and-the-question-of-palestine/interwar-period/EA727938F11DF8FA2E975D9EB1456669), [106](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/emergence-of-international-society-in-the-1920s/imperial-internationalism/A1BC6F0777464760F7128B62D6832BF7)]. The emergence of the League Against Imperialism, a global movement that operated in direct opposition to the League's principles, underscored the irreconcilable conflict at the heart of the new international order [[3](https://mronline.org/2018/07/20/the-league-against-imperialism-1927-37-an-early-attempt-at-global-anti-colonial-unity/), [8](https://www.academia.edu/3087708/Hub_of_the_Anti_Imperialist_Movement_The_League_against_Imperialism_and_Berlin_1927_1933)].

With the advent of the United Nations, the dynamic evolved but remained fundamentally contradictory. The Trusteeship System was crafted as a more robust successor to the Mandates System, designed to provide closer oversight and hasten the transition to independence for a select number of territories [[61](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e563?p=emailAGM/Ejv7bMB02&d=/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e563), [79](https://repository.law.umich.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=8653&context=mlr)]. However, its limited scope and reliance on the cooperation of administering powers rendered it a peripheral player in the broader decolonization drama. The true agency of the UN in dismantling empire lay elsewhere. The General Assembly, reshaped by the votes of newly independent states, transformed into the principal political and legal battlefield [[26](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2194737)]. Through mechanisms like the Special Committee on Decolonization and the powerful rhetoric of its resolutions, the GA turned the tables on the colonial powers. The adoption of the 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples (Resolution 1514) was the watershed moment, converting the abstract principle of self-determination into a binding norm of international law and a universal standard for judging the legitimacy of all governments [[28](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/dicc/dicc.html), [85](https://brill.com/view/journals/nord/91/2/article-p189_001.xml?language=en), [104](https://kennedyhumanrights.org/our-voices/modern-decolonization-the-role-of-the-un-and-the-responsibility-of-all-of-us/)]. In this phase, the UN shifted from managing the colonial transition to actively presiding over its conclusion.

Ultimately, decolonization did not merely add new members to international organizations; it rewrote their DNA. The infusion of the Global South's perspective replaced the East-West divide of the Cold War as the primary fault line in global politics, fostering new alignments centered on economic inequality [[92](https://diplowiki.com/g77-global-south-coordination)]. The rise of coalitions like the Group of 77 endowed developing nations with a collective voice, enabling them to champion ambitious agendas like the New International Economic Order, which sought nothing less than a fundamental restructuring of the global economy to rectify historical imbalances [[38](https://investmentpolicy.unctad.org/international-investment-agreements/treaty-files/2775/download), [41](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09614524.2025.2534149)]. Simultaneously, the instability unleashed by the end of empire compelled the UN to expand its functions, pioneering complex peacekeeping operations like ONUC in the Congo and confronting the messy reality that political independence did not guarantee state stability or security [[44](https://www.walterdorn.net/40-intelligence-and-peacekeeping-the-un-operation-in-the-congo-1960-64), [45](https://www.manchesterhive.com/display/9781526137906/9781526137906.00009.xml)]. The journey from the League's sanctioned mandates to the UN's legally binding declarations and economically transformative agendas illustrates a profound arc of change. International organizations, born of empire, were ultimately harnessed by the forces of anti-colonialism to help dismantle it, leaving behind a legacy of profound contradiction: they were both the creators and the critics, the managers and the dismantlers of the 20th century's great imperial edifice.
