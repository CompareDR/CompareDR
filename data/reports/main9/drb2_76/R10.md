# International Organizations and Decolonization in the Twentieth Century: A Historical Literature Review

## Introduction

Between 1919 and the end of the twentieth century, international organizations were simultaneously the inheritors of imperial supervisory techniques and the institutional vehicles by which decolonization reshaped the global order. This review covers the literature available through early 2022, organized in three parts: the League of Nations era (1919–1945), the early United Nations period (1945–c. 1965), and the consequences of decolonization for international organizations themselves (c. 1955–2000). Throughout, the relationship between the two is treated as a contradiction, not as either endorsement or rejection of empire. The review draws on the standard monographs and primary documents of the field, while being explicit about historiographic disagreement where it matters.

## Part One — The League of Nations Era: Dual Roles and Anti-Imperialist Counter-Currents (1919–1945)

### 1.1 The Mandates System as Imperial Preservation

The Mandates System established by Article 22 of the Covenant of the League of Nations (in force 10 January 1920) is the first international supervisory regime for colonial territory [1]. Fourteen territories detached from the defeated Ottoman and German empires were assigned to "advanced nations" — in practice, Britain, France, Belgium, Japan, and the British Dominions — to be administered on behalf of the League until their peoples were judged able to stand alone [2]. The Mandates were framed by the "sacred trust of civilisation" language of Article 22 and by two operative principles: non-annexation and tutelage for the benefit of the native population [1].

Yet the very architecture preserved imperial control. The territories were assigned not through any objective standard of "advancement" but through wartime bargains (the 1916 Sykes–Picot agreement for the former Ottoman lands, the 1919 Paris decisions for the German colonies), and the distribution of mandates closely tracked the strategic and economic interests of the victorious Allies [2]. Britain received Iraq, Palestine and Transjordan (Class A), Tanganyika and parts of Cameroons and Togoland (Class B), and a portion of the Pacific mandates; France obtained Syria and Lebanon (Class A), the bulk of Cameroons and Togoland (Class B), and the mandate for Ruanda-Urundi after 1924 [2]. The three-class scheme (A for ex-Ottoman, B for ex-German sub-Saharan Africa, C for ex-German Pacific and South West Africa) was a hierarchy of expected sovereignty, not of legal status: only Class A mandates were described in the Covenant as having reached a stage at which their "existence as independent nations can be provisionally recognised" [2].

The Permanent Mandates Commission (PMC), the supervisory body established in 1924, embodied the limits of the system. Its members were appointed by the League Council but selected from the same imperial club they were meant to oversee; it could receive annual reports and hear petitioners, but it had no enforcement powers and could not, for example, compel Britain to admit Assyrian refugees to Iraq in 1933 [3]. Susan Pedersen's *The Guardians: The League of Nations and the Crisis of Empire* (2015) frames the system as an "experiment in internationalism" that, in administrative practice, did not differ substantially from colonial administration elsewhere — the difference was mostly rhetorical [3]. The South West Africa case shows the boundary most clearly: South Africa refused to place the territory under international trusteeship after 1946, and the General Assembly's attempts to supervise it through the Committee on South West Africa ran into the formal refusal of an administering power to cooperate [4].

### 1.2 The Mandates as a Stage for International Oversight

In spite of the system's imperial character, the Mandates also created — for the first time in the history of European colonialism — a regular, public, transnational forum in which colonial administration was discussed [3]. Mandatory powers had to submit annual reports; petitioners from the territories had a recognized right of audience; the League published the reports; and the PMC's sessions in Geneva produced a body of jurisprudence on what "well-being" and "social progress" actually meant. The 1932 admission of Iraq to the League of Nations, after the Sixth Committee of the League's General Assembly reviewed the mandatory's record, is the most cited example: a Class A mandate could, in principle, graduate out of the system, and the procedure by which it did so was set by an international body [5]. Pedersen is therefore right to argue that the Mandates were not just "a cover for empire" but a venue in which imperial claims were, for the first time, required to be defended in a quasi-judicial setting — a venue that the colonized and their advocates learned to use [3].

The two readings are not mutually exclusive. The system was a compromise born of an Anglo-American desire to find a middle position between outright annexation and self-determination; the compromise preserved the substance of imperial administration while creating the techniques by which it could later be challenged. The supervisory tools of the Mandates — annual reports, petition mechanisms, public hearings, and the requirement that imperial rule justify itself in legal terms — were the direct institutional ancestors of the UN's Trusteeship Council and of Chapter XI of the UN Charter.

### 1.3 The League Against Imperialism: A Transnational Counter-Public

The most ambitious interwar attempt to organize a counter-current to the League's imperial inheritance was the League Against Imperialism (LAI), founded at the First International Congress against Colonial Oppression and Imperialism held in the Egmont Palace, Brussels, on 10–14 February 1927 [6]. The congress brought together 175 delegates, 107 of them from 37 colonized or semi-colonized countries, alongside European and American anti-colonialists and labour representatives. Jawaharlal Nehru attended as a representative of the Indian National Congress; Mohammad Hatta represented the Perhimpoenan Indonesia; Lamine Senghor, the Senegalese leader of the Comité de Défense de la Race Nègre, addressed the "Negro revendications"; Messali Hadj spoke for the North-African Star; the Chinese Kuomintang and Communist parties both sent emissaries (Liao Huanxing for the Kuomintang, Liu Puqing and Shao Lizhi as additional representatives); and on the European side, Willi Münzenberg (the German communist who chaired the Workers' International Relief and was the LAI's principal organizer) was joined by figures including Albert Einstein, Henri Barbusse, Romain Rolland, and the British Labour politician Fenner Brockway [6][7].

The congress adopted three resolutions — on the anti-imperialist struggle in China, on US intervention in Latin America, and on the "Negro revendications" — and issued a manifesto addressed "to all colonial peoples, workers and peasants of the world" calling for the organization of resistance against "imperialist ideology" [6]. The choice of the word "league" in the organization's name was, as the Indian Marxist historian Vijay Prashad notes, a direct attack on the League of Nations and on the way the Mandates System made colonial control permanent [6]. The LAI's Executive Committee included Nehru, Hatta, Senghor, the South African ANC president Josiah Tshangana Gumede, and Chatto (Virendranath Chattopadhyaya), the Indian revolutionary who had been a leading figure in the Berlin Committee during the First World War [6][7].

The LAI held a second congress in Frankfurt in July 1929, attended by 84 representatives from colonized countries, and was active in supporting anti-colonial movements throughout the late 1920s [6]. It faced two structural difficulties from the beginning. The first was that, although Münzenberg conceived the LAI as a genuine cross-political coalition, the Comintern's "third period" turn after 1928 pushed the organization in a more orthodox Communist direction and alarmed potential non-Communist allies [7]. The Second International refused to attend the Brussels congress; Jean Longuet dismissed it as "vague Sovietic chitchat" [6]. The second was police repression: in December 1931, after the Comintern's policies had made the LAI an obvious target in Germany, the Berlin headquarters were raided by the police; the organization moved briefly to Paris and then to London [7]. By the mid-1930s, the LAI was barely operational. Münzenberg was expelled from the German Communist Party and the Comintern in 1938 and was found dead in suspicious circumstances near Grenoble in October 1940 [7].

The LAI's significance is twofold. First, it was the first international forum in which the colonized met on equal terms with European and American anti-imperialists and worked out a common programme. The 1927 Brussels congress and the 1929 Frankfurt congress are recoverable moments in the formation of what would later become the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM): Nehru, Hatta, and Messali Hadj, who were in Brussels in 1927, were among the founding generation of post-1945 Third World leaders. Second, the LAI's fate illustrates a recurring pattern in interwar international politics: transnational counter-publics of the colonized were structurally vulnerable to metropolitan police repression and to the Comintern's own doctrinal zigzags. Pedersen, Antony Anghie, and the German historian Fredrik Petersson (in his monograph *Hub of the Anti-Imperialist Movement: the League against Imperialism and Berlin, 1927–1933*, 2015) treat the LAI as both the high-water mark of interwar anti-imperialist internationalism and a cautionary case of how such movements could not be sustained in the 1930s [3][7].

### 1.4 The Bridge to 1945

The interwar international order ended in two stages. The Mandates System was fatally weakened by the Italian invasion of Ethiopia (1935–1936), by Japan's departure from the League (1933), and by the PMC's inability to act against the major powers; the body limped on until the League's effective dissolution in 1946. The political logic that would replace it had already been articulated. The Atlantic Charter of 14 August 1941 — the joint declaration by Roosevelt and Churchill, issued after the Atlantic Conference at Placentia Bay — included a clause (the third) recognizing "the right of all peoples to choose their own form of government" [8]. The Charter was deliberately ambiguous about whether this right applied to the populations of the British, French, and Dutch empires; the inter-Allied meeting at St James's Palace (June 1941) and the subsequent Declaration by United Nations (1 January 1942) did not resolve the ambiguity [8][9]. By 1943, the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and China were agreed at Moscow (October 1943) that a new international organization should replace the League; at Dumbarton Oaks (August–October 1944) the four powers drafted its basic structure, and at Yalta (February 1945) they settled the voting formula of the Security Council [9]. The question of colonial territories was left for San Francisco (April–June 1945), where the discussions produced the compromise that became Chapters XI, XII, and XIII of the United Nations Charter.

## Part Two — The United Nations Era: The Trusteeship System, the General Assembly, and the 1960 Declaration (1945–c. 1965)

### 2.1 The UN's Founding Posture on Colonial Questions

The Charter did not use the word "colonial." The compromise of San Francisco dealt with colonial questions by inventing two parallel regimes: Chapter XI (Articles 73–74), a "Declaration regarding Non-Self-Governing Territories" applicable to the dependencies of the eight powers that had possessed colonies in 1945; and Chapter XII (Articles 75–85), a more intrusive "International Trusteeship System" that applied only to territories voluntarily placed under it through trusteeship agreements [10]. Article 73(e) required administering powers to transmit regularly to the Secretary-General "statistical and other information of a technical nature relating to economic, social, and educational conditions" in their non-self-governing territories — a reporting obligation that the Charter's drafters had deliberately limited to these three areas to keep "political" questions out of the General Assembly's reach [10][11].

In 1946, eight administering powers (Australia, Belgium, Denmark, France, the Netherlands, New Zealand, the United Kingdom, and the United States) submitted a list of 72 non-self-governing territories to the Secretary-General; the General Assembly, by Resolution 66 (I) of 14 December 1946, established an ad hoc committee of eight administering members and eight elected members to "examine the Secretary-General's summary and analysis of the information transmitted under Article 73 e" [11]. The committee was originally named the "ad hoc committee"; it was later renamed the Special Committee on Information Transmitted under Article 73 e, then the Committee on Information from Non-Self-Governing Territories, and was finally dissolved in 1963 when its functions were absorbed by the larger Special Committee on the Situation with regard to the Implementation of the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples (the C-24) [12]. The 1946 list was the institutional seedbed of the post-1945 anti-colonial order; without it, the General Assembly's authority over non-self-governing territories would have rested on a much thinner legal foundation.

### 2.2 The Trusteeship System and the Eleven Trust Territories

The Trusteeship System established by Chapters XII and XIII of the Charter was the direct successor of the Mandates System. Article 77 listed three categories of territory that could be placed under trusteeship: former League mandates; territories detached from enemy states as a result of the Second World War; and territories voluntarily placed under the system by the states responsible for their administration [10]. Most former mandates were converted into trust territories; the conspicuous exception was South West Africa, where South Africa refused trusteeship and the matter was to occupy the General Assembly, the Security Council, and the International Court of Justice (ICJ) for the next forty-four years [4]. The Trusteeship Council, with its unique composition (administering members, permanent SC members not administering territories, and enough elected members to balance the two), was designed to make supervision more intrusive than the PMC's had been: it could consider reports, accept petitions (after 1948, in consultation with the administering authority), and arrange periodic visits to the territories [10].

By the time the Trusteeship Council formally suspended its substantive operations in 1994, eleven territories had been placed under trusteeship: Tanganyika (UK), Cameroons under French administration (France), Cameroons under British administration (UK), Togoland under British administration (UK), Togoland under French administration (France), Western Samoa (New Zealand), Ruanda-Urundi (Belgium), New Guinea (Australia), Nauru (Australia, with the UK and New Zealand), the Trust Territory of Somaliland under Italian administration (Italy), and the Strategic Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands (United States) [12]. The last of these — Palau — became independent only in 1994, which is why the Trusteeship Council's work was officially terminated that year, although it remained formally available to meet "as and where occasion may require" [12]. Ten of the eleven territories had become independent or self-governing by 1980, mostly in the 1960s.

South West Africa stands as the test of the system. The ICJ's 1950 advisory opinion confirmed that South Africa remained bound by its League mandate and that the General Assembly had inherited the League's supervisory competence; the Court's 1955 opinion (Voting Procedure) further held that the Assembly was not bound by the unanimity rule that the League Council had used; and the 1971 advisory opinion (Legal Consequences for States of the Continued Presence of South Africa in Namibia), requested by the Security Council after Resolution 276 (1970), declared South Africa's continued presence in Namibia illegal and obliged all UN member states to refrain from recognizing South African acts in the territory [4]. Namibia finally became independent on 21 March 1990, after the implementation of Security Council Resolution 435 (1978) [4]. The case shows both the strength and the slowness of the system: the legal architecture could be made to work, but only over the course of decades, and only against an administering power that had already lost the political will to resist.

### 2.3 The General Assembly as Anti-Colonial Arena

The general drift of the historiography is that, after 1945, the centre of gravity for anti-colonial politics shifted to the UN General Assembly. The Security Council was the body the Charter endowed with the strongest powers, but its permanent members included the three colonial powers with the largest empires (Britain, France, and the United States); any serious anti-colonial resolution would have been vetoed. The General Assembly had no enforcement power, but it had near-universal membership (after 1945, plus 51 by 1960, plus 60 by 1965, plus 144 by 1980), a one-state-one-vote rule, and the practice of holding substantive debates on the issues of the day [13]. The institutional mechanisms by which the Assembly exercised this role were: the special committees (the Committee on Information from Non-Self-Governing Territories, then the C-24); the Fourth Committee of the General Assembly, which handled decolonization items; the annual flood of resolutions on specific territories; and the practice of inviting petitioner-deputies from the colonized peoples — a continuation of the PMC's procedures adapted to a larger body [11][12].

The Assembly's authority to decide which territories were subject to Chapter XI's reporting obligation was an early and consequential expansion of its power. When Portugal joined the UN in 1955, it claimed that its African territories (Angola, Mozambique, Guinea-Bissau) were not "non-self-governing" because they were "overseas provinces" of the Portuguese state; the General Assembly rejected this claim and eventually placed the territories on the list. Similarly, Southern Rhodesia was added to the list in 1963, and the Assembly refused to accept the administering powers' attempts to remove territories from the list on the ground of having "integrated" them with the metropolitan state — as with Alaska, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and Greenland, the cases where the Assembly did accept the cessation of information [11][12].

### 2.4 The 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples

Resolution 1514 (XV), adopted by the General Assembly on 14 December 1960 by 89 votes to none, with 9 abstentions, is the central document of UN-era decolonization [14]. The vote broke down as follows: in favor, 89 states (almost all of the recently independent African and Asian countries, the Soviet bloc, and most of Latin America); abstaining, Australia, Belgium, the Dominican Republic, France, Portugal, Spain, the Union of South Africa, the United Kingdom, and the United States [14]. The abstention of the United States, on which the Eisenhower administration insisted under pressure from London, was particularly striking; the US had supported decolonization rhetorically since 1941, and Senator Wayne Morse denounced the abstention as a sign that the State Department had "bent over backwards to read the paragraph incorrectly" [14].

The Declaration's text is brief — seven operative paragraphs — and the operative content is unambiguous. Paragraph 1 declares that "the subjection of peoples to alien subjugation, domination and exploitation constitutes a denial of fundamental human rights, is contrary to the Charter of the United Nations and is an impediment to the promotion of world peace and cooperation." Paragraph 2 affirms "the right of self-determination" and the right of all peoples "freely to determine their political status and freely to pursue their economic, social and cultural development." Paragraph 3 states that "inadequacy of political, economic, social or educational preparedness must never serve as a pretext for delaying the moment of independence." Paragraph 4 calls for an end to "all armed action or repressive measures of all kinds directed against dependent peoples." Paragraph 5 calls for the immediate transfer of powers in trust and non-self-governing territories. Paragraph 6, anticipating the post-decolonial state-collapse cases (notably the Congo, whose crisis had begun in July 1960), warns that "any attempt aimed at the partial or total disruption of the national unity and the territorial integrity of a country is incompatible with the purposes and principles of the Charter." Paragraph 7 binds all states to observe the Charter, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and the present Declaration [14].

Resolution 1514 was accompanied by Resolution 1541 (XV) of 15 December 1960, which laid down three "modes" by which a non-self-governing territory could be said to have reached "a full measure of self-government": emergence as a sovereign independent State; free association with an independent State; or integration with an independent State [15]. This second resolution is the technical-legal annex that allowed France, the United Kingdom, and others to argue that they had not opposed self-determination in principle. Together, the two resolutions constitute the legal backbone of the General Assembly's decolonization work for the next three decades.

The historiography on Resolution 1514 is unusual in that it is broadly agreed that the vote was not a moral triumph but a political fact: by 1960, anti-colonial states already constituted an absolute majority in the General Assembly. Adom Getachew's *Worldmaking After Empire: The Rise and Fall of Self-Determination* (2019) argues that the Declaration was not merely a recognition of nation-state sovereignty but a constitutive moment in which anti-colonial nationalists — Nnamdi Azikiwe, W. E. B. Du Bois, George Padmore, Kwame Nkrumah, Eric Williams, Michael Manley, Julius Nyerere — sought to "make a world" through the United Nations that transcended the racial and economic hierarchies of the existing order [16]. Christian Reus-Smit's 2011 article in *International Organization* argues that Resolution 1514 "produced a tectonic shift in international legitimacy" by "successfully undermining the institution of empire" [14]. The drafting history of the resolution — originally proposed by the Soviet Union in a more radical form, then reworked by 43 Asian and African states into the form that was adopted — confirms that the document was a compromise, but a compromise that, by its vote count, no colonial power was prepared to oppose directly.

### 2.5 The Special Committee on Decolonization (C-24)

Resolution 1654 (XVI) of 27 November 1961 established the Special Committee on the Situation with regard to the Implementation of the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples — the C-24 — to monitor the implementation of Resolution 1514 [12]. The Committee originally had 17 members, which was expanded to 24 by the end of 1962; it has remained the principal organ through which the General Assembly exercises day-to-day supervision of decolonization [12]. The C-24 took over the work of the dissolved Committee on Information from Non-Self-Governing Territories in 1963 (under Resolution 1970 (XVIII)) and produced a revised list of 64 non-self-governing territories to which the 1960 Declaration applied, including the two remaining trust territories (Nauru and the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands), the non-self-governing territories of the existing administering powers, the territories of Portugal, Namibia, and Southern Rhodesia [12]. From 1960 to 2002, 54 territories attained self-government, and 17 non-self-governing territories remain on the Assembly's list as of the early 2020s [12].

The C-24's work is sometimes dismissed as a talking-shop, but in institutional terms it has been a continuous presence. Its annual reports, its working papers on individual territories, and the regular pattern of its visits and hearings have kept the question of decolonization on the General Assembly's agenda long after the largest waves of independence had passed. Its refusal to allow the administering powers to remove territories from the list unilaterally (Portugal tried to do this in 1960, the United Kingdom attempted it for Gibraltar in the 1960s, France and the Netherlands have tried it for their overseas territories) is the institutional mechanism by which the Assembly's authority over the list was consolidated.

## Part Three — Decolonization and the Transformation of International Organizations (c. 1955–2000)

### 3.1 The "Year of Africa" and the Reshaping of UN Membership

Between 1945 and 1955, the United Nations had 76 members; by 1960, 99; by 1970, 127; by 1980, 154; by 1990, 159; by 2000, 189 [13]. The crucial inflection point was 1960, the "Year of Africa" — 17 African countries gained independence, and 16 of them, plus Cyprus, were admitted to the UN in September of that year [17]. The same year saw the adoption of Resolution 1514. Adom Getachew, in her contribution to a 2020 *New York Times* interactive feature on the Year of Africa, frames the moment as a victory for the Black Atlantic project of "worldmaking" — the use of UN membership as a platform not only for national independence but for the construction of an alternative international order [18]. Whatever the framing, the demographic transformation of the General Assembly is the basic fact without which the rest of Part Three is unintelligible.

The Security Council was less affected. The Council's permanent membership, set in 1945, was not expanded, but the number of non-permanent seats was raised from 6 to 10 by the Charter amendment of 17 December 1963 (in force 31 August 1965), and ECOSOC's membership was raised from 18 to 27 at the same time [13]. These constitutional changes were a direct response to the increase in UN membership and a recognition that the smaller organs could not be left at their 1945 sizes. The 1965 amendments did not, however, change the Council's geography: the non-permanent seats have remained dominated by regional groups whose internal politics, as the UN Chronicle's later retrospectives observe, are themselves a legacy of the decolonization process [17].

### 3.2 From East-West to North-South

The conventional shorthand is that the post-1945 international system was organized around an "East-West" cleavage (the Cold War between the United States and the Soviet Union) and that, in the 1960s and 1970s, a new "North-South" cleavage (industrialized versus developing countries) became at least equally important. The shorthand is correct in its broad outlines but misleading in its implication that the North-South cleavage replaced the East-West one. The two coexisted, and the political skill of the Non-Aligned Movement was to position itself between them. The Brandt Commission's report of 1980, *North-South: A Programme for Survival*, popularized the terminology and made it common in diplomatic usage, but the cleavage it named was already fifteen years old by the time the report was published [19].

The shift in agenda items, from Cold War security questions to questions of development, commodity prices, and the structure of the international economic order, was the substantive content of the North-South turn. By the mid-1960s, UNCTAD (the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development) had become a permanent organ of the General Assembly; UNIDO (the United Nations Industrial Development Organization) was established in 1966; UNEP (the United Nations Environment Programme) was created in 1972; and the 1972 Stockholm Conference on the Human Environment had taken the North-South cleavage as one of its organizing themes. None of these would have been politically possible without the post-1955 transformation of the General Assembly's membership.

### 3.3 The Non-Aligned Movement

The Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) was the political expression of the post-1955 majority. Its immediate antecedent was the Asian-African Conference at Bandung, Indonesia, 18–24 April 1955, attended by 29 governments representing more than half the world's population; the conference adopted the "Ten Principles of Bandung," which included respect for sovereignty, non-aggression, non-interference, equality, and peaceful co-existence [20]. The first NAM summit was held in Belgrade on 1–6 September 1961, attended by 25 countries: Afghanistan, Algeria, Burma, Cambodia, Ceylon, the Congo (Leopoldville), Cuba, Cyprus, Egypt, Ethiopia, Ghana, Guinea, India, Indonesia, Iraq, Lebanon, Mali, Morocco, Nepal, Saudi Arabia, Somalia, Sudan, Tunisia, Yemen, and Yugoslavia [20]. The five architects — Tito of Yugoslavia, Nasser of Egypt, Nehru of India, Sukarno of Indonesia, and Nkrumah of Ghana — were already, with the partial exception of Nehru, deeply engaged in the process of decolonization; the 1961 Belgrade summit took place four months after the 1960 Declaration.

NAM's initial coherence rested on the Cold War: the member states were formally non-bloc, but the conference's specific concerns (anti-colonialism, opposition to apartheid, disarmament, decolonization) reflected a Southern agenda. The Algiers summit of September 1973 turned NAM decisively toward economic questions: President Houari Boumédiène of Algeria hosted the conference and made a major speech calling for a "new international economic order," and the Algiers economic declaration became one of the principal intellectual inputs into the Sixth Special Session of the UN General Assembly in 1974 [21]. The 1973 Algiers summit was the moment when NAM made the structural economic critique of the post-1945 order the centerpiece of its agenda.

NAM's historiography is divided. Some scholars (notably Robert Mortimer in *The Third World Coalition in International Politics*, 1984) treat the movement as a coherent diplomatic actor; others (Mark Berger; Christopher Clapham) emphasize the heterogeneity of its members and the limited concrete results of its summits. The truth is somewhere in between. NAM provided the diplomatic cover under which the Group of 77 could pursue its economic agenda in UN forums; it was less effective as an autonomous diplomatic actor. By the early 2020s NAM had over 120 members, but its meetings had become mostly ritual.

### 3.4 The Group of 77 and UNCTAD

The Group of 77 (G-77) was established on 15 June 1964 at the conclusion of the first United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD I) in Geneva, by 77 developing countries that signed the "Joint Declaration of the Seventy-Seven Developing Countries" [22]. The Joint Declaration was the founding document of a new economic coalition; UNCTAD itself, established as a permanent organ of the General Assembly in 1964, was its institutional home. Raúl Prebisch, the Argentine economist and Executive Secretary of the UN Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC, 1950–1963), became UNCTAD's first Secretary-General (1964–1969), and his structuralist analysis of the world economy — the argument that primary-commodity exporters were trapped in a long-term deterioration of their terms of trade — was the intellectual foundation of the G-77's program [22].

The G-77 grew rapidly. By May 2013, it had 134 members; today it has 134 (with China as a close collaborator but not a formal member) [22]. It operates without a permanent secretariat; its institutional work is done through annual ministerial meetings, regional chapters, and a rotating chairmanship. Its major declarations — the Charter of Algiers (1967), the Lima Declaration (1971), the Manila Declaration (1976), and the Arusha Programme (1979) — set out, in increasing detail, the G-77's position on commodities, trade, finance, industrialization, technology transfer, and the regulation of transnational corporations [22]. The Charter of Algiers of 24–25 October 1967 was particularly important: it gave the G-77 an institutional charter and established the principle that the Group was to be the principal organ through which the developing countries negotiated economic questions within the UN system.

The G-77's intellectual debt to the dependency-theory tradition (Prebisch, Hans Singer, the Brazilian Celso Furtado, the Ghanaian Kwame Nkrumah, the Egyptian Samir Amin) is widely noted. What is sometimes missed is that the G-77 was as much a diplomatic coalition as an economic program. The 77 signatories of 1964 were held together less by a shared economic analysis than by a shared sense that the existing international economic order had been designed without their participation and that this exclusion was a problem they could collectively address.

### 3.5 The New International Economic Order (NIEO)

The NIEO was the G-77's most ambitious attempt to translate political decolonization into a structural economic program. Its origins lay in the 1973 oil shock (OPEC's quadrupling of the posted price of crude from about $3 to $12 a barrel), which demonstrated that commodity producers could, in favorable circumstances, reverse the terms of trade against the industrial world [23]. The 1973 Algiers NAM summit gave the Southern coalition a political platform; UNCTAD III (Santiago, 1972) and the 1973 Non-Aligned summit gave it an economic program; and the Sixth Special Session of the General Assembly (9 April – 2 May 1974, the first ever devoted to economic rather than security questions) gave it an institutional setting.

The Sixth Special Session produced two resolutions adopted without a vote on 1 May 1974: the Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order (Resolution 3201 (S-VI)) and the Programme of Action (Resolution 3202 (S-VI)) [23]. The Declaration's central claim was that "the gap between the developed and the developing countries continues to widen in a system which was established at a time when most of the developing countries did not even exist as independent States and which perpetuates inequality." Its specific demands included permanent sovereignty over natural resources; the regulation of transnational corporations; a just and equitable relationship between the prices of raw materials exported by developing countries and the prices of manufactured goods they imported; the restructuring of the international monetary system; and the strengthening of the UN system as a forum for the resolution of economic disputes [23]. The Seventh Special Session of September 1975 (Resolution 3362 (S-VII)) reaffirmed the NIEO and called for a series of negotiations.

The Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States, adopted as Resolution 3281 (XXIX) on 12 December 1974 by 120 votes to 6 with 10 abstentions, was the NIEO's attempt to convert its political declaration into something approaching law [24]. The Charter's Article 2 affirmed "full permanent sovereignty, including possession, use and disposal, over all its wealth, natural resources and economic activities" and asserted each state's right to "regulate and exercise authority over foreign investment within its national jurisdiction" and to "regulate and supervise the activities of transnational corporations" [24]. The Charter was proposed by the Mexican President Luis Echeverría at UNCTAD III in 1972 and was drafted by a UN working group from 1973 to 1974. The United States, the United Kingdom, West Germany, Belgium, Denmark, and Luxembourg voted against; France, Italy, Japan, Canada, the Netherlands, and others abstained — a vote that signaled, in advance, the Charter's limited binding effect [24].

The NIEO's substantive achievements were modest. The General Assembly's resolution of 1962 (1803 (XVII)) had already established the principle of permanent sovereignty over natural resources; the NIEO extended the principle to a broader program of structural reform [23]. The 1974–1977 Conference on International Economic Co-operation (CIEC, also known as the North-South Conference or the Paris Conference) was the principal forum in which the NIEO was negotiated with the industrial countries, and its failure to produce an agreement was a major setback. The 1981 Cancún Summit (22–23 October 1981), held at Brandt's initiative, brought together 22 heads of state but produced no concrete results [19]. By 1983 the Brandt Commission's second report, *Common Crisis*, was already acknowledging that none of the major NIEO proposals had been implemented [19].

The NIEO's decline is usually attributed to four causes, which the literature treats as cumulative rather than mutually exclusive. First, the 1980s debt crisis and the rise of US interest rates in 1979–1981 forced most debtor states to negotiate with the IMF and the World Bank under conditions of fiscal emergency; the structural adjustment programs that resulted subordinated NIEO priorities to short-term balance-of-payments management. Second, the Reagan administration's hostility to the NIEO (and the broader neoliberal turn in the US and UK) removed the political space for the kind of structural reform the NIEO demanded. Third, the WTO's Uruguay Round (1986–1994) created a trade regime in which the G-77's preferences were institutionalized in a way that, the G-77 argued, locked in the existing asymmetries. Fourth, the G-77's own internal heterogeneity (OPEC versus non-OPEC, big emerging economies versus least developed countries, commodity exporters versus manufactures exporters) prevented the coalition from acting with the unity that the NIEO's ambitious agenda required.

### 3.6 UN Peacekeeping and the Colonial Inheritance

UN peacekeeping was not in the Charter. It emerged, in the years after 1948, as a pragmatic workaround for the East-West paralysis of the Security Council. The first UN peacekeeping mission was the United Nations Truce Supervision Organization (UNTSO), authorized by the Security Council on 29 May 1948 to monitor the Arab-Israeli armistice [25]. The United Nations Military Observer Group in India and Pakistan (UNMOGIP), established in January 1949 to monitor the ceasefire in Kashmir, has continued in operation since. Both were unarmed observer missions; the innovation of the lightly armed peacekeeping force came in 1956.

The Suez Crisis of October–November 1956 was the first major test of UN capacity in a great-power context. After the British, French, and Israeli intervention in Egypt, the General Assembly established the First United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF I) under the Canadian Secretary of State for External Affairs Lester B. Pearson's proposal, and the Canadian diplomat was awarded the 1957 Nobel Peace Prize for the idea [25]. UNEF I was the model for all subsequent "traditional" peacekeeping: consent of the parties, impartiality, and use of force only in self-defense. The model is the institutional ancestor of the modern UN peacekeeping system.

The UN's experience in the Congo (the United Nations Operation in the Congo, ONUC) was, however, the moment at which the limits of the UNEF I model became visible. ONUC was authorized by Security Council Resolution 143 of 14 July 1960, four days after the Force publique had mutinied and the mining province of Katanga had declared independence under Moïse Tshombe, with Belgian support [25]. The mission grew to nearly 20,000 troops at its peak and was led, in its early phase, by UN Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld. ONUC was mandated initially to ensure the withdrawal of Belgian forces and to assist the Congolese government in restoring law and order; it was progressively expanded, against the wishes of the Soviet Union, to include the use of force to end the Katangese secession. The mission's evolution from a peacekeeping to a peace-enforcement posture is the central case study of the gap between the two concepts in UN practice.

The Soviet Union's response to the expanded ONUC mandate, and to Hammarskjöld's death in a plane crash near Ndola, Northern Rhodesia, on 18 September 1961, led to a constitutional crisis at the UN. In 1964–1965, the Soviet Union and France refused to pay their assessed contributions for ONUC and two other peacekeeping missions, precipitating the famous "Article 19 crisis" of whether a member state in arrears could lose its General Assembly vote [25]. The crisis was resolved by a procedural compromise in 1965 that left the underlying question unresolved but enabled the UN to continue operating. Hammarskjöld's death and the Article 19 crisis together established the political limits within which UN peacekeeping would operate for the next quarter-century: the major powers would tolerate (and even fund) peacekeeping in the third world, but not the use of force against the interests of any one of them.

The only other UN peacekeeping operation directly related to decolonization in the period under review was the UN Security Force in West New Guinea (UNSF, 1962–1963), which administered the territory of West New Guinea during its transfer from Dutch to Indonesian sovereignty under the terms of the 1962 New York Agreement [25]. The 1965 intervention in the Dominican Republic (DOMREP) was the first time the UN operated alongside a regional organization (the OAS) in a way that effectively subordinated the UN mission to a US-led operation; it is usually treated as a precedent for the limits of UN action in Cold War proxy conflicts rather than as a decolonization operation.

The Nobel Peace Prize awarded to UN peacekeepers in 1988 marked the international community's recognition of peacekeeping as a distinct UN function. By that time, more than 70 peacekeeping operations had been established, of which 13 were still active. After the end of the Cold War, the Security Council's paralysis gave way to a dramatic expansion: more peacekeeping operations were authorized between 1991 and 1994 than in the previous 45 years combined [25]. The post-Cold War surge was not, however, a return to the East-West or North-South patterns of the earlier period; it was driven by intra-state conflicts (Somalia, Bosnia, Rwanda, Cambodia) in which the Cold War's proxy logic no longer applied.

### 3.7 Reform of the UN System in Response to Membership Change

The decolonization process produced changes in the UN system beyond the General Assembly's agenda. The 1963 expansion of the Security Council and ECOSOC was one; the creation of UNCTAD (1964), UNIDO (1966), and UNEP (1972) were others [13]. The Group of 77's success in institutionalizing the North-South agenda at the UN depended in large part on its control of the General Assembly's agenda-setting process after the mid-1960s. By the 1970s, the United Nations had become the principal international forum for the discussion of development, and most of the new international economic institutions of the 1970s (the UN Conference on Trade and Development; the Common Fund for Commodities, agreed in 1980 after prolonged G-77 pressure; the various regional development banks' soft-loan windows) were either created by, or heavily shaped by, the post-colonial members of the General Assembly.

The UN system's evolution was, however, constrained by the structure of the post-1945 economic order, which gave the United States and Western Europe a continuing dominant position in the IMF, the World Bank, and (after 1995) the WTO. The G-77's demands for restructuring these institutions (e.g., for a more equitable distribution of voting power in the IMF, or for a more development-oriented trade regime) were largely unmet. The persistence of the Bretton Woods institutions in their 1944 form, even after the Bretton Woods par-value system had itself collapsed in 1971–1973, is the principal institutional fact that the NIEO's advocates had to come to terms with.

## Conclusion: The Contradiction at the Heart of the Story

The history of international organizations in the twentieth century, viewed through the lens of decolonization, is the history of a contradiction. The League of Nations' Mandates System was at once a tool of imperial preservation and the first international supervisory regime for colonial territory; the League Against Imperialism was at once the first genuine transnational forum of the colonized and an organization structurally vulnerable to the politics of the Comintern and the police of the European powers. The UN Trusteeship Council was at once a continuation of the Mandates System and a more intrusive supervisory body; the General Assembly was at once the principal anti-colonial arena of the post-1945 order and a body whose authority over non-self-governing territories depended on a reporting obligation (Article 73(e)) that the administering powers themselves had largely defined. The Non-Aligned Movement was at once the political expression of the post-1955 majority and a coalition that depended on Cold War non-alignment for its coherence. The Group of 77 was at once the most successful coalition of developing countries in the history of international economic negotiations and a coalition whose internal heterogeneity prevented it from capitalizing on its institutional victories. The NIEO was at once a serious attempt to translate political decolonization into a structural economic program and a program that was largely defeated by the very economic forces (the debt crisis, the neoliberal turn, the persistence of the Bretton Woods institutions) that it had been designed to address. UN peacekeeping was at once a creative institutional workaround for the East-West paralysis and a system whose first major application (the Congo) revealed the gap between peacekeeping and peace enforcement that has continued to haunt the UN's interventions.

What is historically settled, on the evidence available through early 2022, is the broad institutional story: the dual role of the League in preserving and supervising empire; the continuity between the Mandates System and the UN Trusteeship System; the post-1955 transformation of the General Assembly; the 1960 Declaration as the political codification of an already existing power shift; the rise of the NAM and the G77 as the principal political and economic expressions of the post-colonial majority; the NIEO's failure to restructure the international economic order; and the institutional innovations (UNCTAD, UNIDO, UNEP, the C-24) that the new majority forced onto the UN system.

What remains contested is the broader meaning. Susan Pedersen emphasizes the contingent, often contradictory, character of the Mandates System and warns against reading it as either a cover for empire or as a proto-decolonization; Antony Anghie's *Imperialism, Sovereignty and the Making of International Law* (2005) reads the Mandates and the UN Charter together as the foundation of a sovereign-territorial form that ultimately served the reproduction of imperial structures; Adom Getachew treats the 1960 Declaration and the NIEO as a coherent anti-colonial worldmaking project, even though that project did not succeed on its own terms. These are not exclusive readings. The literature through early 2022, taken together, is best understood as an account of how a system designed to manage decolonization was gradually, and incompletely, transformed by the political force of the decolonizing world — and of how, in the process, both the system and the world that produced it were reshaped.
