# International Organizations and Decolonization in the Twentieth Century: A Research Report

## Executive Summary

The relationship between international organizations and decolonization was neither simply emancipatory nor simply imperial. My assessment is that twentieth-century international organizations became important to decolonization **less because they were originally designed to dismantle empire than because they created procedures, vocabularies, and arenas that anti-colonial actors later used against imperial rule**. The League of Nations Mandates System preserved many imperial practices, but it also made colonial administration internationally visible. The United Nations inherited this ambiguity: its Charter did not explicitly proclaim “decolonization,” yet its provisions on trusteeship and non-self-governing territories became the legal and institutional foundation for a powerful anti-colonial politics centered in the General Assembly. After 1960, the entry of newly independent states transformed the UN from a postwar organization dominated by great powers and empires into a forum where the “global South” pressed claims about sovereignty, development, racial equality, and economic justice.

## Part One: From World War I to World War II — The League of Nations, Empire, and Anti-Imperial Internationalism

### 1. The Mandates System as an Imperial Compromise

The League of Nations Mandates System emerged after World War I as a compromise between outright annexation of defeated empires’ territories and Woodrow Wilson’s language of trusteeship and self-determination. Article 22 of the Covenant of the League of Nations framed the administration of former German and Ottoman territories as a “sacred trust of civilisation,” formally rejecting annexation and claiming that mandatory powers would administer territories for the benefit of their inhabitants. In practice, however, this formula allowed victorious imperial powers to retain substantial control over territories that might otherwise have been treated as colonies outright ([League of Nations mandate, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations_mandate)).

The mandates were divided into three categories, reflecting a paternalist hierarchy of “development.” Class A mandates, mainly former Ottoman Arab territories, were described as closest to provisional independence; Class B mandates, mainly former German African colonies, were placed under more direct administrative tutelage; and Class C mandates, including South West Africa and Pacific island territories, could be governed almost as integral parts of the mandatory power. This structure made the distinction between “mandate” and “colony” highly unstable, especially in Class C territories where control often resembled annexation ([The Nation, n.d.](https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/imperialism-with-an-internationalist-face/)).

| Mandate Class | Typical Territories | Official Logic | Practical Problem |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Class A | Former Ottoman territories such as Palestine, Syria, Mesopotamia/Iraq | Near readiness for independence | Britain and France often retained decisive power and suppressed revolts |
| Class B | Tanganyika, Ruanda-Urundi, Cameroon, Togoland | Welfare and protection under tutelage | Economic exploitation and forced labor issues persisted |
| Class C | South West Africa, Western Samoa, Pacific mandates | Administered under laws of mandatory power | Frequently resembled colonial annexation |

The Mandates System therefore maintained imperial interests in several ways. First, mandatory powers retained day-to-day administrative control. Second, the League’s Permanent Mandates Commission had limited executive power and relied heavily on reports, dialogue, and moral pressure rather than direct enforcement. Third, no firm deadlines for independence were established. These limits meant that the system could legitimate continued empire while presenting it in internationalist language ([League of Nations mandate, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations_mandate)).

### 2. The League as an Unintended Stage for Colonial Scrutiny

The League’s role was nevertheless not reducible to imperial preservation. The Permanent Mandates Commission created a new form of international oversight by requiring mandatory powers to justify their conduct before an international audience. This did not amount to democratic self-government for colonized peoples, but it changed the diplomatic environment: empire now had to be explained, defended, and documented in Geneva.

Petitioning became one of the most important mechanisms through which local actors engaged the mandate system. Petitions allowed inhabitants and political groups to complain about administrative abuses, land policy, forced labor, repression, or exclusion. Yet the process was tightly constrained. Petitioners generally could not challenge the basic premise that they were not yet capable of full self-rule, and petitions often had to pass through the very mandatory authorities they criticized. The Permanent Mandates Commission did not function as a court; its treatment of petitions was political, selective, and dependent on the interests of League members and mandatory powers ([The Nation, n.d.](https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/imperialism-with-an-internationalist-face/)).

The tension was visible in South West Africa, where South Africa treated the mandate much like a colony, and in Middle Eastern mandates, where British and French repression undermined the legitimacy of the system. Susan Pedersen’s analysis, summarized in the Toynbee Prize Foundation interview, emphasizes that the mandate system was not “decolonization avant la lettre,” but it inadvertently opened space for new claims about statehood, international accountability, and self-government ([Toynbee Prize Foundation, n.d.](https://toynbeeprize.org/posts/susan-pedersen/)).

My view is that the Mandates System’s historical importance lies in this contradiction: it preserved empire institutionally, but it delegitimized purely private imperial sovereignty by making colonial rule an international matter. This was not enough to free colonized peoples, but it gave later anti-colonial movements a language of international accountability.

### 3. Anti-Imperialist Organizations and the League Against Imperialism

The interwar period also saw the rise of anti-imperialist organizations that stood outside, and often against, the League of Nations. Among the most important was the League Against Imperialism and Colonial Oppression, commonly known as the League Against Imperialism. It was founded in Brussels in 1927 as a transnational anti-imperialist organization bringing together colonial activists, left-wing intellectuals, labor organizers, and figures linked to anti-colonial nationalist movements. Its very name was a challenge to the League of Nations, which many anti-imperialists saw as preserving colonial rule through the Mandates System ([League Against Imperialism and Colonial Oppression, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_Against_Imperialism_and_Colonial_Oppression)).

The League Against Imperialism should be understood as part of a wider interwar world of pan-African, pan-Asian, socialist, communist, and nationalist organizing. Its founding reflected dissatisfaction with a global order in which colonized peoples had little direct representation in official international institutions. Unlike the League of Nations, which was composed of states and imperial powers, the League Against Imperialism sought to connect movements and activists across imperial boundaries.

Its main activities included conferences, propaganda, networking among anti-colonial leaders, and efforts to frame colonialism as a global system rather than a series of isolated imperial possessions. The organization was influenced by the Communist International, but it was not only a communist body; it attracted a wider range of anti-colonial activists. It declined by the mid-1930s, especially as Popular Front strategies shifted communist priorities toward anti-fascist alliances in Europe ([League Against Imperialism and Colonial Oppression, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_Against_Imperialism_and_Colonial_Oppression)).

The League Against Imperialism’s long-term influence is best seen in its relationship to later Afro-Asian solidarity. Sukarno explicitly identified it as an intellectual and organizational predecessor to the 1955 Bandung Conference. Yet there was also a major discontinuity: the League Against Imperialism represented a more diffuse, de-territorialized anti-imperialism, whereas Bandung increasingly located decolonization and post-colonial legitimacy in sovereign territorial nation-states ([Shield, n.d.](https://journal.ugm.ac.id/globalsouth/article/view/103842)).

## Part Two: The United Nations After World War II — From Trusteeship to Decolonization

### 1. The UN’s Initial Ambiguity on Colonial Questions

The United Nations Charter did not explicitly use the term “decolonization.” Instead, colonial issues were addressed through three institutional and legal frameworks: Chapter XI on Non-Self-Governing Territories, Chapter XII on the International Trusteeship System, and Chapter XIII on the Trusteeship Council ([United Nations Dag Hammarskjöld Library, n.d.](https://research.un.org/en/docs/decolonization)).

This absence of the word “decolonization” reflected the political balance of 1945. The UN was created by victorious powers, several of which still possessed large empires. The Charter therefore did not immediately declare a universal right to colonial independence. It did, however, introduce language that anti-colonial states and movements later expanded. Chapter XI recognized that administering powers had responsibilities toward territories whose peoples had “not yet attained a full measure of self-government.” Article 73 declared that the interests of inhabitants were paramount and that administering members accepted a “sacred trust” to promote their political, economic, social, and educational advancement, develop self-government, and protect them against abuses ([United Nations Office of Legal Affairs, 2016](https://legal.un.org/repertory/art73.shtml)).

### 2. From League Mandates to UN Trusteeship

The UN Trusteeship System developed partly out of the League Mandates System. Some former League mandates became UN trust territories, while other territories detached from defeated Axis powers also entered trusteeship. The Trusteeship Council supervised these territories more directly than the League’s Permanent Mandates Commission had supervised mandates. However, the system applied only to a limited number of territories. The Trusteeship Council was active from 1946 to 1994, dealt with only 11 trust territories, and suspended regular activity after Palau became independent in 1994 ([United Nations Dag Hammarskjöld Library, n.d.](https://research.un.org/en/docs/decolonization)).

The distinction between trust territories and non-self-governing territories is crucial. Trust territories were subject to direct UN supervision through the Trusteeship Council. Non-Self-Governing Territories under Chapter XI, by contrast, were mainly governed through a reporting obligation: administering states were required under Article 73(e) to transmit statistical and technical information to the Secretary-General on economic, social, and educational conditions, subject to security and constitutional limitations. Territories under Chapters XII and XIII were excluded from Article 73(e) because they belonged to the trusteeship framework ([United Nations Office of Legal Affairs, 2016](https://legal.un.org/repertory/art73.shtml)).

| Feature | League Mandates | UN Trust Territories | UN Non-Self-Governing Territories |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | Article 22, League Covenant | UN Charter Chapters XII–XIII | UN Charter Chapter XI |
| Supervisory body | Permanent Mandates Commission | Trusteeship Council | General Assembly committees and reporting system |
| Main mechanism | Reports, petitions, scrutiny | Direct UN supervision | Article 73(e) information transmission |
| Independence obligation | Uneven and often vague | Stronger movement toward self-government/independence | Initially limited, later expanded by GA practice |
| Scope | Former German/Ottoman territories | 11 trust territories | Broader colonial/dependent territories |

The UN framework therefore marked both continuity and change. It inherited the language of “sacred trust,” but it gradually became more explicitly anti-colonial as newly independent states used the General Assembly to reinterpret the Charter.

### 3. Article 73 and the Reporting System

Article 73 was central to this transformation. It required administering powers to promote advancement, develop self-government, respect local cultures, protect inhabitants from abuses, and transmit information to the UN. Article 73(e) was especially important because it created a regular flow of information from colonial administrations to the Secretary-General. The UN Repertory provides historical materials covering the operation of Article 73 across successive periods, including 1945–1954, 1954–1955, and 1955–1959, making it a key source for tracing how the reporting system worked before the great wave of 1960s decolonization ([United Nations Office of Legal Affairs, 2016](https://legal.un.org/repertory/art73.shtml)).

At first, this reporting system was weak. It depended on administering powers’ cooperation and focused on technical information rather than direct political supervision. Over time, however, the General Assembly turned Article 73(e) into a broader accountability mechanism. In 1946, the General Assembly adopted Resolution 66(I) on transmission of information under Article 73(e), and a list of 72 Non-Self-Governing Territories was compiled from reports by administering states. Later committees reviewed this information and connected it to broader claims about self-government and independence ([United Nations Dag Hammarskjöld Library, n.d.](https://research.un.org/en/docs/decolonization)).

The Committee on Information from Non-Self-Governing Territories, operating between 1947 and 1963, became an important institutional bridge. Although formally concerned with information, it gave anti-colonial states a forum to question colonial administration and pressure administering powers. According to the Max Planck Encyclopedia entry on Non-Self-Governing Territories, the General Assembly gradually transformed Chapter XI from a relatively modest declaration into a “real system of close supervision” over the administration of such territories ([Mbengue, 2013](https://access.archive-ouverte.unige.ch/access/metadata/1e633402-806d-40ba-97f1-9598e84d2ba7/download)).

### 4. The General Assembly as the Main Arena of Anti-Colonial Struggle

The General Assembly became the key arena for decolonization because each member state had one vote and no permanent member veto applied. This made it structurally different from the Security Council. As Asian and African states entered the UN, they gained the numbers necessary to challenge colonial powers diplomatically. India and other anti-colonial states used the Assembly to chip away at administering powers’ claims of exclusive control over dependent territories ([Cornell University Press, n.d.](https://cornellpress.cornell.edu/un-support-for-decolonization-sixty-years-on/)).

The transformation accelerated in 1960, the “Year of Africa,” when 17 African states joined the UN. These new members helped create the voting majority that made a more assertive decolonization agenda possible. Forty-three African and Asian states sponsored the draft that became Resolution 1514(XV), demonstrating that decolonization had become a collective project of the postcolonial and still-colonized world rather than a matter controlled by imperial powers ([Cornell University Press, n.d.](https://cornellpress.cornell.edu/un-support-for-decolonization-sixty-years-on/)).

### 5. The 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples

The 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples, General Assembly Resolution 1514(XV), was a turning point. Adopted on 14 December 1960 by 89 votes in favor, none against, and nine abstentions, it declared that the subjection of peoples to alien domination was contrary to fundamental human rights and affirmed the right of all peoples to self-determination. The abstaining states included several administering or colonial powers, such as Australia, Belgium, France, Portugal, South Africa, the United Kingdom, and the United States ([United Nations Audiovisual Library of International Law, n.d.](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/dicc/dicc.html)).

The Declaration’s main content included several principles:

1. Colonial domination was a denial of fundamental human rights.
2. All peoples had the right to self-determination.
3. Inadequate political, economic, social, or educational preparedness should not be used as a pretext to delay independence.
4. Immediate steps should be taken to transfer powers to peoples in trust and non-self-governing territories.
5. Attempts to disrupt national unity and territorial integrity were condemned.

Its historical significance was threefold. First, it made decolonization a central UN project even though the Charter had not explicitly used the term. Second, it shifted legitimacy away from imperial tutelage and toward sovereign equality. Third, it linked political self-determination to later claims about economic sovereignty, including permanent sovereignty over natural resources and demands for a New International Economic Order ([United Nations Audiovisual Library of International Law, n.d.](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/dicc/dicc.html)).

In 1961, the General Assembly adopted Resolution 1654(XVI), establishing the Special Committee on the Situation with regard to the Implementation of the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples, commonly known as the Committee of Twenty-Four or C-24. This committee became the broad supervisory mechanism for remaining colonial questions and continues to consider Non-Self-Governing Territories ([United Nations Dag Hammarskjöld Library, n.d.](https://research.un.org/en/docs/decolonization)).

## Part Three: How Decolonization Changed International Organizations

### 1. The Membership Revolution and the Rebalancing of the UN

Decolonization transformed international organizations from within. The UN began in 1945 with 51 members, many of them imperial powers or states aligned with them. As former colonies became independent, they entered the General Assembly and changed its voting arithmetic, language, and agenda. The numerical breakthrough was especially visible in 1955, when 16 new states joined the UN, and in 1960, when 19 more states were admitted. This altered the political balance that made Resolution 1514 possible ([United Nations Audiovisual Library of International Law, n.d.](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/dicc/dicc.html)).

The effect was not merely symbolic. Newly independent states used international organizations to redefine global priorities. Issues such as racial discrimination, apartheid, development finance, commodity prices, trade inequality, foreign investment, resource sovereignty, and economic dependency became central to UN debates. The General Assembly increasingly became the institutional voice of a postcolonial majority.

### 2. From East–West Confrontation to North–South Divide

The Cold War remained important, but decolonization introduced another axis of international politics: the North–South divide. The East–West conflict concerned capitalism, communism, military alliances, and nuclear rivalry. The North–South divide concerned the structural inequalities between industrialized, wealthy, often former imperial states and poorer, newly independent states seeking development and economic autonomy.

This did not replace the Cold War; rather, it complicated it. Newly independent states often tried to avoid subordination to either bloc while using superpower competition to gain aid, diplomatic support, or strategic space. The UN became a site where East–West and North–South issues overlapped. Anti-colonial states could criticize Western imperialism while also resisting Soviet domination or great-power intervention.

### 3. Bandung, Non-Alignment, and Territorial Sovereignty

The Bandung Conference of 18–24 April 1955 brought together 29 mostly Asian and African governments to discuss peace, Cold War non-involvement, economic development, and decolonization. It helped consolidate an Afro-Asian diplomatic identity and linked anti-colonialism to sovereign equality, territorial integrity, and non-interference ([Non-Aligned Movement, n.d.](https://nam.go.ug/history)).

The Non-Aligned Movement was formally established at the Belgrade Summit in September 1961, drawing on Bandung principles. Its founding participants included states such as India, Indonesia, Egypt, Ghana, Yugoslavia, and others seeking an alternative to alignment with either NATO or the Soviet bloc. The movement’s objectives included national independence, sovereignty, territorial integrity, anti-colonialism, anti-racism, and resistance to great-power domination ([Non-Aligned Movement, n.d.](https://nam.go.ug/history)).

Bandung and non-alignment also reveal a major shift in anti-imperialist politics. Interwar anti-imperialism had often been transnational and movement-centered. By the 1950s and 1960s, the main vehicle of decolonization was increasingly the territorial nation-state. Sukarno’s invocation of the League Against Imperialism at Bandung showed continuity, but Bandung’s emphasis on state sovereignty showed that anti-colonial legitimacy had become strongly territorialized ([Shield, n.d.](https://journal.ugm.ac.id/globalsouth/article/view/103842)).

### 4. The Group of 77 and the New International Economic Order

The Group of 77 was founded in 1964 by developing countries at the first United Nations Conference on Trade and Development. It became the principal coalition through which developing states coordinated positions on trade, finance, development, and economic sovereignty. Its formation reflected the argument that political independence alone was insufficient if newly independent states remained economically dependent on former colonial powers and unequal global markets ([Group of 77, n.d.](https://www.g77.org/doc/)).

This logic culminated in demands for a New International Economic Order in the 1970s. The NIEO sought to restructure global economic relations by strengthening developing countries’ control over natural resources, improving commodity terms of trade, expanding development finance, regulating multinational corporations, and increasing technology transfer. The UN Audiovisual Library’s discussion of Resolution 1514 emphasizes the legal and political connection between decolonization, permanent sovereignty over natural resources, and later resolutions such as the 1974 Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order ([United Nations Audiovisual Library of International Law, n.d.](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/dicc/dicc.html)).

| Organization/Bloc | Key Moment | Main Objective | Connection to Decolonization |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Bandung Conference | 1955 | Afro-Asian solidarity, peace, development, anti-colonialism | Linked independence to sovereignty and non-alignment |
| Non-Aligned Movement | 1961 | Avoidance of Cold War bloc subordination | Protected political autonomy of new states |
| Group of 77 | 1964 | Collective economic bargaining by developing states | Shifted agenda from flag independence to economic restructuring |
| NIEO Campaign | 1970s | Reform of trade, resources, finance, and development rules | Claimed economic decolonization after political independence |

### 5. Decolonization and New UN Functions, Including Peacekeeping

Decolonization also expanded the UN’s practical functions. As empires withdrew, new states often faced border disputes, civil conflicts, secessionist crises, and Cold War intervention. UN peacekeeping developed partly in response to these crises. Although peacekeeping began before the full acceleration of decolonization, operations in postcolonial contexts—most notably the Congo in the early 1960s—showed that the UN was increasingly expected to manage conflicts linked to imperial withdrawal, state formation, and superpower competition ([United Nations Peacekeeping, n.d.](https://peacekeeping.un.org/en/our-history)).

This expansion was ambivalent. Peacekeeping could help prevent escalation and protect fragile political transitions, but it could also expose the UN to accusations of great-power manipulation or interference in domestic sovereignty. Decolonization therefore did not simply empower the UN; it also burdened it with conflicts created by arbitrary colonial borders, uneven institutions, and external intervention.

## Conclusion: A Complex but Decisive Relationship

International organizations did not cause decolonization by themselves. Anti-colonial movements, wars of liberation, labor struggles, nationalist parties, global economic change, and imperial exhaustion were all essential. Yet international organizations shaped the environment in which decolonization occurred.

The League of Nations preserved empire through the Mandates System, but it also made imperial governance internationally visible and contestable. The United Nations began with a cautious and ambiguous Charter, but its language of “sacred trust,” self-government, reporting, and trusteeship was gradually transformed by anti-colonial states and movements. The General Assembly became the central arena because it combined visibility, voting equality, and freedom from Security Council vetoes. Resolution 1514 then crystallized a new standard of legitimacy: empire was no longer a normal form of international order.

The deepest transformation came after independence. Decolonization changed the composition of the UN and turned international organizations into battlegrounds over development, racial justice, economic sovereignty, and global inequality. The rise of Bandung, the Non-Aligned Movement, the Group of 77, and the NIEO showed that decolonization was not only about acquiring flags and seats at the UN. It was also about contesting the unequal structures that survived empire.

My concrete conclusion is that international organizations were **not inherently anti-colonial institutions**, but they became indispensable arenas of decolonization once colonized and postcolonial actors learned to use their rules, committees, procedures, and moral language. Their role was therefore contradictory but historically decisive: they helped stabilize empire, then helped delegitimize it, and finally became vehicles through which newly independent states attempted to remake world order.
