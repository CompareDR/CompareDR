# International Organizations and Decolonization in the Twentieth Century

*A study of the uneasy partnership between the institutions built to order the world and the movements that dissolved its empires — from the League of Nations Mandates System to the United Nations, the Group of 77, and beyond (coverage through early 2022)*

---

## Executive Summary

The relationship between twentieth-century international organizations and the process of decolonization resists any simple verdict. The two great multilateral experiments of the century — the **League of Nations** (1920–1946) and the **United Nations** (founded 1945) — were each, at birth, compromised by empire. The League's **Mandates System** classified the former German and Ottoman territories by their supposed level of "development" and placed them under the administration of the victorious imperial powers as a "sacred trust of civilisation" ([Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/leagcov.asp)); the United Nations Charter, drafted at San Francisco in 1945, deliberately avoided the word *independence* in **Chapter XI**, the "Declaration regarding Non-Self-Governing Territories," which bound administering powers only to transmit information and to promote the well-being of dependent peoples ([OPIL](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1074)). Yet both organizations also internationalized colonial questions that empires had long insisted were domestic. The League's **Permanent Mandates Commission** received reports and petitions and held mandatory powers to public account; the UN General Assembly went far further, converting the soft language of Article 73 into a monitoring regime and, on **14 December 1960**, adopting the **Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples** (Resolution 1514 (XV)) by **89 votes to 0, with 9 abstentions** — a roll call on which not a single state was prepared to defend colonial rule openly ([UN Yearbook 1960](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Declaration_on_granting_independence.djvu/1)).

Three conclusions organize the evidence assembled in this report. *First*, the League performed a genuinely **dual role**: it stabilized the imperial order at its moment of greatest crisis after 1918 — distributing former enemy colonies among the victors and legitimatizing their rule — while simultaneously creating, almost by accident, the first standing machinery of international oversight of colonial administration, machinery that anti-colonial actors learned to exploit and that made empire harder, not easier, to defend over time ([Columbia University](https://history.columbia.edu/person/pedersen-susan-g/)). *Second*, the United Nations became the principal **arena** in which decolonization was fought diplomatically: through the trusteeship system, the petition and reporting procedures of Chapter XI, the Assembly's claim to decide unilaterally which territories were "non-self-governing," and the bloc politics of an organization whose membership grew from **51** states in 1945 to **99** in 1960 and **193** by 2011, overwhelmingly through the admission of former colonies ([UN](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/growth-in-un-membership)). *Third*, decolonization did not merely happen *within* international organizations — it **remade** them. The new majority shifted the UN's agenda from East–West security confrontation toward the **North–South divide**, built new collective vehicles such as the **Non-Aligned Movement** (1961) and the **Group of 77** (1964), mounted the century's most ambitious assault on the economic order of the old empires through the **New International Economic Order** (1974), and pushed the organization into entirely new functions, notably **peacekeeping**, which was invented in 1948 and first militarized in 1956 largely in response to crises of imperial retreat ([UN Peacekeeping](https://peacekeeping.un.org/en/our-history)).

The story is therefore one of irony and appropriation rather than of design. Institutions conceived partly to manage and contain the colonial world ended up supplying the vocabulary, the procedures, and the stages on which that world was dismantled; and the dismantling, in turn, transformed the institutions themselves. Neither side of that ledger should be suppressed: the Mandates System protected mandatory powers from annexation charges while it policed their administration; Resolution 1514 was both a Soviet Cold War initiative and a text that forty-three African and Asian states made their own; the New International Economic Order was simultaneously a serious programme for world reform and a casualty of the bloc politics it intensified. The timeline below situates the main waypoints of this entangled history.

## Part One — The League of Nations and the Mandates System, 1919–1945

### 1.1 "A sacred trust of civilisation": how the Mandates System preserved the imperial order

The Mandates System was born of a collision between two incompatible principles: the victors' determination to keep the territories they had conquered in 1914–1918, and Woodrow Wilson's equally public determination that the peace should not look like old-fashioned annexation. Article 22 of the League Covenant resolved the contradiction by declaring that the "colonies and territories" detached from Germany and the Ottoman Empire were "inhabited by peoples not yet able to stand by themselves under the strenuous conditions of the modern world," and that "there should be applied the principle that the well-being and development of such peoples form a **sacred trust of civilisation**," to be discharged by "advanced nations" acting "as Mandatories on behalf of the League" ([Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/leagcov.asp)). The formula converted conquest into tutelage. Sovereignty over the mandated territories was left deliberately undefined — vested, various lawyers argued, in the League, in the inhabitants, or nowhere at all — while practical administration was handed to Britain, France, Belgium, Japan, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand. In substance, the empire was preserved; in form, it was internationalized.

The system's architecture was openly hierarchical. Article 22 sorted the mandated peoples into three classes according to their presumed "stage of development," a classification that mapped almost perfectly onto contemporary racial-geographic assumptions, as the table below summarizes. **Class A** covered the former Ottoman Arab provinces, judged nearly ready for independence; **Class B** covered the former German colonies of central Africa, to be administered as colonies under a League "open door"; **Class C** covered **South West Africa** and the Pacific islands, which could be administered "as integral portions" of the mandatory's own territory — annexation in all but name ([Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/leagcov.asp)). The mandates were allocated among the victorious powers at San Remo in 1920 and confirmed by the League Council in 1922, in most cases to the power already in military occupation. The imperial character of the settlement was not lost on its critics: Germany's colonies changed hands without the consultation of a single inhabitant, and the one apparent act of self-determination built into the system — the Class A commitment to "provisional recognition" of independence — was hedged by the mandatory's "administrative advice and assistance."

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** — "such stage of development that their existence as independent nations can be provisionally recognized" | Ottoman successor communities | Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan (Britain); Syria, Lebanon (France) | "Administrative advice and assistance" by a mandatory until able to stand alone | Iraq independent **1932** and admitted to the League ([Iraqi MFA](https://mofa.gov.iq/2021/26881/)); Transjordan 1946; Lebanon 1943; Syria 1946; Palestine unresolved at the League's death |
| **B** — peoples requiring the mandatory "to be responsible for the administration" | Ex-German central Africa | Tanganyika (Britain); Ruanda-Urundi (Belgium); British and French Togoland; British Cameroons and French Cameroun | Direct administration; freedom of conscience; "open door" trade; no fortifications or native military training except for police | All six became **UN trust territories** in 1946; independent or merged 1957–1962 |
| **C** — territories which "can be best administered as integral portions" of the mandatory | Ex-German South West Africa & Pacific | South West Africa (South Africa); German Samoa (New Zealand); New Guinea and Nauru (Australia, for Britain); North Pacific islands (Japan) | Administration as part of mandatory's territory, subject to safeguards | All became trust territories except **South West Africa**, whose mandatory refused trusteeship — the dispute that produced the ICJ's landmark **1950 advisory opinion** ([ICJ](https://www.icj-cij.org/en/case/10)) |

The system's most revealing test came in the one Class A case that ran to completion. Iraq, placed under British mandate in 1920, rebelled the same year; by the end of the decade Britain calculated that sponsoring Iraq's admission to the League was cheaper than holding it. On **3 October 1932**, the League Assembly admitted the Kingdom of Iraq to membership — the first and only mandate to "graduate" in the League's lifetime — terminating the mandate in exchange for Iraqi guarantees on minorities and a 1930 treaty that preserved British air bases and influence for twenty-five years ([Iraqi MFA](https://mofa.gov.iq/2021/26881/), [Daily History](https://dailyhistoryai.com/events/iraq-independence-1932)). The episode established two enduring precedents at once: it demonstrated that a mandate was legally *terminable* — that the system pointed, however hesitantly, toward independent statehood — and it pioneered a model of constrained independence, sovereignty granted alongside treaty reservations that entrenched the former mandatory's strategic position. Both precedents would echo through the decolonization era after 1945, in arrangements from the French Community to the independence settlements of the 1960s.

What the Mandates System plainly did *not* do was hasten the end of empire on any schedule the colonized would have chosen. No Class B or C mandate approached independence under the League; France crushed the Syrian revolt in 1925–1927 without League intervention; Japan's Pacific mandate became a fortified strategic zone closed to outsiders; and South Africa's rule in South West Africa replicated its domestic colour bar, including the aerial bombing of the **Bondelswarts** community in May 1922, in which about **one hundred people were killed**, among them women and children ([Military History](https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Bondelswarts_Rebellion)). If the system had functioned only as designed, it would deserve to be remembered as empire laundered through Geneva. That it is also remembered as the beginning of international accountability for colonial rule is the result of mechanisms whose significance their authors did not foresee — the subject of the next section.

### 1.2 The uninvited guest: international oversight and the Permanent Mandates Commission

The League Covenant required each mandatory to render an **annual report** to the Council, and it created a **Permanent Mandates Commission** (PMC) — a small body of expert members, a majority of them nationals of non-mandatory states — to examine those reports and advise the Council ([Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/leagcov.asp)). To these reporting duties the mandatory instruments added a right of **petition**: inhabitants of the mandated territories, and outsiders on their behalf, could address the League, although petitions had to pass through the mandatory power itself, which appended its comments. The Commission had no power to visit the territories, no sanctions to impose, and no authority to alter a mandate. On paper, this was oversight without teeth. In practice, as Susan Pedersen's definitive history of the system argues, the allies "sought to use the League to safeguard imperial authority, but that authority was undermined by the mechanisms for international oversight they had themselves created" ([Columbia University](https://history.columbia.edu/person/pedersen-susan-g/)). Geneva became a stage: mandatory officials were cross-examined on labour practices, land alienation, and police violence; humanitarian organizations and colonial nationalists learned to time petitions and publicity to the Commission's sessions; and governments that resented the settlement — above all Germany and Italy — attended in hopes of demanding a repartition of the spoils.

The Bondelswarts affair of 1922 showed both the reach and the limits of this stage. When South African aircraft bombed the Bondelswarts into submission, the Permanent Mandates Commission subjected the mandatory's report to unusually sharp scrutiny, and the matter reached the floor of the League Assembly, where delegates protested the disproportionate violence — making the episode "one of the first uprisings to be examined by the Permanent Mandates Commission" and prompting a genuinely international debate about the ethics of colonial policing ([Military History](https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Bondelswarts_Rebellion)). No punishment followed, and South African rule continued unchanged. But a precedent had been set that mattered more than the outcome: a mandatory power could be called, in public session of the world's organization, to answer for the blood it shed in a territory it did not formally own. The same pattern repeated across the interwar years — over forced labour in South West Africa's diamond economy, famine in Ruanda, labour abuses in New Guinea, the terms of oil concessions in Iraq — each scandal exhausting a little more of the imperial powers' patience and legitimacy. Pedersen notes that by the 1930s some imperial statesmen had begun to wonder whether independent states "might be easier to deal with than territories subject to international scrutiny," a quiet inversion of the system's original purpose ([Columbia University](https://history.columbia.edu/person/pedersen-susan-g/)).

The oversight regime was, to be sure, deeply flawed, and a balanced account must record its limits as precisely as its innovations. Petitions travelled through the very administrations they accused; the Commission's members were establishment figures who shared most of the era's civilizational assumptions; the Assembly could deplore but not compel; and the system's gaze never extended beyond the sixteen mandates to the vastly larger colonial world of British India, French Indochina, the Netherlands East Indies, or the Belgian Congo. Two durable legacies nonetheless emerged. Normatively, the system inscribed into international practice two principles the ICJ would later identify as the mandate's "paramount" rules — **non-annexation**, and the proposition that the well-being and development of dependent peoples is an international responsibility rather than a domestic prerogative ([ICJ](https://www.icj-cij.org/en/case/10)). Procedurally, it bequeathed a toolkit — annual reports, questionnaires, petitions, public examination — that the United Nations would inherit, sharpen with oral hearings and visiting missions, and turn from a gentlemen's review into an instrument of systematic political pressure. The League's critics understood this earlier than its guardians did; to the most important of those critics we now turn.

### 1.3 The counter-international: the League Against Imperialism

The League of Nations was not the only "league" organizing across borders in the 1920s. On **10 February 1927**, some **174 delegates from 34 countries** gathered at the Palais d'Egmont in Brussels to found the **League Against Imperialism and for National Independence** (LAI) — a deliberate counter-international, convened to expose and oppose the imperial order that Geneva had legitimized ([ProQuest](https://search.proquest.com/openview/7efbd981cb08c1527a8c3d27a0338331/1.pdf?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=18750&diss=y)). The conference was the creation of **Willi Münzenberg**, the German communist impresario of Workers International Relief, acting with the backing — and, as Comintern archives later confirmed, the funding and direction — of the Communist International, which had sponsored a predecessor body, the League Against Colonial Oppression, in 1926 ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/three-meanings-of-colonialism-nehru-sukarno-and-kotelawala-debate-the-future-of-the-third-world-movement-195461/41920E719970B4E2D14BF0AF908CC9B3)). What made Brussels remarkable was its breadth: roughly two-thirds of the delegates came from the colonized world, and the platform united communists, socialists, trade unionists, pacifists, and bourgeois nationalists who agreed on little except the enemy. Among those present or associated were **Jawaharlal Nehru** for the Indian National Congress, **Mohammad Hatta** for the Indonesian national movement, Sen Katayama of Japan, Lamine Senghor of Senegal, and sympathizers including Albert Einstein, Henri Barbusse, and Madame Sun Yat-sen ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/three-meanings-of-colonialism-nehru-sukarno-and-kotelawala-debate-the-future-of-the-third-world-movement-195461/41920E719970B4E2D14BF0AF908CC9B3)).

The LAI's significance for this report lies in the relationship — hostile, parasitic, and formative — it bore to the official internationalism of the League of Nations. Nehru, who attended League meetings in Geneva while living nearby, came away convinced that the official organization was a closed club of empires from which peoples like his own were effectively excluded; Brussels supplied the missing forum, a place where "stateless" nations could enjoy something like diplomatic representation and where anti-colonialism was linked to metropolitan labour politics through "Hands Off China" campaigns and solidarity committees in Berlin, London, and Paris ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/three-meanings-of-colonialism-nehru-sukarno-and-kotelawala-debate-the-future-of-the-third-world-movement-195461/41920E719970B4E2D14BF0AF908CC9B3), [ProQuest](https://search.proquest.com/openview/7efbd981cb08c1527a8c3d27a0338331/1.pdf?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=18750&diss=y)). The organization institutionalized a fateful conceptual shift as well: at Münzenberg's insistence, its declared goal was *anti-imperialism* rather than *anti-colonialism*, aiming the struggle at the European "centre" rather than the colonial "periphery" — a framing that embedded the national question in a global critique of capitalism and prefigured the economic agendas of the 1970s ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/three-meanings-of-colonialism-nehru-sukarno-and-kotelawala-debate-the-future-of-the-third-world-movement-195461/41920E719970B4E2D14BF0AF908CC9B3)).

The coalition did not survive the decade. The Comintern's "class against class" turn of 1928 subordinated broad alliance to sectarian loyalty; the Kuomintang's massacre of its communist allies destroyed the Chinese partnership; the second congress at Frankfurt in 1929 passed resolutions aligned with Moscow's "Third Period" line; and by 1929–1931 figures such as Nehru had been expelled or pushed out, leaving a narrower, more plainly communist body that was quietly dissolved around **1936–1937**, first in Paris and finally in London ([ProQuest](https://search.proquest.com/openview/7efbd981cb08c1527a8c3d27a0338331/1.pdf?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=18750&diss=y)). Historical judgment remains divided, and the division itself is instructive. Read one way, the LAI was a Comintern front whose solidarity was instrumental and whose collapse proved that anti-colonial unity could not be built on a great power's foreign policy; read another, it was the most ambitious interwar attempt to organize global anti-colonialism across ideological lines, and the personal networks it forged mattered more than its resolutions. Hatta himself later described the Brussels gathering as an important precursor of the **Bandung Conference of 1955**, and scholars routinely treat 1927 as one of the founding moments of what would become the Third World movement ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/three-meanings-of-colonialism-nehru-sukarno-and-kotelawala-debate-the-future-of-the-third-world-movement-195461/41920E719970B4E2D14BF0AF908CC9B3)). The lesson its alumni drew — that colonized peoples needed international vehicles of their own, autonomous from any patron — would be acted upon, a generation later, at Bandung, Belgrade, and within the United Nations itself.

## Part Two: The United Nations and the Making of Decolonisation, 1945–1961

### 2.1 San Francisco, 1945: an organisation founded in the shadow of empire

The United Nations was designed, in the first instance, to prevent another world war, not to abolish empire. The Dumbarton Oaks proposals of 1944, negotiated among the United States, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom and China, said almost nothing about dependent territories, and the colonial question reached the San Francisco Conference of April–June 1945 largely through the initiative of the administering powers themselves: it was the British delegation, drawing on inter-war experience and on wartime planning begun under pressure from the Atlantic Charter, that tabled the drafts which became Chapter XI of the Charter, the "Declaration Regarding Non-Self-Governing Territories" ([Oxford Public International Law, "Non-Self-Governing Territories"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1074)). The very form of Chapter XI is revealing. It is styled a "declaration" — the only chapter of the Charter so designated — and its obligations were deliberately framed as a statement of policy rather than a set of justiciable commitments, a compromise that allowed France, Britain, Belgium, and the other metropolitan powers to accept international language on colonies without conceding any external authority over them. The result was an ambiguous founding text. On one reading, the UN of 1945 was an organisation of empires: five of its founding members held colonies, the colonial powers sat permanently on the Security Council, and the Charter nowhere condemned colonialism as such. On another reading, the Charter universalised the vocabulary in which empire could be judged. Article 1(2) named the "self-determination of peoples" among the organisation's purposes — a formula inserted on Soviet initiative with Chinese support, over the reservations of the Western colonial powers ([Cambridge University Press, *Nationalities Papers*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/21A6A8289D38CB1866B948A9A8EB3F25/core-reader)) — and Article 73 obliged administering members to accept "as a sacred trust" the obligation to promote the well-being of the inhabitants of territories under their administration, "to develop self-government," and to transmit regular information on conditions in those territories to the Secretary-General ([United Nations, Charter Chapter XI](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/chapter-11)).

Mark Mazower has cautioned against reading the San Francisco settlement as an anti-colonial breakthrough, emphasising instead the continuities of personnel and purpose between the League and the UN: the new organisation's framework, he argues, was shaped by statesmen — among them Jan Smuts of South Africa, a draftsman of both the League Covenant and the UN Charter's preamble — who saw international organisation as a way of stabilising, not dismantling, a world ordered by the great powers ([Cambridge University Press, *Ethics & International Affairs*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ethics-and-international-affairs/article/no-enchanted-palace-the-end-of-empire-and-the-ideological-origins-of-the-united-nations-mark-mazower-princeton-nj-princeton-university-press-2009-232-pp-25-cloth/8296E50AF94761DA327FE0CE85C47860)). That reading captures an important truth about 1945, but an incomplete one. The same conference that reassured the colonial powers also inserted into the Charter, for the first time in a universal treaty, the word "independence" as a legitimate political objective. Article 76(b), governing the trusteeship system that succeeded the mandates, listed among its basic objectives the "progressive development towards self-government or **independence**," appropriate to the circumstances of each territory and "the freely expressed wishes of the peoples concerned" ([United Nations, Charter Chapter XI](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/chapter-11)). The colonial powers accepted this language partly because its reach seemed narrow: trusteeship would apply only to territories voluntarily placed under it, and "strategic areas" could be carved out of any trusteeship agreement and placed under Security Council — that is, veto-protected — supervision, as the United States would do with the Pacific islands it had taken from Japan. The gap between Chapter XI's prudent silence on independence and Article 76's explicit naming of it would structure UN politics on the colonial question for the next fifteen years: anti-colonial delegations constantly sought to read the trusteeship standard back into Chapter XI, while administering powers insisted on the letter of each. What neither side fully foresaw in 1945 was how quickly the composition of the organisation itself would tilt that argument.

### 2.2 From mandates to trusteeship: continuity with a sharper edge

The trusteeship system was the institutional heir of the mandates system, and its genealogy shows both how much the UN inherited from the League and how the terms of international accountability had shifted. Of the League's remaining mandated territories, ten were eventually placed under trusteeship agreements approved by the General Assembly between 1946 and 1949; only South Africa refused, declining to convert South West Africa and instead seeking the Assembly's consent to incorporate it outright — a request the Assembly rejected in 1946, setting up a confrontation that would run for more than four decades ([Oxford Public International Law, "South West Africa/Namibia"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e209)). The new system preserved the League's basic architecture — administering authority, supervisory organ, annual reports — but sharpened nearly every edge. Where the Permanent Mandates Commission had been a body of private experts appointed by the Council, the Trusteeship Council was a body of governments, balanced between administering and non-administering members; where the PMC's petition procedure had been indirect and discretionary, the trusteeship agreements gave inhabitants a recognised right to petition, and the Trusteeship Council conducted regular visiting missions to the territories themselves; and where Article 22 had spoken only of tutelage and the capacity to "stand by themselves," the Charter made self-government **or independence** the declared destination of every trust territory. The comparison with Chapter XI is equally instructive, for the trusteeship system covered at its peak only eleven territories, while the far larger colonial world fell under the deliberately softer declaration regime.

The eleven trust territories, and their divergent roads out of trusteeship, map the geography of mid-century decolonisation almost exactly. The first to leave the system was British Togoland, where a UN-supervised plebiscite on **9 May 1956** produced a majority — about **58 per cent** — for integration with the neighbouring Gold Coast, and the territory joined the new state of Ghana at its independence on **6 March 1957**; the General Assembly had advised a plebiscite as early as December 1955, responding to years of Ewe unification petitions from both Togolands ([Commonwealth Chamber of Commerce, Togo](https://commonwealthchamber.com/member-countries/togo/)) ([Alchetron, "British Togoland"](https://alchetron.com/British-Togoland)). French Togoland became independent as Togo in 1960, the same year as French Cameroun, Italian Somaliland (which joined British Somaliland to form Somalia), and the Belgian trust territory's neighbours across the Congo basin; the British Cameroons was split by the 1961 plebiscites between Nigeria and Cameroon; Tanganyika (1961), Ruanda-Urundi (as Rwanda and Burundi, 1962) and Western Samoa (1962) followed, then Nauru (1968) and New Guinea (as part of Papua New Guinea, 1975). Last and strangest was the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands, the United States' strategic trust, whose four component units completed their political settlements only with Palau's independence on **1 October 1994** — the event that finally allowed the Trusteeship Council to suspend operation on 1 November 1994, its historic mission complete ([United Nations, Main Bodies](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/main-bodies)).

Two features of this settlement deserve emphasis precisely because they resist a simple narrative of progress. The first is that trusteeship's harder edge was blunted at the great powers' discretion: the United States administered its Pacific trust as a "strategic area" answerable to the Security Council, where its veto shielded nuclear testing in the Marshall Islands from effective international scrutiny for decades, and South Africa's simple refusal to place South West Africa under the system exposed how little the organisation could compel a determined member. When the International Court of Justice held in its 1950 advisory opinion that South Africa remained bound by the mandate's obligations and subject to UN supervision, Pretoria ignored the finding; when Ethiopia and Liberia — acting in their capacity as former League members — brought the issue back as a contentious case, the Court's 1966 judgement declined on standing grounds to rule on the merits, a decision so widely condemned that the General Assembly responded in October 1966 by declaring the mandate terminated and South Africa's continued presence illegal ([Oxford Public International Law, "South West Africa/Namibia"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e209)) ([International Court of Justice, South West Africa cases](https://www.icj-cij.org/en/case/10)). The second feature is that the system's successes were real and were registered by the colonised themselves: petitioners and nationalist movements treated the Trusteeship Council's visiting missions and hearings as resources, timing campaigns to UN calendars and quoting Charter language back at administrators. Trusteeship, in other words, did not cause the independence of its eleven territories — colonial retreat was driven by forces far larger than any council chamber — but it internationalised their transitions, fixed independence as their legal terminus, and supplied a repertoire of supervision that the General Assembly would soon try to extend across the whole colonial world.
### 2.3 Chapter XI in practice: the General Assembly becomes the anti-colonial arena

The Charter had given the General Assembly no explicit power over non-self-governing territories beyond receiving information, but from its very first session the Assembly began converting that slender textual foothold into a standing jurisdiction over colonial questions. In December 1946, by resolution **66(I)**, the Assembly noted the first transmissions under Article 73(e) and listed **72 territories** on which eight administering members — Australia, Belgium, Denmark, France, the Netherlands, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the United States — had undertaken to report ([United Nations, Non-Self-Governing Territories](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/nsgt)). The scale of the world thereby placed, however tenuously, under a UN reporting obligation was immense: at the organisation's founding, roughly **750 million people — about one-third of humanity — lived in colonial territories** ([United Nations, Decolonization history](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/history)). The administering powers understood the danger at once and fought a long rearguard action over competence: Article 73(e) required information on economic, social and educational conditions but pointedly omitted political development, and metropolitan delegates insisted that any discussion of political affairs in their territories violated the domestic-jurisdiction clause of Article 2(7). The counter-coalition — Asian and Arab members first, soon joined by the Latin American majority and the Soviet bloc — answered with procedural persistence. In 1947 the Assembly established, initially for three years, a **Special Committee on Information** under Article 73(e), comprising the administering states and an equal number of elected non-administering members; renewed triennially and then made permanent in 1955 as the Committee on Information from Non-Self-Governing Territories, it became the institutional shell inside which colonial policy was debated, reported upon and criticised every single year ([United Nations, Decolonization history](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/history)).

The decisive battles were fought over standards — over who defined self-government, and who decided when a territory had achieved it. Administering states maintained that each metropole alone judged the constitutional maturity of its dependencies and simply ceased transmitting information when it saw fit, as France did for Algeria after 1952 and the United States for Puerto Rico in 1953. The Assembly's answer, built up resolution by resolution, was to claim that judgement for itself. Resolution **742(VIII)** of 1953 adopted a list of factors — political, economic, social and educational — indicative of the attainment of self-government, asserting that the Assembly, not the administering power, was competent to pronounce on whether a territory remained under Chapter XI ([Oxford Public International Law, "Non-Self-Governing Territories"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1074)). Resolution **1541(XV)** of 1960 then defined the three legitimate forms of full self-government — emergence as a sovereign independent state, free association with an independent state, or integration with one — and required that the latter two result from the freely expressed wishes of the territory's people. The same day, the Assembly applied the new doctrine coercively: resolution **1542(XV)** declared Portugal's overseas provinces to be non-self-governing territories within the meaning of Chapter XI, overriding Lisbon's claim that they were integral parts of the Portuguese nation, and in 1962 resolution **1747(XVI)** extended the same classification to Southern Rhodesia over British objection ([Oxford Public International Law, "Non-Self-Governing Territories"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1074)). This was a constitutional coup executed by majority vote: a "declaration" the colonial powers had accepted as hortatory in 1945 had been converted, through the Assembly's power to interpret its own procedures, into a supervisory regime the administering states had never consented to — and it worked precisely because the Assembly needed no one's consent to speak.

Yet the arena had real limits, and acknowledging them is essential to any honest account. The Assembly's Chapter XI jurisdiction reached only "non-self-governing territories," and the metropolitan powers retained influence over how that category was drawn: the so-called **salt-water or blue-water doctrine**, promoted by Belgium in the 1950s and absorbed into Assembly practice, confined decolonisation to territories geographically separate from and ethnically distinct from the metropole, thereby excluding the claims of indigenous peoples within settler states and of minority nations within Europe itself ([Kosovo 2.0, "From a de facto colony to a de jure state"](https://kosovotwopointzero.com/en/from-a-de-facto-colony-to-a-de-jure)). The doctrine entrenched the territorial integrity of existing states — self-determination was to operate **within colonial borders**, however artificial — and it left the Assembly with no answer when administering powers simply ignored its resolutions, as South Africa, Portugal and Rhodesia did for years ([Oxford Public International Law, "Peoples"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1454)). What the arena could do was manufacture legitimacy and transmit pressure. Petitioners learned its rhythms: the Ewe unification movement petitioned the Trusteeship Council and the Assembly through the early 1950s until the General Assembly advised a plebiscite in British Togoland, held under UN supervision in May 1956; South West African petitioners, channelled by the Anglican reverend Michael Scott, addressed the Assembly's Fourth Committee from 1947 onward, and the International Court confirmed in 1956 that the UN's supervisory practice over the mandate could lawfully include oral hearings — small procedural victories that kept a territory's claim alive for four decades ([Commonwealth Chamber of Commerce, Togo](https://commonwealthchamber.com/member-countries/togo/)) ([Oxford Public International Law, "South West Africa/Namibia"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e209)). Measured against its own aspirations, the Chapter XI regime looks almost absurdly successful: of the 72 territories listed in 1946, only **17** remained on the UN list in the early 2020s, home to fewer than two million people — while the great majority of their former inhabitants now live in sovereign member states ([United Nations, Non-Self-Governing Territories](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/nsgt)).

### 2.4 The 1960 Declaration: resolution 1514 and the normative revolution

Everything came to a head in 1960 — the "**Year of Africa**," when **seventeen African states** attained independence (fourteen of them former French colonies) and sixteen of them entered the United Nations in a single autumn, shifting the Assembly's balance of numbers almost overnight ([Wikipedia, "Year of Africa"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_Africa)). It was into this transformed chamber that Nikita Khrushchev, addressing the fifteenth session on **23 September 1960**, threw a calculated challenge: a Soviet proposal for a declaration granting immediate independence to all colonial countries and peoples, designed in part to embarrass the Western powers and to court the new Afro-Asian majority ([UN Yearbook 1960, via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Declaration_on_granting_independence.djvu/1)). What emerged instead was the majority's own text. An Afro-Asian draft, sponsored by **43 states**, was negotiated through the autumn and adopted on **14 December 1960** as the **Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples**, resolution **1514(XV)**, by **89 votes to none, with 9 abstentions** — Australia, Belgium, the Dominican Republic, France, Portugal, Spain, South Africa, the United Kingdom and the United States, a roll-call that reads like a map of the remaining colonial world and its closest allies ([UN Yearbook 1960, via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Declaration_on_granting_independence.djvu/1)).

The Declaration's seven operative paragraphs mark the point at which the United Nations ceased to describe colonialism as a condition to be managed and declared it a wrong to be ended. Its premises are uncompromising: "the subjection of peoples to alien subjugation, domination and exploitation constitutes a denial of fundamental human rights, is contrary to the Charter of the United Nations and is an impediment to the promotion of world peace and co-operation"; "all peoples have the right to self-determination; by virtue of that right they freely determine their political status and freely pursue their economic, social and cultural development"; and "inadequacy of political, economic, social or educational preparedness should never serve as a pretext for delaying independence" — a sentence aimed directly at the tutelage doctrine on which a century of imperial justification, from Article 22 of the Covenant onward, had rested ([OHCHR, Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/declaration-granting-independence-colonial-countries-and-peoples)). Equally consequential was what the Declaration said about method and territory: "immediate steps shall be taken, in Trust and Non-Self-Governing Territories or all other territories which have not yet attained independence, to transfer all powers to the peoples of those territories, without any conditions or reservations"; and "any attempt aimed at the partial or total disruption of the national unity and the territorial integrity of a country is incompatible with the purposes and principles of the Charter" ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/declaration-granting-independence-colonial-countries-and-peoples)). Decolonisation was thus simultaneously accelerated and bounded: rapid, unconditional, and confined within existing frontiers. In November 1961 the Assembly gave the Declaration an enforcement organ of sorts, creating by resolution **1654(XVI)** the **Special Committee of Seventeen** — soon of Twenty-Four, and known ever since as the **Special Committee on Decolonization, or C-24** — to examine its application territory by territory and recommend action; the committee survives today and remains the institutional memory of 1960 ([United Nations, Special Committee on Decolonization](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/about)).

How should the Declaration be judged? As a Cold War artefact, it plainly was one: the Soviet initiative that provoked it was an instrument of bloc competition, and Washington's inability to vote against it — the Eisenhower administration's abstention, reportedly imposed on a furious London and Paris at the last moment — exposed how completely anti-colonialism had captured the rhetorical centre. But to see only the Cold War in 1514 is to miss its authors: the text was drafted and carried by the Afro-Asian group, and its language was theirs, fusing the Charter's self-determination clause, the human-rights covenants then in preparation, and the Bandung communique of 1955 into a single instrument that colonial powers could abstain on but no longer openly oppose ([UN Yearbook 1960, via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Declaration_on_granting_independence.djvu/1)). Its legal status was deliberately ambiguous — a "declaration," not a treaty — yet within a decade the Assembly, the International Court and state practice were treating its core norm as authoritative: the Court's 1971 Namibia opinion would cite it in holding South Africa's presence illegal, and later opinions on Western Sahara and the Chagos Archipelago extended the same logic. Its limits were equally structural. By tying independence to existing colonial boundaries and to statehood as the only legitimate destination, 1514 foreclosed other imaginable futures — federation, associated statehood on equal terms, indigenous sovereignty within settler states — and it offered no mechanism beyond shame and solidarity against a metropole that simply refused, as Portugal did until its own revolution in 1974. The Declaration is therefore best read neither as the UN's redemption nor as an empty gesture, but as a transfer of normative authority: after 1960, colonialism required justification before an international audience, and justification was a burden empires increasingly failed to carry.

## Part Three: How Decolonisation Remade International Organisation, 1955–2022

### 3.1 The new majority: numbers, agendas, and backlash

Decolonisation did not merely add flags to the hall of the General Assembly; it changed what kind of organisation the United Nations was. Membership grew from **51** states in 1945 to **76** in 1955 — when a Soviet-Western "package deal" ended a decade of admission deadlock — then leapt to **99** in 1960 as the Year of Africa's new states arrived, passed **127** by 1965, and reached **159** by the mid-1980s and **193** by 2011; the overwhelming majority of the new members were former colonies and trust territories of Asia and Africa ([United Nations, Growth in UN Membership](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/growth-in-un-membership)). The political arithmetic was stark. By the early 1960s the Afro-Asian group alone could muster a simple majority, and with the Latin American and communist blocs it could reach the two-thirds required for important questions; the states that had founded the organisation and written its Charter could no longer reliably win a vote in its plenary organ. Every organ felt the shift — the Security Council and ECOSOC were enlarged by Charter amendment in 1965 precisely to accommodate it — but the deepest change was in the Assembly, which the new majority turned toward the questions that mattered to it: apartheid, the remaining Portuguese territories, Southern Rhodesia, Namibia, Palestine, and the structure of the world economy.

Figure 5. United Nations membership, 1945–2011: the decolonisation wave in numbers

The new majority used its numbers with considerable tactical sophistication, and the results were concrete. It converted the symbolic politics of resolutions into standing machinery: the Special Committee against Apartheid, the UN Council for Namibia created in 1967 to administer the territory in place of South Africa — an unprecedented assertion that the organisation could substitute itself for a delinquent administering power — and the Decolonization Committee's annual territory-by-territory scrutiny ([Oxford Public International Law, "International Territorial Administration"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1297?p)). It pressed the Security Council into its first mandatory sanctions regime, against Rhodesia after 1966, and kept the South African item on the agenda for four decades until the system it targeted collapsed. And it demonstrated its capacity to settle scores among the great powers themselves: on **25 October 1971**, resolution **2758(XXVI)** transferred China's seat from Taipei to Beijing by **76 votes to 35, with 17 abstentions**, a victory carried by the enlarged Third World membership over prolonged American resistance ([Wikipedia, "Twenty-sixth session of the UN General Assembly"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-sixth_session_of_the_United_Nations_General_Assembly)). For the post-colonial states, this was the point of membership: the UN was the one arena where military and economic weakness translated, through sovereign equality, into institutional weight.

The backlash was equally concrete, and it exposes the ambivalence at the heart of the organisation's transformation. Western governments and commentators denounced the "automatic majority" and the "tyranny of numbers," complaining that states representing a small fraction of world population and budget contributions could outvote those who paid; Washington's disillusionment deepened through the 1970s over resolutions it regarded as hostile — most notoriously the 1975 equation of Zionism with racism, repealed only in 1991 — and found institutional expression in withdrawal and financial pressure. The United States quit **UNESCO in 1984**, citing politicisation and mismanagement, and did not rejoin until 2003; the United Kingdom and Singapore followed it out in 1985 ([Global Banking & Finance, "US-Trump-UNESCO timeline"](https://www.globalbankingandfinance.com/USA-TRUMP-UNESCO-TIMELINE-57018c77-8d5b-4904-aebe-2f54c314c53f/)) ([Baidu Baike, UNESCO](https://baike.baidu.com/en/item/United%20Nations%20Educational%2C%20Scientific%20and%20Cultural%20Organization/1471665)). Both readings of the new majority contain truth. The anti-colonial coalition was frequently instrumentalised — by the Soviet bloc, by regional rivalries, and by governments whose own domestic records sat awkwardly beside their denunciations of colonial repression — and its majoritarian habits at times degraded the Assembly into a theatre of symbolic victories. Yet the charge of illegitimacy cuts oddly against the principle on which the organisation rests: the "automatic majority" was, for the first time in history, a majority of humanity's sovereign states speaking in a universal forum, and the agenda it imposed — racial equality, self-determination, development — was drawn directly from the Charter the founders had written. The crisis of the 1970s and 1980s was thus not the capture of the UN by an alien force but the discovery, by its architects, of what their institution looked like when everyone joined it.

### 3.2 From East–West to North–South: Bandung, Belgrade, and the Group of 77

The new majority's deepest structural achievement was to rotate the axis of international politics. The UN of 1945 had been organised around the East–West confrontation of the Cold War; by the mid-1960s the agenda of its economic and social organs was dominated instead by the **North–South divide** between industrialised and post-colonial states. The genealogy of that rotation runs back through the inter-war League Against Imperialism to the **Bandung Conference of April 1955**, where twenty-nine Asian and African governments — representing, with China, more than half of humanity — met without any colonial power present and asserted their claim to speak as a world force on disarmament, economic development and self-determination ([Britannica, "Bandung Conference"](https://www.britannica.com/event/Bandung-Conference)). Bandung's political line continued in the **Non-Aligned Movement**, founded at Belgrade in 1961 by Tito, Nehru, Nasser, Nkrumah and Sukarno, which sought to keep the new states out of the rival alliance systems and grew into a grouping of some **120 members** — nearly two-thirds of the UN — that still meets at summit level ([Britannica, "Non-Aligned Movement"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Non-Aligned-Movement)). Its economic line was institutionalised inside the UN itself: on **15 June 1964**, at the close of the first United Nations Conference on Trade and Development, seventy-seven developing states issued a Joint Declaration founding the **Group of 77**, which today numbers **134 members** plus China and functions as the South's negotiating caucus across the UN system ([Ahram Online, "Egypt and the Chairmanship of the G77"](https://english.ahram.org.eg/NewsContentP/4/288434/Opinion/Egypt-Chairmanship-of-the-G.aspx)).

The G77's founding achievement was to make UNCTAD permanent. What the industrial powers had intended as a one-off conference became, over their objections, a standing UN organ with its own secretariat under the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch, dedicated to the proposition that the existing rules of world trade systematically disadvantaged commodity exporters; its pressure helped force the addition of **Part IV on trade and development to the GATT in 1965**, the first formal acknowledgment in the trade regime that equal treatment of unequals was not equality ([Oxford Public International Law, "New International Economic Order"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1542)). The pattern is worth underlining, because it generalises: the post-colonial states, excluded from effective power in the Security Council, the IMF and the World Bank by weighted voting and veto rights, used the one-man-one-vote organs of the UN to build parallel institutions of their own — UNCTAD, the UN Development Programme, the industrial development organisation, a thickened web of regional commissions — and thereby changed the organisation's centre of gravity from peace-and-security maintenance toward development. The UN of the 1960s and 1970s did far more economic and social work, and proportionally less collective-security work, than its founders had planned, and decolonisation is the single largest reason why.

It would be a mistake, though, to romanticise "the South" as a unified actor, and the record of the North–South turn is inseparable from its fractures. The G77 and NAM aggregated states with radically different economies — oil exporters and oil importers, middle-income industrialisers and least-developed agrarian economies — whose interests diverged sharply as soon as negotiations moved from principles to prices; non-aligned members fought wars against one another (India and China in 1962, Iran and Iraq after 1980) and competed for the same Western markets and credits. The Third World's solidarity was real but performative as well as practical, held together by what its members were against more than by agreement on what they were for. The same heterogeneity that made the coalition powerful in symbolic votes made it fragile in distributive bargaining — a structural weakness the industrial powers would exploit in the 1980s, when the debt crisis gave them the leverage to deal with developing states one by one rather than as a bloc. The North–South axis, in other words, never replaced the East–West one; it overlaid it, and the UN spent the rest of the twentieth century negotiating between the two.
### 3.3 The New International Economic Order: the high-water mark of post-colonial worldmaking

The North–South turn reached its climax in 1974. Emboldened by the OPEC oil embargo of 1973 — which seemed to prove that commodity power could bend the industrial world — Algeria's Houari Boumediène convened the General Assembly's **sixth special session** on raw materials and development, and in May the Assembly adopted, without a vote, the **Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order (resolution 3201(S-VI))** and its accompanying **Programme of Action (resolution 3202(S-VI))** ([Oxford Public International Law, "New International Economic Order"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1542)). The NIEO was decolonisation's second charter, an attempt to extend the logic of 1514 from politics to economics: formal sovereignty, its authors argued, was hollow without control over the resources and rules on which prosperity depended. Its core demands followed accordingly — **permanent sovereignty over natural resources**, including the right to nationalise foreign property with compensation judged by domestic law; **indexation** linking commodity prices to the price of manufactured imports; preferential and non-reciprocal market access; a target of **0.7 per cent of GNP** in official development assistance; technology transfer on favourable terms; regulation of transnational corporations; and greater voting power for developing states in the international financial institutions ([Oxford Public International Law](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1542)). Six months later the Assembly sharpened the programme into the **Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States (resolution 3281(XXIX))** — and this time the West refused consensus: the Charter passed by **120 votes to 6, with 10 abstentions**, the negative votes coming from the United States and five of its closest industrial allies, a division that announced, in advance, how the struggle would end ([Oxford Public International Law](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1542)).

The historian Adom Getachew has taught us to read the NIEO not as a wish-list of grievances but as **worldmaking**: an effort by post-colonial states — often the same diplomats and intellectuals who had led independence movements — to build international institutions that would secure the equality of states in substance and not merely in form, from the economic self-determination clauses of the NIEO to the common-heritage regime of the deep seabed ([Princeton University Press, *Worldmaking after Empire*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691179155/worldmaking-after-empire)). That reading restores the ambition of the project; the historical record must also register its defeat. The industrial powers fought the NIEO through the late 1970s conference-by-conference, and the **Brandt Commission's** 1980 report — *North–South: A Programme for Survival* — attempted to reframe the confrontation as a problem of mutual interest requiring managed reform ([World Bank Group Archives, Brandt Commission records](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/782131602496614823-0560011980/original/WorldBankGroupArchivesFolder30124821.pdf)). Its proposals went nowhere. The **Cancún summit of October 1981**, co-chaired by Mexico and Canada and gathering leaders from twenty-two countries, was the last serious attempt at global North–South negotiation; it produced no agreement, and the debt crisis that broke over Latin America the following year destroyed the coalition's bargaining position, delivering the indebted states one by one to IMF and World Bank structural-adjustment programmes that implemented, point for point, the opposite of the NIEO's agenda — liberalisation, privatisation, and the subordination of national policy to external creditors ([United Nations Digital Library, "History of UNCTAD"](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/576638)).

Yet the obituary was premature in one respect: the NIEO lost the battle for the commanding heights of the world economy while leaving deep institutional sediment. The principle of permanent sovereignty over natural resources passed into treaty law and arbitration practice; the "common heritage of mankind," first proclaimed by the Assembly for the seabed in 1970, was written into the 1982 Law of the Sea Convention; the 0.7 per cent aid target, never met, remains the standing benchmark against which donor performance is measured; and the right to development, proclaimed by the Assembly in 1986, survives in the human-rights canon and in the framing of the Sustainable Development Goals. The G77 itself did not dissolve with defeat — it grew, and it continues to negotiate as a bloc in trade, climate and development forums, so that every contemporary climate-finance conference still operates along the fault lines drawn in 1974 ([Ahram Online, "Egypt and the Chairmanship of the G77"](https://english.ahram.org.eg/NewsContentP/4/288434/Opinion/Egypt-Chairmanship-of-the-G.aspx)). The fairest verdict on the NIEO is therefore double. As a bid to redistribute wealth and decision-power in the world economy it failed, defeated by the structural leverage of capital and by divisions within the South itself; as an episode in the history of international organisation it succeeded in changing the terms of legitimacy — after 1974, the justice of the international economic order itself, and not merely the terms of access to it, became a permanent item on the agenda of every multilateral institution.

### 3.4 Keeping the peace in a decolonising world

The least anticipated of the UN's transformations was the invention of peacekeeping — a function nowhere described in the Charter, improvised in the space between the Chapter VI pacific settlement of disputes and the Chapter VII enforcement that great-power veto had paralysed. Its first instance was itself a product of decolonisation: the **United Nations Truce Supervision Organization (UNTSO)**, established in **1948** to monitor the armistice after the first Arab-Israeli war that followed Britain's withdrawal from Palestine, is still deployed today, the oldest of more than seventy operations fielded across the organisation's history ([United Nations Peacekeeping, "Our History"](https://peacekeeping.un.org/en/our-history)). The institution's modern form was fixed in **November 1956**, when the Anglo-French-Israeli invasion of Egypt — a last, calamitous assertion of imperial prerogative over a decolonising state — met a veto-bound Security Council, and the General Assembly, acting under the Uniting for Peace procedure, created the first **United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF I)** through resolutions 997, 999, 1000 and 1001 (ES-I) to interpose itself between the armies and supervise the withdrawal ([Cambridge, *American Journal of International Law*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-journal-of-international-law/article/united-nations-peacekeeping-and-host-state-consent/F8BAD9CF0746216B581E4329C7F49BBC)). The founding principles codified then — consent of the host state, impartiality, and force used only in self-defence — were not abstractions: consent mattered precisely because the troops would deploy on the territory of newly sovereign states acutely jealous of their sovereignty, and Egypt's agreement to UNEF's presence became the model of conditional, revocable international authorisation that decolonised states were willing to accept.

The Congo crisis of 1960–64 revealed both the scale of the new function and its dangers. Within days of Congolese independence on 30 June 1960 — army mutiny, Belgian intervention, and the secession of mineral-rich Katanga followed at once — the Security Council authorised the **Opération des Nations Unies au Congo (ONUC)**, which grew into a force that at points numbered around **14,500 troops from some ten countries** and evolved from buffer into active agent of the Congo's territorial integrity, fighting to end the Katangese secession in defiance of both Belgian interests and the secession's Western sympathisers ([United Nations Peacekeeping, "Past Operations"](https://peacekeeping.un.org/en/past-peacekeeping-operations)) ([UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2018/03/1005092)). The costs were severe: ONUC suffered roughly **250 fatalities**, including Secretary-General **Dag Hammarskjöld**, killed in a plane crash over Northern Rhodesia in September 1961 while flying to negotiate a ceasefire ([ResearchGate, "UN Peacekeeping in Africa"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374330899_UN_Peacekeeping_in_Africa_Nature_Scope_and_Development)). The operation also precipitated the organisation's gravest constitutional crisis: the Soviet Union and France refused to pay their shares of the costs, the International Court ruled in the 1962 *Certain Expenses* opinion that peacekeeping expenses were "expenses of the Organization" binding on all members, and the Article 19 confrontation over arrears brought the Assembly to a standstill in 1964–65. Peacekeeping was thus born entangled with decolonisation's hardest questions — secession versus territorial integrity, the legacies of colonial borders, the uses of international force in post-colonial civil wars — and the Congo experience taught a generation of diplomats that the organisation could act where the great powers were divided, but only at the price of political and financial exposure.

The same entanglement marked the function's later history. The UN's long stewardship of the Namibia question culminated in **UNTAG (1989–90)**, the transition assistance group that supervised South Africa's withdrawal, organised the elections of November 1989, and shepherded the territory — the last trust-territory-era dispute on the agenda, and the direct institutional descendant of a League mandate — to independence on **21 March 1990**, an operation widely regarded as the model of a decolonising peacekeeping success ([Oxford Public International Law, "International Territorial Administration"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1297?p)). In the post-Cold War decades the organisation went further still, assuming in Kosovo and East Timor transitional administrative powers so extensive that critics and defenders alike described them as a revival of trusteeship — the League's oldest function returning, under a new legitimacy, to govern territories pending self-determination ([Oxford Public International Law, "International Territorial Administration"](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1297?p)). By early 2022 the UN was running **12 peacekeeping operations** with roughly **87,000 uniformed and civilian personnel**, about half of them deployed in Africa, and the largest troop contributors were themselves post-colonial states — Bangladesh, Nepal, India, Pakistan, Rwanda and Ethiopia near the top of the table — a quiet reversal in which the decolonised world now supplies the soldiers of international order ([United Nations Peacekeeping, "Troop and Police Contributors"](https://peacekeeping.un.org/en/troop-and-police-contributors)). The symmetry is suggestive but should not be overdrawn: contributing troops earns poor states hard currency and diplomatic credit even as it burdens their armies with the system's most dangerous work, and the consent-based, under-resourced peacekeeping of the early 2020s bore the marks of the same hierarchy between mandating powers and supplying states that had structured international organisation since 1919.

## Conclusion: neither handmaid nor grave-digger

Across the century surveyed here, the relationship between international organisations and decolonisation resists every simple formulation. The League of Nations was created with empire's collaboration and administered a mandates system that rationalised colonial rule, yet its procedures — petitions, annual reports, the public scrutiny of the Permanent Mandates Commission — gave the colonised their first standing before an international audience and taught them the repertoire of international claim-making ([Columbia University, Susan Pedersen](https://history.columbia.edu/person/pedersen-susan-g/)). The United Nations was founded by empires, with a colonial chapter deliberately written as a non-binding declaration, yet within fifteen years its General Assembly had become the principal arena in which colonialism was delegitimised, and by 1960 the organisation had proclaimed, without a single dissenting vote, that alien subjugation was a denial of fundamental human rights ([OHCHR, Declaration on the Granting of Independence](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/declaration-granting-independence-colonial-countries-and-peoples)). And the decolonised states, once admitted, used their majority to rotate the organisation's agenda from East–West security toward North–South justice — founding UNCTAD and the G77, proclaiming a New International Economic Order, and turning peacekeeping into one of the UN's defining activities — before discovering, at Cancún and in the debt crisis, the limits of what resolutions could achieve against the structure of the world economy ([United Nations Digital Library, "History of UNCTAD"](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/576638)).

Three structural observations follow. First, international organisations mattered to decolonisation less as causes than as **multipliers of legitimacy**: no territory became independent because the UN willed it, but the UN's changing norms determined which claims could be made, in whose name, and with what presumption of right — and in the long run, as 1514 demonstrated, a presumption of right that no great power dares vote against is itself a political force. Second, the colonised were never merely objects of these institutions: from the Ewe petitioners and the South West African delegations to the diplomats of the G77, anti-colonial actors worked the procedural margins of international organisation with extraordinary skill, converting expert committees into forums, declarations into law, and votes into leverage. Third, the encounter was transformative in both directions: an organisation of 51 states designed to manage great-power peace became, through the admission of more than 140 new members, an organisation whose routine business is development, human rights and post-conflict administration — functions its Charter barely mentions ([United Nations, Growth in UN Membership](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/growth-in-un-membership)).

The story has no terminus, and the early 2020s found it in a characteristic state of incompletion. Seventeen territories remain on the UN's decolonization list, most of them small islands whose peoples have repeatedly chosen, in UN-observed acts of self-determination, arrangements other than sovereign independence — a reminder that the 1541 menu of independence, association and integration remains live, and that self-determination can now counsel staying as readily as leaving ([United Nations, Non-Self-Governing Territories](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/nsgt)). The great hierarchies the anti-colonial movement contested have proven more durable than the empires that created them: permanent seats, weighted votes and creditor power still structure the institutions the new majority captured by number but never fully remade by rule. To narrate this century as the triumph of international organisation over colonialism would be false; to narrate it as the irrelevance of international organisation to decolonisation would be falser still. The more defensible judgement is the one the evidence sustains throughout: that international organisations were simultaneously instruments of imperial management and stages for imperialism's undoing, and that the peoples of the colonised world — acting through institutions built without them, and often against them — remade both the institutions and the world those institutions govern.
