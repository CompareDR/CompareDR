# International Organizations and Decolonization in the Twentieth Century: A Complex Relationship

## Introduction

The twentieth century witnessed two parallel and deeply intertwined developments: the rise of formal international organizations as permanent features of global governance, and the dismantling of colonial empires that had shaped world politics for centuries. These two processes were not merely contemporaneous; they were constitutive of one another. International organizations provided both the institutional scaffolding within which colonial powers sought to legitimize their rule and the political arenas in which anti-colonial movements challenged that rule. Decolonization, in turn, transformed the very character of these organizations, altering their membership, agendas, and power dynamics in ways their founders could scarcely have anticipated.

This report traces this complex relationship across three periods. Part One examines the League of Nations era, from the aftermath of World War I to the end of World War II, focusing on the dual role of the Mandates System and the emergence of organized anti-imperialist opposition. Part Two analyzes the United Nations era, tracing the evolution from the Mandates System to the Trusteeship System, the Charter provisions on non-self-governing territories, and the General Assembly's transformation into the primary arena of anti-colonial struggle. Part Three examines how the influx of newly independent states reshaped international organizations, shifting the global agenda from East-West confrontation to North-South division, and giving rise to the Non-Aligned Movement, the Group of 77, and the campaign for a New International Economic Order.

Throughout, this report resists the temptation of simplistic praise or condemnation. The relationship between international organizations and decolonization was marked by structural contradictions, unintended consequences, and the persistent tension between the rhetoric of self-determination and the realities of imperial power. Understanding these complexities is essential to grasping how international institutions both constrained and enabled the end of formal colonial rule.

## Part One: The League of Nations Era — Mandates, Imperialism, and Counter-Movements (1919–1945)

### 1.1 The Mandates System: Imperial Interest Masquerading as International Accountability

The establishment of the League of Nations in 1919 represented both a rupture with and a continuation of nineteenth-century imperial order. Article 22 of the League Covenant, adopted at the Paris Peace Conference, created the Mandates System, which placed the former German colonies and Ottoman territories not under direct annexation but under the "tutelage" of mandatory powers acting on behalf of the League. This arrangement was presented as a progressive departure from old-style colonialism, premised on the principle that the "well-being and development" of these peoples constituted "a sacred trust of civilization" (Article 22, Covenant of the League of Nations).

The Mandates System classified territories into three categories. **A Mandates** applied to former Ottoman territories in the Middle East — Iraq, Palestine, and Syria-Lebanon — where the populations were deemed to have "reached a stage of development where their existence as independent nations can be provisionally recognized." The mandatory power was to provide "advice and assistance" until these territories could "stand alone." At the San Remo Conference in April 1920, Britain received the mandates for Palestine and Mesopotamia (Iraq), while France received the mandate for Syria and Lebanon. Under A Mandate terms, these territories were nominally independent but subject to mandatory administration until they achieved "political maturity."

**B Mandates** governed former German colonies in West and Central Africa — Tanganyika, Cameroon, and Togoland — where the mandatory power was responsible for direct administration, including guarantees of freedom of conscience and religion, prohibition of abuses such as forced labor, and freedom of trade. **C Mandates** covered South West Africa (to South Africa), New Guinea (to Australia), and Nauru and other Pacific islands (to Japan, Australia, and New Zealand), where the mandatory power was permitted to administer the territory "as an integral portion of its own territory" — a provision that effectively sanctioned disguised annexation.

The system's structural ambiguity is evident in its simultaneous functions. On one hand, the Mandates System served imperial interests by legally transferring control over strategically vital territories to the victorious Allied powers under a veneer of international legitimacy. The mandate agreements were negotiated and assigned by the League Council, which was dominated by the very colonial powers that would administer the territories. As scholars have noted, the Mandates System was "colonialism by another name" — it preserved the substance of imperial control while changing its legal form.

On the other hand, the system inadvertently created mechanisms for international oversight that colonial powers had not previously been subject to. The Permanent Mandates Commission (PMC), composed of experts rather than government representatives, was tasked with examining annual reports from mandatory powers, reviewing petitions from inhabitants of mandated territories, and advising the League Council on mandate administration. This represented a historically unprecedented intrusion of international oversight into what had previously been treated as the purely domestic affairs of colonial powers.

### 1.2 The Petition System: Cracks in the Imperial Edifice

The petition mechanism, though limited in its formal powers, opened what scholars have described as "several cracks through which light could enter." The PMC received and reviewed petitions from individuals and organizations within mandated territories, creating an official channel through which colonial subjects could appeal to an international body over the heads of their colonial administrators. While the PMC lacked enforcement authority and could only make recommendations to the League Council, the petition process itself subjected colonial administration to a form of international scrutiny that had no precedent.

A notable case involved South West Africa, mandated to South Africa as a C Mandate. The PMC examined South Africa's suppression of the Nama people in the Bondelzwarts rebellion of 1922, in which South African forces used military aircraft against civilians. This marked one of the first instances of an international body scrutinizing a colonial power's use of force against its subject population. The PMC's questioning of South African administration, though ultimately unable to compel policy change, established the principle that colonial governance was a matter of legitimate international concern — a principle that would be developed much further under the United Nations.

The mandates system also drew the attention of early Pan-African and anti-colonial activists. W.E.B. Du Bois, the African American scholar and activist, engaged with League institutions in an effort to apply the principle of international oversight to colonial governance in Africa. Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association similarly sought to leverage the rhetoric of self-determination that Wilson had promulgated during the Paris Peace Conference. While these efforts yielded limited concrete results, they established a pattern of anti-colonial activists engaging with international institutions that would intensify in subsequent decades.

### 1.3 The League Against Imperialism: Organized Opposition

While the League of Nations provided a limited stage for colonial oversight, a more confrontational form of anti-imperialist internationalism emerged outside and in opposition to it. The League Against Imperialism and for National Independence (LAI), founded on February 10, 1927, at the first International Congress against Colonial Oppression and Imperialism in Brussels, represented the most significant organized international challenge to colonialism in the interwar period.

The Brusselselles Congress was initiated by Willi Münzenberg, a German Communist organizer and propagandist, with the support of the Communist International (Comintern). The Congress brought together 175 delegates, of whom 107 came from 37 colonized countries, making it the most geographically diverse gathering of anti-colonial activists to that date. Delegates included Jawaharlal Nehru from India, Messali Hadj from Algeria, Mohammad Hatta from Indonesia, and representatives from China, Egypt, and various African and Latin American countries. The diversity of participation gave the Congress a genuinely global anti-colonial character that transcended the Comintern's organizational role.

The historical significance of the LAI lies in several dimensions. First, it created a transnational network linking anti-colonial movements across Asia, Africa, and the Americas — a network that would prove durable even after the LAI itself ceased to function. Nehru's participation at Brussels, for instance, established connections with Indonesian, Egyptian, and Chinese nationalists that informed his later role in organizing the Asian Relations Conference (1947) and the Bandung Conference (1955). Second, the LAI provided a public platform where colonial subjects could articulate their grievances before an international audience, circumventing the restrictions that colonial powers imposed on political organizing within their territories.

From 1927 to 1933, Berlin served as the operational center of the LAI. The organization maintained publications, coordinated solidarity campaigns, and attempted to organize a second congress (planned for Frankfurt in 1929, though it was ultimately held in Frankfurt in 1931 with reduced participation). The LAI's publications and activities provided a coherent intellectual framework for anti-colonial internationalism, linking the struggle against colonialism to broader critiques of capitalist imperialism.

The LAI's dependence on Comintern support, however, was both its enabling condition and its structural limitation. The Comintern's influence on the selection of delegates and the framing of the Congress agenda meant that the LAI's anti-imperialism was filtered through the lens of Soviet strategic priorities. This became particularly problematic after the Comintern's "Third Period" turn (from 1928), which classified social democratic parties as "social fascists" and led to the marginalization of non-communist anti-colonial voices within the LAI.

The rise of Nazism in Germany delivered the decisive blow. On January 30, 1933, Hitler came to power; the LAI's Berlin headquarters was shut down and its leadership scattered. Although the organization nominally continued to exist until 1937, it had ceased to function as an effective international organization after 1933. The LAI's dissolution was thus a consequence of geopolitical rupture rather than internal failure, though the structural constraints imposed by Comintern dependence had already weakened its capacity to serve as a genuinely broad-based anti-colonial coalition.

The legacy of the LAI proved more durable than the organization itself. Former LAI participants and affiliates carried the experience of transnational anti-colonial cooperation into the post-World War II era. George Padmore, a Trinidadian communist who had been a key figure in the LAI's Negro Bureau before breaking with the Comintern in 1934, went on to organize the Fifth Pan-African Congress in Manchester in 1945 — a gathering that many historians regard as a pivotal moment in the trajectory toward African independence. The LAI's model of international anti-colonial congresses also provided a template for the Bandung Conference of 1955, which launched the Non-Aligned Movement. In this sense, the LAI served as a precursor to the postwar decolonization movement, demonstrating both the possibilities and the limitations of organized international opposition to colonialism.

## Part Two: The United Nations Era — Institutional Frameworks and Anti-Colonial Struggle (1945–1970s)

### 2.1 The UN's Founding Stance: Compromise and Contradiction

The United Nations, established at the San Francisco Conference (April 25–June 26, 1945), inherited the contradictions of its predecessor while operating within a fundamentally transformed geopolitical context. The conference was attended by delegations from 46 nations, with the Big Four — the United States, Great Britain, the Soviet Union, and China — playing the leading roles. While the conference's dominant discussions concerned the structure of the Security Council and the veto power of permanent members, the colonial question generated significant debate that resulted in a characteristic compromise.

The founding members of the United Nations included both the world's leading colonial powers (Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium) and states committed to anti-colonial principles (the Soviet Union, China, and several Latin American republics). The resulting Charter reflected this tension. Article 1 of the Charter listed among the UN's purposes "equal rights and self-determination of peoples," and Article 55 called for "conditions of stability and well-being... based on respect for the principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples." These provisions enshrined self-determination as a founding principle of the new organization — a significant advance over the League Covenant, which had mentioned self-determination only in the limited context of A Mandates.

However, this commitment was substantially qualified by Article 2(7), which prohibited the UN from intervening "in matters which are essentially within the domestic jurisdiction of any state." Colonial powers consistently invoked this clause to argue that the administration of their colonial territories was a matter of domestic jurisdiction, not subject to international supervision. The tension between the self-determination principle and the domestic jurisdiction reservation defined the institutional battlefield on which anti-colonial struggles would be fought within the UN for decades.

### 2.2 From Mandates to Trusteeship: The Evolution of International Supervision

The UN Trusteeship System, established under Chapters XII and XIII of the Charter (Articles 75–91), represented both a continuation of and a significant departure from the League's Mandates System. Like its predecessor, the Trusteeship System placed certain territories under international supervision, with administering powers responsible for their governance. Article 77 specified three categories of territories eligible for the trusteeship system: territories previously held under mandate, territories detached from enemy states as a result of World War II, and territories voluntarily placed under the system.

The critical innovation lay in Article 76, which defined the basic objectives of the trusteeship system. These included promoting "the political, economic, social, and educational advancement of the inhabitants of the trust territories, and their progressive development towards self-government or independence." The explicit mention of "independence" as a possible outcome of the trusteeship process marked a normative advance over the Mandates System, where independence had been promised only to A Mandate territories (and even there provisionally). Under the trusteeship system, independence became a recognized end goal for all trust territories, to be achieved "as may be appropriate to the particular circumstances of each territory and its peoples and the freely expressed wishes of the peoples concerned."

Article 80 provided a bridge between the two systems, stipulating that nothing in the Charter should alter the rights of states under existing mandate agreements until new trusteeship agreements were concluded. This provision ensured legal continuity during the transition, and the International Court of Justice later relied on it to confirm that League mandates continued to exist in international law despite the League's dissolution.

The Trusteeship Council, established as a principal organ of the UN, was responsible for supervising the administration of trust territories. Its functions included examining annual reports from administering powers, considering petitions from inhabitants (continuing the PMC's practice), and sending periodic visiting missions to trust territories. These visiting missions were a significant innovation — international observers physically traveling to trust territories to assess conditions and consult with local populations. The missions created a direct mechanism of international presence in colonial territories that went beyond the PMC's paper-based oversight, and scholars have noted that these visiting missions accelerated the movement toward self-determination by providing trust territory populations with direct access to international fora.

### 2.3 Chapter XI: The Non-Self-Governing Territories Provisions

Perhaps the most significant — and most contested — colonial provisions of the UN Charter were contained in Chapter XI (Articles 73–74), titled "Declaration Regarding Non-Self-Governing Territories." Unlike the Trusteeship System, which applied only to territories specifically placed under it through individual agreements, Chapter XI applied automatically to all territories whose peoples had not yet attained "a full measure of self-government." This meant that the vast majority of colonial territories — those administered by colonial powers under ordinary colonial sovereignty, not under mandate or trusteeship — were brought within the scope of UN concern.

Article 73 imposed what the Charter called a "sacred trust" on member states administering non-self-governing territories. The specific obligations included:

- **Article 73(a):** Ensuring the political, economic, social, and educational advancement of inhabitants
- **Article 73(b):** Developing self-government, "taking due account of the political aspirations of the peoples, and assisting them in the progressive development of their free political institutions"
- **Article 73(c):** Promoting international peace and security
- **Article 73(d):** Promoting constructive measures of development
- **Article 73(e):** Transmitting regularly to the Secretary-General information relating to economic, social, and educational conditions in the territories

The last provision — the obligation to transmit information — was particularly significant. It created a mandatory reporting requirement that brought colonial administration within the scope of international scrutiny, even if the reporting itself did not constitute intervention in domestic jurisdiction. The information transmitted under Article 73(e) became the empirical basis for the UN's examination of colonial conditions, and the reporting requirement created the institutional foundation upon which the anti-colonial majority in the General Assembly would build increasingly assertive oversight mechanisms.

### 2.4 The General Assembly as Arena: Anti-Colonial Struggle Through Institutional Channels

The transformation of the General Assembly into the primary arena of anti-colonial struggle was not foreseen by the UN's founders but followed logically from the Charter's institutional structure and the changing composition of UN membership. The General Assembly, where each member state had one vote regardless of size or power, was the one UN organ where newly independent states could exercise influence proportionate to their numbers. As decolonization proceeded and former colonies joined the UN, the balance of voting power in the Assembly shifted decisively.

The **Committee on Information from Non-Self-Governing Territories**, established under Article 73(e), was the first institutional mechanism through which the UN examined colonial conditions. The Committee reviewed the information transmitted by colonial powers and provided a forum for questioning administering powers about conditions in their territories. While its formal powers were limited to examining reports, the Committee created a precedent: colonial administration was subject to international examination, and colonial powers were required to justify their governance before an international body.

The **Special Committee on Decolonization** (known as the C-24), established by the General Assembly in 1961, represented a more assertive institutional response. The C-24 was mandated to monitor progress toward granting independence to territories that had not yet achieved "a full measure of self-government." Its mandate derived directly from the 1960 Declaration on Decolonization, and its establishment signaled the General Assembly's willingness to move beyond passive report examination toward active promotion of decolonization. The C-24 could hear petitioners from non-self-governing territories, send missions to assess conditions, and make recommendations to the General Assembly. The Fourth Committee (Special Political and Decolonization Committee) of the General Assembly served as the primary deliberative body where these issues were debated, with petitioners from colonial territories sometimes appearing to testify about conditions under colonial rule.

The significance of these institutional mechanisms lies not in their enforcement capacity — which remained limited — but in their role in shifting the normative framework. By requiring colonial powers to report, by hearing testimony from colonial subjects, and by passing resolutions condemning colonial practices, the General Assembly and its subsidiary committees created an environment in which colonial rule became progressively more difficult to legitimize. The institutional arena of the UN became a site of anti-colonial struggle in a double sense: it was a place where anti-colonial states could organize politically, and it was a forum where the illegitimacy of colonialism was continuously asserted and reinforced.

### 2.5 The 1960 Declaration: A Watershed in International Law

The adoption of the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples (General Assembly Resolution 1514) on December 14, 1960, marked a watershed in the international community's stance toward colonialism. The Declaration, adopted by a vote of 89 to 0 with 9 abstentions, declared that "the subjection of peoples to alien subjugation, domination and exploitation constitutes a violation of the Charter of the United Nations" and that "all peoples have the right to self-determination."

The key provisions of Resolution 1514 included:

1. The necessity of bringing "a speedy and unconditional end to colonialism in all its forms and manifestations"
2. The affirmation that "all peoples have the right to self-determination; by virtue of that right they freely determine their political status and freely pursue their economic, social and cultural development"
3. The requirement that "inadequacy of political, economic, social or educational preparedness should never serve as a pretext for delaying independence" — a direct rejection of the "sacred trust" logic that had justified gradualism under both the Mandates and Trusteeship Systems
4. The prohibition of attempts aimed at "the partial or total disruption of the national unity and the territorial integrity of a country"
5. The call for immediate steps to be taken in trust and non-self-governing territories to transfer all powers to the peoples of those territories

The voting pattern is itself revealing. The 89 states voting in favor included all the newly independent African and Asian states, the Soviet bloc, and most Latin American countries. The 9 abstentions — Britain, France, Belgium, Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands, Australia, South Africa, and the United States — comprised the principal colonial powers and their closest allies. The fact that no state voted against the Declaration indicated that even the colonial powers could not openly oppose the principle of decolonization. Their abstention, however, signaled their refusal to endorse an obligation they regarded as going beyond the Charter's compromises and violating the domestic jurisdiction principle.

The Declaration's historical significance is multifaceted. First, it reinterpreted the "sacred trust" of Article 73, transforming it from an obligation to govern responsibly into an obligation to transfer power. The argument that colonial powers had a duty to prepare their subject peoples for self-government was replaced by the argument that they had a duty to grant independence immediately. Second, by rejecting "inadequacy of preparedness" as a pretext for delay, the Declaration struck at the heart of the paternalistic justification for colonialism that had underpinned both the Mandates and Trusteeship Systems. Third, the Declaration immediately gave rise to new institutional mechanisms: the C-24 was established in 1961 specifically to monitor its implementation, demonstrating that the Declaration was not merely symbolic but was intended to generate operational consequences.

## Part Three: The Transformative Impact of Decolonization on International Organizations

### 3.1 The Changing Composition of the United Nations

The most visible consequence of decolonization for international organizations was the dramatic transformation of their membership. The United Nations was founded with 51 member states in 1945, most of them Euro-Atlantic powers and Latin American republics. By 1955, membership had grown to 76. The watershed year was 1960, when 16 new African states were admitted, bringing total membership to 99. By 1965, membership stood at 117 — more than double the founding number. By the mid-1970s, the UN had 144 members, and by 2026, 193. Since 1945, more than 80 former colonies comprising some 750 million people have gained independence through the decolonization process.

This quantitative change produced a qualitative transformation. The General Assembly, where each state had one vote, became an institution where Western and colonial powers could no longer control outcomes through their own voting power. The newly independent states of Asia and Africa formed a natural majority on colonial issues and increasingly on economic and social questions as well. This majority did not always vote as a monolithic bloc — the diversity of interests among developing countries meant that unity was easier to achieve on declaratory anti-colonial resolutions than on substantive policy issues. Nevertheless, the structural shift in voting power meant that the UN's agenda increasingly reflected the priorities of the new majority rather than those of its founding powers.

The impact extended beyond the General Assembly. The Secretariat, the specialized agencies, and the UN's developmental and technical assistance programs were all reshaped by the influx of new member states. Development issues — which had been marginal to the UN's founding agenda, dominated as it was by security concerns — moved to the center of the organization's work. The expansion of technical cooperation programs, the creation of new funds and programs focused on developing countries, and the increasing centrality of economic and social questions to the UN's agenda were all direct consequences of the changing membership.

### 3.2 From East-West to North-South: The Shift in Global Cleavages

The influx of newly independent states altered the fundamental axis of international conflict within the UN. During the founding period and the early Cold War, the dominant cleavage in international politics was the East-West division between the Soviet bloc and the Western alliance. The newly independent states, however, did not fit neatly into this binary. While many received support from the Soviet bloc in their liberation struggles and adopted socialist-oriented economic policies, others aligned with the West or pursued non-aligned positions. The defining cleavage for these states was not the Cold War ideological divide but the economic and political gap between the industrialized North and the developing South.

This shift from East-West to North-South was not merely rhetorical. It restructured the bargaining dynamics within the UN and other international organizations. On colonial issues, the North-South cleavage produced near-automatic majorities in favor of anti-colonial resolutions. On economic issues, it generated sustained pressure for redistribution of global economic power — pressure that found expression in the demands for a New International Economic Order. The North-South framework also reshaped how issues like human rights, arms control, and environmental protection were discussed, as developing countries insisted that these issues could not be separated from the economic inequalities produced by colonialism.

The shift was not without tension. The superpowers continued to pursue their Cold War rivalries through proxy conflicts in the decolonized world, and the Non-Aligned Movement's effort to maintain equidistance from both blocs was tested by the competitive interventions of the United States and Soviet Union in post-colonial states. Nevertheless, the emergence of the North-South framework meant that international organizations could no longer be understood primarily as arenas of superpower rivalry; they had become sites of a more fundamental contestation over global economic and political order.

### 3.3 The Non-Aligned Movement

The Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) emerged as the most important political organization of newly independent states, providing a collective voice for countries seeking to navigate between the Cold War blocs. The NAM's origins lie in the Bandung Conference of April 1955, held in Bandung, Indonesia, where 29 Asian and African states met to discuss common concerns including colonialism, racism, and Cold War tensions. The Bandung Conference articulated the principles of peaceful coexistence — mutual respect for sovereignty, non-aggression, non-interference in internal affairs, and peaceful resolution of disputes — which became the foundational principles of non-alignment.

The first official Conference of Non-Aligned Countries was held in Belgrade in September 1961, at the initiative of Yugoslavia, Egypt, India, and Indonesia. The Belgrade Conference established the NAM as a formal movement, with regular summits and a coordinating mechanism. During the Cold War, the NAM played a significant role in mediating superpower tensions, advocating for disarmament, and particularly in accelerating the decolonization process through its advocacy within the UN. The movement also served as a vehicle for developing countries to promote their collective interests in international economic relations, pushing for reform of what they saw as an inequitable global economic order inherited from the colonial era.

The NAM's relationship with international organizations was symbiotic. The movement operated primarily through the UN, using the General Assembly as its principal arena for collective action. NAM members coordinated their voting positions in the General Assembly and used their numerical majority to advance anti-colonial and pro-development resolutions. At the same time, the NAM's existence as an organized bloc amplified the influence of its members within the UN, transforming the General Assembly from a forum dominated by great-power diplomacy into an institution where collective action by smaller and less powerful states could produce real outcomes.

### 3.4 The Group of 77 and Economic Demands

The Group of 77 (G-77) was established in 1964 at the first United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) in Geneva, when 77 developing countries issued a Joint Declaration presenting a comprehensive set of proposals on international economic relations, trade, and development. The G-77 was created to strengthen the bargaining power of developing countries in international economic negotiations, to promote their collective economic interests, and to push for reforms to what they viewed as an unjust international economic order.

The G-77's founding objectives included changing unequal international economic relations, establishing a more equitable and fair international economic order, and pursuing economic modernization free from the structural constraints inherited from colonialism. The G-77 operated through coordinated positions in UN economic fora, annual ministerial meetings, and coordinated voting in the General Assembly on economic questions. By 2025, the G-77 had grown to 133 member states, making it the largest intergovernmental grouping of developing countries in the United Nations.

The G-77's activities were organized around several key institutions: the UNCTAD conferences, where developing countries pressed for reform of the international trading system; the General Assembly's economic committees; and special sessions on economic issues. The G-77's most ambitious initiative was the campaign for a New International Economic Order.

### 3.5 The New International Economic Order

The New International Economic Order (NIEO) was a comprehensive package of policy proposals aimed at restructuring the global economy to improve the position of developing countries relative to the industrialized states. The NIEO agenda was assembled at the Non-Aligned Movement conference in Algiers in September 1973 and was subsequently pursued through the G-77 at the United Nations.

In April 1974, at a special session of the UN General Assembly, the G-77 secured the adoption of the Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order (Resolution 3201) and the Programme of Action (Resolution 3202), despite the opposition of the United States and a small group of advanced industrialized countries. The NIEO demands included:

- Reform of the international trading system to ensure stable and remunerative prices for commodity exports from developing countries
- Regulation of transnational corporations, including a code of conduct
- Reform of the international monetary system, including greater developing-country participation in decision-making
- Increased development assistance and technology transfer
- Sovereign control over natural resources, including the right to nationalize foreign-owned assets
- Reform of the Law of the Sea to benefit developing coastal states

The NIEO was described as a political and economic challenge to the industrialized countries because it aimed to change traditional North-South economic relations in favor of the developing world. Western industrialized countries viewed the demand for a NIEO as an assault on the free market economy and feared the establishment of a state-directed international planned economy. The NIEO campaign represented the high-water mark of developing-country assertiveness within the UN, demonstrating how the changed composition of the organization had enabled a fundamental challenge to the existing global economic order.

However, the NIEO initiative ultimately failed to achieve its objectives. The oil crises of the 1970s, the debt crisis of the 1980s, and the broader shift toward neoliberal economic policies under the Reagan and Thatcher administrations undermined the political and economic conditions that had made the NIEO campaign possible. After the dissolution of the Soviet empire and the end of the East-West conflict — which developing countries had leveraged as leverage to advance their demands — the NIEO agenda lost momentum. The structural shift in the world economy toward deregulation, privatization, and market-oriented reforms further marginalized the NIEO's state-centered approach to economic justice. Nevertheless, the NIEO episode remains significant as a demonstration of how decolonization had transformed international organizations from institutions managing the existing order into arenas where the fundamental principles of that order could be contested.

### 3.6 New Functions: Peacekeeping and the Management of Decolonization

Decolonization also generated new institutional functions for the United Nations, most notably in the area of peacekeeping. While the UN Charter did not originally envision peacekeeping as a core function, the pressures of decolonization and the Cold War created situations that demanded forms of international presence that the Charter's frameworks of collective security and trusteeship could not address.

The precedent for UN peacekeeping was established during the Suez Crisis of 1956, when the first armed UN peacekeeping force (UNEF I) was deployed to the Sinai to separate Egyptian and Israeli forces following the Anglo-French intervention. The concept, developed by Canadian Secretary of State for External Affairs Lester Pearson and UN Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld, involved the deployment of lightly armed international forces with the consent of the parties to a conflict, mandated to maintain ceasefires and stabilize post-conflict situations. The General Assembly's "Uniting for Peace" resolution (1950) had already established that the Assembly could act on matters of international peace when the Security Council was paralyzed by veto, providing the legal basis for Assembly-authorized peacekeeping.

Decolonization directly generated several major peacekeeping operations. The UN Operation in the Congo (ONUC), deployed from 1960 to 1964, was the largest and most complex peacekeeping operation of its era, involving nearly 20,000 personnel at its peak. ONUC was created in response to the crisis that followed the abrupt independence of the Congo (now Democratic Republic of the Congo) from Belgian colonial rule, which had left the new state without functioning institutions and facing a mutiny within the army and secessionist movements in Katanga. The operation's mandate evolved from traditional peacekeeping to include the use of force to prevent civil war and the reunification of the country — a controversial expansion that foreshadowed later debates about the appropriate scope of peacekeeping mandates.

The UN Peacekeeping Force in Cyprus (UNFICYP), established in 1964, addressed another conflict with colonial origins: the tensions between Greek Cypriot and Turkish Cypriot communities following Cyprus's independence from Britain in 1960. These operations demonstrated that decolonization did not produce a clean transition to independent statehood but often generated instability, communal conflict, and great-power intervention that required ongoing international engagement.

Peacekeeping thus emerged as a distinct institutional function that was not anticipated in the UN Charter but was necessitated by the disorderly consequences of decolonization and the Cold War's inhibiting effect on Security Council action. The development of peacekeeping illustrated a broader pattern: decolonization not only changed the membership and agenda of international organizations but also generated new institutional capacities that became permanent features of the international system.

## Conclusion

The relationship between international organizations and decolonization in the twentieth century defies simple characterization. International organizations were neither straightforward instruments of colonial liberation nor mere vehicles for the preservation of imperial power. They were, rather, arenas of contestation in which the principles, mechanisms, and language of international governance were continuously negotiated between competing interests and visions.

The League of Nations' Mandates System exemplifies this complexity. It was designed to preserve the colonial order by legitimating the transfer of territory to mandatory powers, yet it also created the first institutional mechanisms for international oversight of colonial administration. The petition system, the Permanent Mandates Commission's review processes, and the principle that colonial governance was subject to international accountability — all unintended consequences of a system designed to manage colonial transfers — established precedents that would be developed under the United Nations into increasingly assertive mechanisms of anti-colonial oversight.

The League Against Imperialism, standing in opposition to the League of Nations, represented a different model of international anti-colonial organization — one that was explicitly oppositional rather than reformist. Its legacy, carried through figures like George Padmore and through the institutional template it provided for later congresses, demonstrated that the relationship between international organizations and decolonization was not confined to formal intergovernmental institutions but also encompassed the transnational networks and organizations that operated alongside and against them.

The United Nations inherited and intensified these contradictions. Its Charter simultaneously enshrined self-determination as a founding principle and protected colonial jurisdiction under the domestic jurisdiction clause. Its Trusteeship System explicitly named independence as an outcome but applied only to a fraction of colonial territories. Chapter XI imposed reporting obligations on colonial powers but could not compel them to act. The resolution of these contradictions came not through institutional design but through political struggle — the steady accumulation of anti-colonial majorities in the General Assembly, the strategic use of institutional mechanisms like the C-24 and the Fourth Committee, and the normative transformation signaled by the 1960 Declaration on Decolonization.

The impact of decolonization on international organizations was equally transformative. The influx of former colonies fundamentally altered the composition, agenda, and power dynamics of the UN and other international bodies. The shift from East-West to North-South cleavage, the rise of the Non-Aligned Movement and the Group of 77, and the campaign for a New International Economic Order all demonstrated that decolonization did not merely add new members to existing institutions but reshaped the very purposes and priorities of those institutions. Even the development of UN peacekeeping — now a defining function of the organization — was substantially driven by the destabilizing consequences of decolonization.

The story of international organizations and decolonization is, ultimately, a story of unintended consequences and structural contradictions. Institutions designed to manage an imperial order became instruments for its dismantling. Principles articulated to legitimate limited reforms became the basis for revolutionary demands. And mechanisms created to preserve stability became catalysts for transformation. The complexity of this relationship — neither a triumphal narrative of liberation through international institutions nor a cynical account of institutional complicity in colonial domination — reflects the genuinely ambivalent role that international organizations have played in one of the most significant political transformations of the modern era.
