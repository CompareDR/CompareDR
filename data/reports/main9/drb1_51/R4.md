# Japan's Elderly Population and Consumer Market, 2020–2050

## 1. The headline answer

**Population.** Japan's population aged 65 and over rises from 36.03 million (28.6% of the total) at the 2020 Census to a peak of 39.53 million (35.8%) in 2043, then eases to roughly 38 million (about 36–37% of the total) by 2050. The total population, already declining since its 2008 peak of 128.0 million, falls from 126.15 million in 2020 to about 104–105 million by 2050 and 87.0 million by 2070. The elderly share keeps rising even after the absolute count peaks, because the total population shrinks faster ([MHLW](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12506000/001106969.pdf); [IPSS](https://www.ipss.go.jp/pp-zenkoku/e/zenkoku_e2023/pp2023e_Summary.pdf); [JCER](https://www.jcer.or.jp/english/new-population-projection-how-does-it-differ-from-the-old-one)).

**Consumption potential.** On a household out-of-pocket basis — excluding public pension, medical-insurance and long-term-care transfers — Japan's elderly consumption market is ≈¥57 trillion/year (2025 prices) in 2020, peaks near ¥60–62 trillion around 2040–2043, and settles at ≈¥58–62 trillion by 2050. The cumulative 2020–2050 out-of-pocket opportunity is on the order of ¥1.7–1.8 quadrillion (2025 prices). The broader "senior market" that also counts publicly funded medical and long-term care is ≈¥96.4 trillion (FY2023) and reaches ≈¥114.7 trillion by FY2040 ([Mizuho Bank Industry Research Dept.](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/pdf/msif_241.pdf)).

**The structural story.** The elderly market is not one market but two: a growing "young-old" (65–74) segment that behaves like a premium consumer market, and a faster-growing "old-old" (75+/85+) segment that behaves like a care, medical and service market. Because the 65+ count peaks in 2043 while the 85+ share keeps rising, the category mix shifts decisively toward health, care, housing and services after 2040 even as the headline population stagnates ([MHLW care-insurance presentation](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12300000/001364995.pdf)).

**Willingness is the binding constraint.** Elderly households are asset-rich but income-poor; willingness to spend is constrained less by ability to pay than by longevity-risk anxiety and prospective care costs. Need-driven categories (medical, care, housing modification, mobility) are therefore more insulated than discretionary categories (clothing, leisure, travel) ([Cabinet Office FY2024 survey](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_3.html)).

## 2. Population projections: how many elderly, and in what structure

### 2.1 Headcount and share

The 65+ population is carried by two large cohorts: the post-war baby boom (born 1947–1949) and the "junior baby boom" (born 1971–1974). The junior boom begins turning 65 in 2036, lifting the count through the early 2040s; the 2043 peak coincides with that cohort being in its late 60s while the pre-war cohorts have largely died out. After 2043, the smaller birth cohorts of the 1980s–90s enter the 65+ group while mortality rises among the large older cohorts, so the absolute count declines. The total population, by contrast, declines throughout because the medium-variant total fertility rate (1.33 in 2020; 1.36 in 2070) is far below replacement ([MHLW](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12506000/001106969.pdf); [IPSS](https://www.ipss.go.jp/pp-zenkoku/e/zenkoku_e2023/pp2023e_Summary.pdf)).

### 2.2 Age structure and care needs

The 65+ block is aging from within. The 85+ band rises from 6.16 million (17.1% of the 65+) in 2020 to 10.06 million (25.6%) in 2040, then eases to 9.61 million (26.1%) in 2050 — a level plateau caused by the smaller birth cohorts of the 1950s–60s reaching advanced age. The 75–84 band follows a "W" path: it dips from 14.49 million (2030) to 12.21 million (2040) as the baby-boom generation moves into the 85+ band, then rebounds to 14.72 million by 2050 as the large 1971–74 cohort enters. By mid-century roughly two-thirds of Japan's elderly are 75 or older and more than one in four is 85 or older — a structure radically different from 2020, when the 65–74 band was the largest single group ([MHLW care-insurance presentation](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12300000/001364995.pdf)).

Women dominate at advanced ages. At 1 October 2024 the 65+ population was 36.24 million — 15.72 million men and 20.53 million women (sex ratio 76.6); among the 85+ the ratio is 47.7. With female life expectancy projected at 91.94 years by 2070 (male 85.89), the oldest-old population is predominantly female and disproportionately solo-living ([高齢社会白書 2025](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_1_1.html)).

Care-need rates — the long-term-care insurance certification rate (要介護・要支援認定率) — rise steeply with age, which is why the 85+ band drives the growth in care-eligible population:

The number of certified persons was 6.81 million in 2022 and is projected to peak at about 9.88 million around 2040, with the increase concentrated in the 85+ band ([健康長寿ネット, citing MHLW](https://www.tyojyu.or.jp/net/kenkou-tyoju/tyojyu-shakai-mondai/kaigojinzai-fusoku.html)). Because a 65–74 person has only a 4.3% certification rate versus 31.3% for the 75+ and 57.7% for the 85+, the care-eligible population grows almost in lock-step with the 85+ headcount, not with the 65+ headcount.

Dependency ratios confirm the pressure: the old-age dependency ratio (65+ per 100 working-age adults) is projected to reach about 79% by 2050, the highest in the OECD ([OECD, 2024](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2024/04/addressing-demographic-headwinds-in-japan-a-long-term-perspective_85b9a67f/96648955-en.pdf)). In IPSS terms, the 15–64 population per 65+ person falls from 12.1 in 1955 to 2.0 in 2024 and 1.3 in 2070 ([高齢社会白書 2025](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_1_1.html)).

### 2.3 Elderly households: the demand-relevant unit

The number of households headed by someone 65+ (高齢者世帯) rises from 20.97 million in 2020 to a peak of 24.31 million in 2045, then eases to 24.04 million in 2050 — 3.07 million (+14.6%) more than 2020, with the share of all households climbing from 37.6% to 45.7% ([IPSS 2024](https://www.ipss.go.jp/pp-ajsetai/j/HPRJ2024/hprj2024_PR.pdf)). The household count peaks two years after the 65+ population peaks because single-person and couple-only formation keeps multiplying even as the headcount stagnates.

Single-elderly households (head 65+, living alone) are the fastest-growing segment: 7.38 million in 2020 to 10.84 million in 2050, a 46.9% increase from 2020; the 2025 level is about 8.16 million, so the 2025→2050 increase is about 32.8% ([全社協, 2026](https://www.zenshakyo.org/oyakudachi/?p=3217); [IPSS 2024](https://www.ohmae.ac.jp/mbaswitch/future-number-of-families/)). By 2050, single-elderly households reach 45.1% of all 65+ households and 20.6% of all households ([MHLW summary of IPSS 2024](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12600000/001607189.pdf)).

Three definitions must not be mixed:

- **高齢者世帯 (elderly household)** = a household whose head (世帯主) is 65+. This is the IPSS projection basis.
- **65歳以上の者のいる世帯 (households containing a 65+ person)** = any ordinary household with a 65+ member, including three-generation households headed by a younger person. The 2020 Census counted 22.66 million such households, 40.7% of ordinary households ([総務省統計局](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kokusei/2020/kekka/pdf/outline_01.pdf)).
- **高齢者単身世帯 (single-elderly household)** = a household whose head is 65+ and who lives alone.

The IPSS projection covers only ordinary households (一般世帯); institutional households (施設等の世帯) — hospital inpatients, social-welfare facility residents, dormitory residents — are excluded and tabulated separately. Market sizing must therefore state whether institutionalized elderly are inside or outside the boundary ([IPSS projection page](https://www.ipss.go.jp/pp-ajsetai/j/HPRJ2024/t-page.asp)).

### 2.4 Regional distribution

Aging is national, but intensity and absolute size diverge sharply. Akita has the highest 65+ share (39.5% in 2024 rising to 49.9% in 2050) and Tokyo the lowest (22.7% rising to 29.6%); 25 prefectures are projected to exceed a 40% 65+ share by 2050. The largest absolute elderly populations remain in the metropolitan prefectures — Tokyo (3,215 thousand in 2024), Osaka (2,418), Kanagawa (2,400), Saitama (2,018), Aichi (1,928), Chiba (1,759), Hokkaido (1,677), Hyogo (1,611), Fukuoka (1,456), Shizuoka (1,101) ([内閣府 高齢社会白書 2025](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_1_4.html)). Single-elderly households exceed 20% of all households in 32 prefectures by 2050, up from zero in 2020; Kochi is highest at 27.0%, followed by Tokushima (25.3%), Ehime (24.9%), Kagoshima (24.8%) and Aomori (24.6%), while Fukui (18.6%) and Tokyo (18.7%) are lowest ([IPSS 2024 prefectural projection](https://www.ohmae.ac.jp/mbaswitch/future-number-of-families/)). Rural prefectures combine the highest aging shares and the greatest care needs with shrinking absolute markets; metropolitan prefectures combine lower aging shares with the largest absolute elderly markets.

### 2.5 Projection variants and revision drift

The IPSS 2023 revision is a family of nine variants (3 fertility assumptions × 3 mortality assumptions), not a single forecast. The 2023 revision moved all three demographic assumptions relative to 2017: fertility down (long-run TFR 1.44 → 1.36), life expectancy up (male +0.94 years, female +0.59 by 2070), and net migration up (≈70,000 → ≈160,000/year). The 65+ peak moved from 2042 (39.35 million) to 2043 (39.53 million), and the year the total population falls below 100 million moved from 2053 to 2056 ([MHLW](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12601000/001091141.pdf); [JCER](https://www.jcer.or.jp/english/new-population-projection-how-does-it-differ-from-the-old-one)).

**Which assumption dominates the 2050 65+ uncertainty?** Fertility has essentially no direct effect through 2050 (a child born in 2020 does not reach 65 until 2085); fertility moves the 0–14 and 15–64 populations and therefore the 65+ *share*. Mortality directly sets survival of the 65+ population and dominates the 65+ count uncertainty. Migration sits in between: the assumed net inflow is concentrated in working ages, raising the total population and lowering the 65+ share more than it raises the 65+ count ([MHLW](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12601000/001091141.pdf); [IPSS](https://www.ipss.go.jp/pp-zenkoku/e/zenkoku_e2023/pp2023e_Summary.pdf)).

| Period | Confidence | Recommended presentation |
|---|---|---|
| 2020–2030 | High | Point estimate (medium/medium) with a narrow range |
| 2030–2040 | Medium | Medium/medium base with the high/low mortality band |
| 2040–2050 | Low | Range only; medium/medium as central tendency; high/low mortality as the primary band |

2050 is near the edge of reliable projection: the 65+ count is past its peak and declining, and the variants can shift it by several million. The exact 2050 65+ values by variant sit in IPSS Tables 1-1 to 1-3 and 2-1 to 2-2 and should be verified before being used as market-size inputs.

## 3. Ability and willingness to pay

### 3.1 Income and pension dependence

Public pension benefits — the 国民年金 (basic pension) and 厚生年金 (earnings-related pension) — are the primary income source for most elderly households. Japan's 2024 pension fiscal verification calculated a model replacement rate (所得代替率, the ratio of the model pension benefit to the average take-home income of a working-age male) of 61.2% for FY2024, based on a model couple receiving the basic pension (¥134,000/month) plus one earnings-related pension (¥92,000/month), against average take-home income of ¥370,000/month. Once the benefit-level adjustment mechanism (マクロ経済スライド, macroeconomic slide) ends (projected FY2063), the rate falls to 45.3%, below the statutory floor of 50% ([厚生労働省 令和6年財政検証](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/001270476.pdf); [第一生命経済研究所](https://www.dlri.co.jp/report/ld/271001.html); figures from search results, pages not independently fetched in this pass). The replacement rate is progressive — lower for higher earners, higher for lower earners — because the basic pension provides a floor. This means the consumption capacity of lower-income elderly households is more dependent on the public pension and less on accumulated assets.

### 3.2 Savings, assets, and dissaving

Elderly households are asset-rich but income-poor. Two-or-more-person households headed by someone 65+ surveyed in the 2014 National Survey of Family Income and Expenditure (NSFIE) held mean net savings (純貯蓄, financial assets minus liabilities) of ¥24.99 million and median net savings of ¥15.88 million ([総務省「高齢者の家計」](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/topics/topi904.html)). Home ownership among households with elderly persons was 81.6% in the 2023 Housing and Land Survey ([総務省](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/jyutaku/2023/pdf/kihon_gaiyou.pdf)); the 2025 White Paper puts 65+ owner-occupation at 83.0% ([内閣府](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_2_4.html)). Personal financial assets stood at ¥2,141 trillion at end-2023 and are projected to reach ¥2,760 trillion by 2040, with cash and deposits accounting for 55.3% ([NLIリサーチ](https://www.nli-research.co.jp/report/detail/id=77962?site=nli); [第一生命経済研究所](https://www.dlri.co.jp/report/ld/423458.html)).

Yet the elderly are already dissaving. The couple-only 65+ non-working household spent ¥263,979/month on consumption in 2025 against disposable income of ¥221,544 — an average monthly shortfall of about ¥42,400 funded from savings ([総務省 家計調査 2025](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2025.pdf); [MUFG Bank](https://www.bk.mufg.jp/column/others/b0117.html)). Single non-working 65+ households spent roughly ¥148,000/month ([FIES 2025](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2025.pdf); [JILI summary](https://www.jili.or.jp/lifeplan/lifesecurity/1130.html)). Horioka finds that declines in the saving rate of retired elderly households are a more important explanation for the recent decline in Japan's household saving rate than demographic age structure alone; the overall household saving rate was 1.1% in 2024, near historic lows ([Horioka, NBER Working Paper 33181](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33181/w33181.pdf)).

The "2000万円 problem" (老後資金2000万円問題) originated in the 2019 FSA report, which calculated that a couple aged 60 would need about ¥20 million in savings to cover the gap between pension income and living expenses over 20–30 years of retirement ([金融庁, 2019](https://www.fsa.go.jp/singi/singi_kinyu/tosin/20190603/01.pdf)). Combined with a strong bequest motive, this suppresses consumption: assets are held as a buffer against longevity risk, future care costs and bequests rather than drawn down to fund current consumption.

### 3.3 Willingness to spend: anxiety, bequest, and precaution

In the Cabinet Office's FY2024 survey of people aged 60+, 65.9% said they live without economic worry and 30.7% said they are worried; the worried share rises to 41.5% among single-living elderly and 42.1% among women aged 80–84. Willingness to spend is constrained less by ability to pay — 75.7% receive public pension income and average financial assets are ¥17.69 million — than by a perceived gap between actual and required resources: average monthly living expenditure (¥206,000) falls ¥42,000 short of what respondents consider necessary (¥248,000), and average financial assets fall roughly ¥8.8 million short of perceived need (¥26.48 million) ([Cabinet Office FY2024 survey, ch. 2-3](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_3.html); [ch. 2-4](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_4.html)).

Only 36.7% feel their savings are sufficient or at least minimal; 35.9% want to leave assets to survivors versus 33.0% who want to use assets for themselves; and 61.2% already draw down savings, but by an average of only ¥68,000 per month — decumulation is real but deliberately slow ([ch. 2-4](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_4.html)).

What concerns elderly consumers is forward-looking: price rises (cited by 70%+), followed by low income/savings, the cost of moving to a paid nursing home or losing independent living, disasters, and excessive medical/care costs. The preparations people regard as necessary are dominated by health (80.7%), then end-of-life planning (38.1%), housing (25.5%) and asset formation (24.2%); only 37.4% are actively preparing anything. When asked what support they need, medical/health tops the list (87.5%), followed by daily-life support (52.8%) and public transport/mobility (51.3%) ([2025 White Paper, s1-3-2](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_3_2.html); [ch. 2-4](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_4.html)). Intergenerational transfers divert spending: 25.2% of the 60+ bear some or most of their children's/grandchildren's living costs, concentrated among men up to 69 and women up to 64; among those co-residing with a child, the share reaches 49.8%. No verified primary source was obtained on the receiving direction, so the net direction of intergenerational flows cannot be established here ([ch. 2-3](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_3.html)).

### 3.4 Who spends: differences by age, household type, income, region

The young-old are materially more active than the old-old. Men up to 64 report work income at 86.1%, with the highest average living expenses (¥240,000/month) and financial assets (¥23.32 million); asset-formation preparedness is over 40% only up to age 64 and declines with age. At the other end, women aged 85+ report the lowest living expenses (¥140,000/month), women aged 80–84 show the highest worry (42.1%), and men aged 80–84 are the most likely to draw down savings (70.9%) ([ch. 2-3](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_3.html); [ch. 2-4](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_4.html)).

Household type is a sharper divider than age: single-living elderly are worried at 41.5%, earn less (¥2.32 million/year vs ¥3.66 million for co-residents), and hold fewer assets (¥12.93 million vs ¥18.82 million). Renters and divorced elderly are the most likely to feel their savings are insufficient (72.9–74.2% and 76.0%, respectively), while the 85+ group is the most likely to feel sufficient (~50%) — likely because needs and housing costs have fallen and assets are largely paid off ([ch. 2-3](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_3.html); [ch. 2-4](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_4.html)).

Is the "reluctant elderly saver" stereotype weakening? The evidence is mixed and cohort-dependent. Among the 65–74 band, higher work income, higher living expenses and continued employment suggest a more active consumer than the stereotype implies. Yet aggregate indicators point the other way: savings sufficiency is felt by only 36.7%, the bequest motive slightly exceeds self-use intent, and 2019→2024 saw rising insurance enrollment and a slight fall in "no worry." A reasonable reading is that the stereotype is stratified: the 65–74 young-old are relatively willing spenders, while the 75+ and single-living women remain the most precautionary ([ch. 2-4](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter2_4.html); [2025 White Paper, s1-3-3](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_3_3.html)).

## 4. Category market sizes and trajectories

### 4.1 Food and beverage

Food is the largest category of elderly out-of-pocket consumption, and its *share* of the budget rises with age: the 2025 Family Income and Expenditure Survey (FIES) puts the Engel coefficient at 31.4% for households headed by someone 70+, 28.2% for those 60–69, and 23.3% for those under 30 ([giken.co.jp summary of 総務省 家計調査 2025](https://www.giken.co.jp/column/household-survey-2025/)). Because elderly *households* keep growing through 2050 even as the 65+ *headcount* peaks in 2043, the food market is more resilient than the population total — but its growth is concentrated in prepared meals, home-delivered meals, care food and functional foods, not in the traditional at-home cooking basket.

| 40–49 | 348,607 | 99,922 | 28.7% |

Growth pockets (industry estimates, several pending direct verification):

- **中食/惣菜 (prepared meals bought at retail for home consumption).** 日本惣菜協会 estimates the 2024 惣菜 market at ¥11.29 trillion (+2.8% YoY), rising to ¥11.71 trillion in 2025. This is a broader definition than MAFF's 中食 statistic of roughly ¥7.6 trillion; the two must not be mixed ([日本惣菜協会](https://www.nsouzai-kyoukai.or.jp/news/pr20250519/); [MAFF](https://www.maff.go.jp/j/shokusan/gaishoku_shokubunka/attach/pdf/index-598.pdf)).
- **配食/在宅配食 (home-delivered meals).** 矢野経済 estimates the combined medical meals + home-delivered meal market at ¥2.41 trillion in FY2024 (+2.4%); the broader food home-delivery market across eight categories was ¥2.64 trillion (+1.7%) ([矢野経済](https://www.yano.co.jp/press-release/show/press_id/3857); [矢野経済](https://www.yano.co.jp/press/press.php/003977)).
- **介護食/嚥下調整食 (care food).** 富士経済 estimates the broad care-food market at just over ¥1,200億 in 2024; 矢野経済 splits FY2024 into 嚥下食 ¥368.6億, 咀嚼困難者食 ¥330.3億, and 介護予防食 ¥313億 (the totals differ in scope and basis; do not add) ([富士経済](https://www.fuji-keizai.co.jp/press/detail.html?cid=24050&la=ja); [矢野経済](https://www.yano.co.jp/press-release/show/press_id/4133)).
- **機能性表示食品 (foods with function claims).** ¥7,274億 in 2024 (+5.2%), with 脂肪（低減）the largest health-claim segment; the older FOSHU (特定保健用食品) segment has been shrinking since 2017 as companies shift to the cheaper framework ([富士経済](https://www.fuji-keizai.co.jp/press/detail.html?cid=25021&la=ja)).

Food waste rises with age: using NSFIE microdata, Shigetomi et al. (2024) show per-capita household food waste increases monotonically with the age of the household head — from 16.6 kg/cap·yr for households in their 20s to 44.4 kg for those in their 60s and 46.0 kg for those 70+ — with vegetables and fruit accounting for 57% of all food waste and "excessive preparation" the main driver for older households ([Shigetomi et al. 2024, *Nature Communications*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-51553-w)). Portion-controlled 中食, pre-cut vegetables and frozen/prepared formats address a real pain point.

**Projection (household-based, upper bound).** Applying the FIES 2025 per-household food spending of ¥82,611/month for 65+ non-working (2+ person) households, held flat in real terms, to the IPSS R6 elderly-household trajectory gives:

| Year | 65+ households (millions) | Per-household food (¥/month, 2025 real) | Food market (¥ trillion/yr, 2025 real) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 20.97 | 82,611 | ≈20.8 |
| 2030 | ~22.0 | 82,611 | ≈21.8 |
| 2040 | ~23.0 | 82,611 | ≈22.8 |
| 2050 | 24.04 | 82,611 | ≈23.8 |

*Sources: household counts [IPSS 2024](https://www.ipss.go.jp/pp-ajsetai/j/HPRJ2024/hprj2024_gaiyo_20240412.pdf); spending [総務省 家計調査 2025](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2025.pdf).*

This is an **upper bound**: the increase in elderly households is concentrated in single-person households (8.16 million in 2025 → 10.84 million in 2050, +33%), which spend far less per household than couple households, so applying the couple-household figure to all elderly households overstates the total. Real food spending per 2+ household fell 1.2% in 2025 and has been flat-to-down for years. The most likely path is a nominal market that grows through 2040, flattens around the 2043 population peak, and stagnates or declines slightly in real terms by 2050 — with the mix shifting decisively toward 中食, 配食, care food and functional foods ([FIES 2025](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2025.pdf)).

### 4.2 Clothing and footwear

Japan's elderly clothing and footwear market is large but structurally stagnant in real, per-household terms. For 2+ person households, monthly clothing and footwear spending was ¥10,063 in 2025, up 0.8% nominally but down 1.8% in real terms, with the real decline driven by 洋服 (Western clothing) and 和服 and partly offset by real increases in 下着類 (underwear) and 履物類 (footwear) ([総務省 FIES 2025](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2025.pdf)). The prior year was the mirror image: ¥9,985 in 2024, nominal +3.5%, real +1.1% ([FIES 2024](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2024.pdf)). The category hovers near ¥10,000/month per 2+ household and is volatile around flat in real terms.

Mizuho defines the 65+ household clothing/footwear market at ¥1.8 trillion in 2023 within a ¥55.7 trillion "living-industry" senior market, and projects ¥1.9 trillion real / ¥2.3 trillion nominal by 2040 (0.5% real CAGR) ([Mizuho 2025](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/pdf/msif_241.pdf)). By 2050, assuming the real market stays roughly flat after the 65+ population peaks in 2043, the category is ≈¥1.8–1.9 trillion. For context, the total domestic apparel retail market was ¥8.501 trillion in 2024, implying the 65+ clothing market is roughly one-fifth of all apparel retail ([矢野経済, lead](https://www.yano.co.jp/press-release/show/press_id/3961)). Clothing is almost entirely household out-of-pocket, so — unlike medical or long-term care — it has no meaningful public-transfer boundary.

The opportunity is in **mix, not volume**. The attributes that drive elderly purchases are functional: front-opening (前開き) garments, one-touch tape fasteners, stretch fabric, magnetic buttons, and reversible socks for reduced dexterity — the core features marketed by care-wear and universal-fashion specialists ([ケアファッションオンラインショップ](https://www.carefashion-online.com/shop/); [NPO法人ユニバーサルファッション](https://www.unifa.jp/)). Mail-order/通販 is the historical senior channel (Nissen, Felissimo), and specialist care-wear players sell primarily online/by catalog with cash-on-delivery and convenience-store payment to lower the trust barrier ([ニッセン](https://www.nissen.co.jp/); [フェリシモ](https://www.felissimo.co.jp/fashion); [G&B](https://www.f-kayama.com/c/kaigo)). No verified market-size figure exists for 介護着 (care wear), 着脱容易アパレル (easy-dressing apparel), or walking-support shoes.

### 4.3 Housing

Japan's elderly housing challenge is a mismatch between stock and need, not a simple unit shortage. About 83% of people aged 65+ live in owner-occupied homes ([内閣府 高齢社会白書 2025](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_2_4.html)), and elderly households are overwhelmingly owner-occupied — 81.6% in the 2023 Housing and Land Survey, far above the national average of 61.4% ([総務省](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/jyutaku/2023/pdf/kihon_gaiyou.pdf); [2020 Census](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kokusei/2020/kekka/pdf/outline_01.pdf)). The addressable market divides into four pools with different buyers and public-transfer treatment: (i) aging-in-place renovation; (ii) purpose-built elderly housing; (iii) utilities and smart-home/energy technologies; and (iv) disposition of vacant houses.

**Purpose-built elderly housing.** The four principal types are 有料老人ホーム (fee-based nursing homes), 軽費老人ホーム (low-cost care homes), シルバーハウジング (silver housing, publicly subsidised rental), and サービス付き高齢者向け住宅 (service-attached elderly housing, サ高住) — the last being ordinary rental housing with mandatory consultation and care referral, and the only one open to private-sector entry without a care licence. Yano Research measured the four-type market at 90.7万戸 in 2019 and forecast 97.0万戸 in 2020 (+6.9% YoY), on a supply-unit basis ([Yano / Nikkei, 2021](https://www.nikkei.com/article/DGXLRSP604645_V00C21A2000000/)). The 国土交通省 target is a 4% supply ratio to the elderly population; against the 2020 65+ population of ~36.0 million, 4% implies ~1.44 million units, so the 2020 supply of 0.97 million met only about two-thirds of the target — a gap of roughly 0.47 million units. サ高住 registered units reached 290,590戸 / 8,346棟 in February 2025 ([MLIT via ai-government-portal](https://ai-government-portal.com/%E3%82%B5%E3%83%BC%E3%83%93%E3%82%B9%E4%BB%98%E3%81%8D%E9%AB%98%E9%BD%A2%E8%80%85%E5%90%91%E3%81%91%E4%BD%8F%E5%AE%85%E3%81%AE%E6%95%B4%E5%A0%87%E4%BF%83%E9%80%B2/), verify). Independent analysis warns that after 2040, when the elderly population declines, occupancy rates could fall ([SMBC](https://www.smbc.co.jp/hojin/report/investigationlecture/resources/pdf/3_00_CRSDReport092.pdf), verify).

**Aging-in-place renovation.** Three renovation drives matter: バリアフリー改修 (barrier-free: handrails, step removal, doorway widening), 耐震改修 (seismic retrofitting), and 省エネ改修 (energy efficiency). The long-term care insurance 住宅改修費 benefit caps a job at ¥200,000, of which 90% (¥180,000) is insured and 10% (¥20,000) is the user co-pay ([cocofump](https://www.cocofump.co.jp/articles/kaigo/223/), verify). Secondary industry estimates put 介護リフォーム at ~¥480億 in FY2013 and ~¥500億 more recently ([reform-online.jp](https://www.reform-online.jp/news/specialty/8818.php), verify; [kaigor.com](https://recruit.kaigor.com/company/business/), verify). The total housing renovation market was ¥7.35 trillion in 2024 (-0.5% YoY), of which repair/remodel was ¥4.6 trillion ([Yano, 2025](https://www.yano.co.jp/press-release/show/press_id/3877), verify). The elderly-driven share is not separately published — a key gap. Over 60% of people in their 60s and 70s judge everyday barrier-free/universal design to be "not very advanced" or "hardly advanced" ([内閣府 高齢社会白書 2025](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_2_4.html)).

**Utilities and smart home.** Utilities (光熱・水道) are a larger monthly line than housing itself for 2+ households — ¥24,547 vs ¥18,678 in 2025 — and rose 6.2% nominally in 2025, faster than total consumption ([総務省 FIES 2025](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2025.pdf)). Smart-home adoption is best understood through HEMS (Home Energy Management System); general-population indicators include cumulative HEMS installations of ~160万戸 by FY2020 ([space-core](https://space-core.jp/media/7471/), verify). Elderly-specific smart-home adoption data are lacking.

**Vacant houses and asset release.** The 2023 Housing and Land Survey records 900万2千戸 vacant houses and a 13.8% vacancy rate, both record highs, out of a total housing stock of 6,504.7万戸 ([総務省 令和5年住宅・土地統計調査, via KJA DATA](https://kankeijinko.jp/data/vacant-house/)). The 385.6万 "other" units (使用目的のない空き家) are roughly 2.1 times the 1998 level ([内閣府 高齢社会白書 2025](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_2_4.html)). As the current elderly cohort decumulates, released housing could add supply, but much sits in low-demand rural areas and is of poor quality, so it is unlikely to convert into liquid consumption. The rental market's reluctance to accept elderly tenants constrains mobility: roughly 70% of landlords retain some reluctance ([内閣府 高齢社会白書 2025](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_2_4.html)). Reverse mortgages (リバースモーゲージ) and two-generation housing remain niche instruments with no verified market series.

**Projection (household-based, upper bound).** Using IPSS R6 elderly household counts and the 2025 per-household 住居 expenditure held constant in real terms:

| Year | 65+ households (millions) | Per-household 住居 (¥/month, 2025 real) | Elderly housing out-of-pocket (¥ trillion/yr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 20.97 | 18,678 | 4.70 |
| 2030 | ~22.0 | 18,678 | 4.93 |
| 2040 | ~23.0 | 18,678 | 5.16 |
| 2050 | 24.04 | 18,678 | 5.39 |

*Sources: [FIES 2025](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2025.pdf); [IPSS 2024](https://www.ipss.go.jp/pp-ajsetai/j/HPRJ2024/hprj2024_gaiyo_20240412.pdf).*

Three qualifications: the per-household figure is an all-age 2+ average, and because 83% of the elderly own outright, the estimate is likely an **upper bound** for out-of-pocket housing consumption; this is a real, constant-price figure; and the renovation and purpose-built supply components have no verified long-run series and are not projected here. The 65+ population peaks in 2043, so the housing-consumption market flattens in the 2040s, and the mix shifts from new-build toward renovation and care-integrated housing.

### 4.4 Transportation and mobility

Japan's elderly mobility market is shifting from car-centred self-driving toward shared, on-demand and accessible transport, and the spending mix is already moving with it. The FIES puts monthly 交通・通信 (transport and communication) consumption at ¥41,731 per 2+ household in 2024, down 2.6% nominal and 4.1% real year on year ([総務省 家計調査 2024](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2024.pdf)). The NSFIE gives a lower all-household figure of ¥35,878/month (14.3% of consumption), with the share falling to 10.3% for household heads aged 80+ — the lowest of any age class ([総務省 令和6年全国家計構造調査](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12002000/001662655.pdf)). Crucially, transport and communication is *not* an over-represented category for the elderly: elderly non-working households over-index on medical care (1.39×), utilities (1.14×), housing (1.11×) and furniture (1.11×), while it is working households under 65 that over-index on transport and communication (1.15×) ([総務省 高齢者の家計](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/topics/topi845.html)). The absolute yen figure is large because elderly households are numerous, not because each spends disproportionately on mobility.

License surrender is rising again. Voluntary license surrender (自主返納) collapsed during the pandemic and has rebounded: 2024 saw 427,914 total surrenders, of which 264,916 (about 62%) were aged 75+, with driving-history certificates issued to 316,005 ([警察庁](https://www.npa.go.jp/policies/application/license_renewal/pdf/rdhtransition_r06.pdf)). As the 65+ population peaks around 2043 and the 75+ share grows, the flow of older drivers exiting the road will accelerate, and each exit converts a car-dependent household into a mobility-service demand case. (The *rate* of surrender requires the age-specific license-holder denominator, which was not retrieved.)

Policy has institutionalised community-transport alternatives. The 国土交通省 toolkit includes public-transit promotion, shared taxis and community buses, taxi pooling, private paid transport (自家用有償運送), mutual-aid transport by NPOs, freight-passenger mixing, and coordination with the long-term-care insurance 移動支援サービス (mobility-support service) ([国土交通省 高齢者の移動手段の確保に関する検討会 中間とりまとめ](https://www.mlit.go.jp/sogoseisaku/transport/content/001728376.pdf)). Autonomous driving is the most plausible long-run substitute: Japan legalised Level 4 (fully automated operation in designated zones) in April 2023, and the Mobility Roadmap 2025 records autonomous-driving demonstrations at over 100 sites in FY2024, with year-round Level 4 operation at 5 sites as of March 2025 ([デジタル庁 モビリティ・ロードマップ2025](https://www.digital.go.jp/assets/contents/node/basic_page/field_ref_resources/ad6ef0ee-922f-48c3-8e3c-7ab0509fe9d6/7d1d77ea/20250613_meeting_promoting_plan_draft_05.pdf)). Reported adoption indicators — community buses in roughly 1,281 municipalities (2020), taxi-dispatch-app users of about 16.64 million and ride-share users of about 810,000 at end-2024, and the GO app exceeding 25 million downloads — are vendor/press figures and should be treated as directional ([国土交通省](https://www.mlit.go.jp/sogoseisaku/transport/htm/all.html); [ICT総研 via 日本経済新聞](https://www.nikkei.com/article/DGXZRSP672384_U4A600C2000000/); [GO press release](https://goinc.jp/news/pr/2024/10/22/1ontvtmzeyuovadbgdrrm4/)).

The core addressable segment is the 交通弱者/移動弱者 (transport-poor/mobility-impaired) — elderly people who cannot drive, cannot use public transit easily, or live where no transit exists. NIES (2018) projected about 1.09 million transport-poor elderly by 2040, equal to 58% of the elderly population in affected areas ([NIES](https://www.nies.go.jp/pr/publications/kankyogi/71/column5.html)); this is a methodologically narrow estimate based on population-distribution and car-availability assumptions, not a needs survey, and should be treated as a directional order of magnitude.

**Net assessment.** The nominal elderly mobility market grows through 2050 because the household base expands and service prices rise; the real vehicle-related component likely peaks before 2040 and flattens, while the real service component grows. The single most sensitive assumption is the per-household spending trend: if the 80+ share decline in transport spending continues, the aggregate could stagnate in real terms even with more elderly households.

### 4.5 Healthcare, nursing care, and wellness

Japan's elderly health and care market is best read as **two separately financed layers that are routinely conflated in "senior market" headlines**. The publicly funded layer — 65+ medical care and long-term-care insurance — was ¥40.7 trillion in FY2023 and is projected to reach ¥54.3 trillion by FY2040 ([Mizuho 2025](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/pdf/msif_241.pdf)). The private, insurance-external layer — preventive/wellness and non-insurance care services — was ¥24.9 trillion in 2020 and is projected to reach ¥39.1 trillion in 2030 and about ¥76.6 trillion in 2050 ([METI/BCG, 2023](https://www.meti.go.jp/meti_lib/report/2023FY/000299.pdf)). These two layers must not be added together without labelling, and neither equals household out-of-pocket consumption.

|---|---|---|---|
| 65+ medical care (public insurance + co-pay) | ¥29.0T (FY2023) | ¥38.7T (FY2040) | [Mizuho 2025](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/pdf/msif_241.pdf) |
| Nursing-care insurance benefits (介護給付費) | ¥11.7T (FY2023) | ¥15.6T (FY2040, Mizuho); ¥25.8T (FY2040, plan-basis) | [Mizuho](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/pdf/msif_241.pdf); [jmedj summary of 2018 estimate](https://www.jmedj.co.jp/blogs/product/product_9958) |
| **Publicly funded subtotal** | **¥40.7T (FY2023)** | **¥54.3T (FY2040)** | [Mizuho](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/pdf/msif_241.pdf) |
| Private insurance-external health/wellness | ¥18.5T (2020) | ¥30.9T (2030); ¥59.7T (2050) | [METI/BCG](https://www.meti.go.jp/meti_lib/report/2023FY/000299.pdf) |
| Private insurance-external care | ¥6.4T (2020) | ¥8.2T (2030); ¥16.9T (2050) | [METI/BCG](https://www.meti.go.jp/meti_lib/report/2023FY/000299.pdf) |
| **Private insurance-external subtotal** | **¥24.9T (2020)** | **¥39.1T (2030); ¥76.6T (2050)** | [METI/BCG](https://www.meti.go.jp/meti_lib/report/2023FY/000299.pdf) |

The long-term care insurance (介護保険) system covers everyone aged 65+ plus people aged 40–64 certified with one of 16 specified diseases; benefits require a 要介護/要支援 (care-need/support-need) certification and are provided as in-kind services with an income-graded co-pay. The private insurance-external care market was ¥6.4 trillion in 2020 against ¥11.7 trillion of insurance benefits — roughly a 35:65 private-to-public split. METI/BCG decompose the insurance-external care segment into daily-life/social-participation support (housekeeping, care taxis, meal delivery, care travel: ¥1.0T→¥3.3T to 2050), living-function and convalescence support (care housing, care food, self-pay rehabilitation: ¥4.5T→¥7.8T), and care-related equipment/robots (¥0.8T→¥5.6T, about a 7× increase) ([METI/BCG, 2023](https://www.meti.go.jp/meti_lib/report/2023FY/000299.pdf)).

**Out-of-pocket schedule and the October 2025 co-pay cliff.**

| Group | Medical co-pay |
|---|---|
| Up to age 69 | 30% |
| Ages 70–74 | 20% (principle) |
| Ages 75+ (general income) | 10% |
| Ages 75+ (above income threshold) | 20% (since 1 Oct 2022) |

*Source: [政府広報オンライン](https://www.gov-online.go.jp/article/202209/entry-10482.html).*

The temporary mitigation measure that capped the monthly outpatient increase at ¥3,000 expired on 30 September 2025, so from 1 October 2025 the affected ~3.1 million higher-income 75+ people face the full 20% outpatient co-pay, with an average annual burden increase of ¥9,000 and a median of ¥6,500 ([MHLW](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/seisakunitsuite/bunya/kenkou_iryou/iryouhoken/newpage_21060.html); [Diamond, Oct 2025](https://diamond.jp/articles/-/374744?page=3)). This is a step change in the 75+ medical baseline: it raises household out-of-pocket exposure for the affluent old-old but does not directly expand the private market. Because the affected group is concentrated in the 75–84 band, the step change shifts the category mix within the elderly medical market.

**Growth engines.** METI/BCG identify the fastest-growing insurance-external categories to 2050 as prevention (¥0.4T→¥7.8T), measurement/screening services and devices (¥1.0T→¥3.6T), health food/supplements (¥3.4T→¥8.7T), and health tourism (¥2.9T→¥12.9T) ([METI/BCG, 2023](https://www.meti.go.jp/meti_lib/report/2023FY/000299.pdf)). Care technology is a distinct, smaller layer: JETRO projected Japan's care/welfare robot market at ¥16.7 billion in 2015 rising to ¥404.3 billion by 2035 ([JETRO, 2014](https://www.jetro.go.jp/ext_images/jfile/report/07001741/07001741.pdf)). The broader 福祉用具 (welfare equipment) industry is reported at about ¥1.6 trillion for FY2023 (JASPA, +1.1% YoY); this figure could not be independently verified and should be treated as a lead.

**Dementia.** Dementia is the single largest demand driver inside the health/care complex, and it illustrates why a consumption-power estimate and a product/services market estimate measure different things. JRI estimates that home-living elderly with dementia or MCI (mild cognitive impairment) numbered ~10.0 million in 2022 (4.43 million dementia + 5.59 million MCI) and will exceed 12.0 million by 2050; their annual consumption expenditure was ¥14.7388 trillion in 2025, rising to ¥16.9845 trillion by 2050 ([JRI, 2025](https://www.jri.co.jp/page.jsp?id=110161)). Separately, JRI estimates the "dementia-friendly" product/services market (seven categories designed to remain usable after a diagnosis) at ¥98.70 billion in 2030, rising to ¥531.28 billion by 2050 ([JRI, 2025](https://www.jri.co.jp/page.jsp?id=110161)). The two JRI numbers differ by a factor of roughly 32 — definitional, not contradictory: the first is a population's entire consumption basket, the second a narrow accessible-design market.

**Care workforce.** MHLW's 9th LTCI Business Plan aggregates prefectural estimates of required care workers at about 2.40 million in FY2026 and 2.72 million in FY2040, against 2.15 million in FY2022 — an increase of 570,000 over 18 years ([MHLW, 12 July 2024](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/newpage_41379.html)). The risk is that unmet formal demand shifts to informal/family care, which would suppress measured private-market growth even as care need rises.

### 4.6 Communication, digital, leisure, and travel

Elderly household out-of-pocket spending on communication and digital services is the only major consumption category to have risen in real terms between 2019 and 2024. The 2024 NSFIE reports all-household 交通・通信 at ¥35,878/month, 14.3% of the consumption basket, up 5.7% nominal and 8.9% real from 2019 ([総務省 令和6年全国家計構造調査](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12002000/001662655.pdf)). The category declines with age: the 家計調査 shows 交通・通信 spending falling as the household head grows older ([第一生命 年金通信](https://nenkintsushin.dai-ichi-life.co.jp/download/2146)). Adoption is the demand-side driver: the 65+ internet-use rate rose from 53.9% in 2020 to 62.9% in 2025, with the 70–79 band rising from 59.6% to 71.9% and the 80+ band from 25.6% to 37.1% ([NICT](https://www.nict.go.jp/info-barrierfree/relate/statistics/elder_net.html)). E-commerce use has diffused from younger to older cohorts: the 2025 EC-utilization rate is roughly 70% for the 50s, 60% for the 60s, and 40% for the 70s, up from about 60%/under-40%/20–29% respectively in 2019 ([通販通信ECMO会「ECアナリストによる解説」第9回](https://www.tsuhannews.jp/news/detail/75698)). Cashless settlement has shifted decisively: credit card use in EC rose from 65.0% (2015) to 79.7% (2025), electronic money from 3.8% to 45.8%, while cash-on-delivery fell from 36.6% to 15.2% ([same](https://www.tsuhannews.jp/news/detail/75698)). A persistent gender gap remains: in 2025, 65+ internet use was 70.6% for men vs 57.0% for women ([NICT](https://www.nict.go.jp/info-barrierfree/relate/statistics/elder_net.html)).

Television still leads for the old-old. The 2025 情報通信メディアの利用時間調査 shows weekday internet use approaching real-time TV (183.9 vs 156.1 minutes), and 60- and 70-year-olds still rely on television most for local news and health information, while the internet dominates from the teens through the 50s ([総務省 報道資料](https://www.soumu.go.jp/menu_news/s-news/01iicp01_02000130.html)). LINE (92.9%) and YouTube (81.5%) lead service usage across all ages.

Leisure, travel and hobbies are a 60s market that fades by 80. Pack-travel spending is highest in 60s-headed households — 3.2× the under-30 level — with the 70+ group second-highest; gardening supplies peak in the 70+ group; sports-club fees peak in the 60s; and health supplements peak in the 70+ group ([総務省 家計調査「4．高齢者の家計」](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/topics/topi904.html)). 交際費 (social and gift expenditure) in non-working elderly households is 1.36× the 2+ household average, reflecting gifts to children's and grandchildren's households ([same](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/topics/topi904.html)). Industry context (not elderly-specific, and figures not independently verified): Japan's overall leisure-related market was reported at ¥75.2 trillion in 2024 ([レジャー白書2025, JPC](https://www.jpc-net.jp/research/detail/007771.html)), domestic travel consumption at ¥26.8 trillion in 2024 ([観光庁](https://www.mlit.go.jp/kankocho/news02_00031.html)), and a 2016 MLIT-commissioned study estimated that raising 70+ travel frequency to the 60s level would add about ¥520 billion to the travel market, or about ¥1.04 trillion if each additional traveller brought one companion ([観光庁](https://www.mlit.go.jp/kankocho/content/001483822.pdf)).

**Projection (household-based, assumptions not verified elderly-specific).**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Communication / digital | ≈6.5T | ≈6.9T | ≈7.2T | ≈7.5T | ¥26,000 |
| Leisure / travel / hobbies | ≈6.0T | ≈6.3T | ≈6.6T | ≈6.9T | ¥24,000 |

Two qualifications: the base case holds real per-household spending constant, so all growth comes from household formation; and leisure/travel per-household spending is likely to decline in real terms as the 85+ share rises (spending falls sharply after 75), so the leisure envelope is an **upper bound** rather than a central forecast. Device purchases are largely outside monthly consumption and would add a separate durables market not captured here.

### 4.7 Personal services, financial services, and end-of-life

These categories do not behave like the FIES consumption categories above. Beauty and pets are large but mature markets whose growth depends on premiumization and the rising senior share; the funeral market grows mechanically with death counts; 終活 (end-of-life planning) services are small but growing about 10% a year; and financial/end-of-life services remain tiny relative to the asset stock elderly households hold, because bequest motives and longevity risk suppress asset release.

The total Japanese cosmetics market was ¥2.58 trillion in FY2024 (+4.1% YoY), forecast at ¥2.65 trillion in FY2025, driven by premiumization, inbound demand and post-COVID recovery; skincare is the largest category (46.3%) ([Yano, Nov 2025](https://www.yano.co.jp/press-release/show/press_id/3922)). A senior-specific cosmetics segment is not separately tracked in official statistics; industry media estimate the 50+ "senior cosmetics" segment at roughly ¥300 billion in 2024, but the underlying survey could not be verified and this should be treated as directional ([商工メディア, Nov 2025](https://www.syogyo.jp/news/2025/11/post_042618), verify). The total pet-related market was ¥1.9108 trillion in FY2024 (+2.6%), forecast at ¥1.9257 trillion in FY2025; senior women are a core ownership segment, with 26.7% of women aged 50–83 owning any pet and monthly dog/cat spending averaging ¥17,985 ([Yano, Aug 2025](https://www.yano.co.jp/press-release/show/press_id/3906); [ハルメク・エイジマーケティング, 2025](https://www.halmek-holdings.co.jp/news/insights/2025/6uq856dm278/)).

Elderly households hold an estimated ¥1,671 trillion in financial and real-estate assets (2023), yet reverse-mortgage utilization is only ~0.2% of potential eligible households, and the 2023 inheritance tax base was ¥22.7 trillion ([DLRI, 2026](https://www.dlri.co.jp/report/ld/560897.html)). The binding constraint is willingness to release assets, not ability to pay: the "老後2,000万円問題" raised the share of people targeting ¥20 million+ in financial assets from 31.2% (2018) to 49.9% (2025), and elderly households run monthly deficits while still holding assets ([DLRI, 2026](https://www.dlri.co.jp/report/ld/560897.html)). No official or industry aggregate for "elderly financial services" exists; the market is best measured by asset stock and flow indicators rather than a single market-size figure.

End-of-life. The funeral business market was ¥1.83 trillion in 2024 (+8.2%), composed of funeral service fees (72.4%), return gifts (14.9%) and hospitality food (12.7%); it excludes temple fees, cremation and altars/graves. Yano forecasts ¥1.8633 trillion in 2025 and ¥1.8915 trillion in 2034, with growth driven by rising death counts and polarization between low-cost small funerals and high-value personalized funerals ([Yano, Aug 2025](https://www.yano.co.jp/press-release/show/press_id/3891)). The 終活 services market was ¥23.45 billion in FY2024 and is forecast at ¥25.73 billion in FY2025 (+9.7%), the fastest growth in this subsection ([Yano, May 2025](https://www.yano.co.jp/press-release/show/press_id/3807)). The METI 特定サービス産業動態統計調査, which historically tracked 葬儀業 sales, ended with the December 2024 survey and was merged into the 総務省 サービス産業動向調査 in January 2025, so official funeral-industry data are no longer updated on the same basis ([METI](https://www.meti.go.jp/statistics/tyo/tokusabido/index.html)). 相続 services lack a reliable market-size series; the ¥22.7 trillion inheritance asset base is the best proxy.

## 5. How consumption habits are changing

### 5.1 Solo living and the 身寄りのない高齢者 challenge

The number of 65+ single-person households is projected to rise from 8.16 million in 2025 to 10.84 million in 2050 ([全社協 / IPSS 2024](https://www.zenshakyo.org/oyakudachi/?p=3217)). Solo living reshapes the basket: single elderly households report markedly lower income and spending than couple/multi-person households, pushing demand toward smaller portions, convenience, ready-prepared meals, delivery and subscription services ([内閣府 高齢社会白書 2025](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_3_2.html)). The sharper edge is social isolation. In 2025, 76,941 people died alone at home (孤独死), of whom 58,919 (~80%) were 65+; the Cabinet Office estimates 22,222 "isolated deaths" (孤立死, found ≥8 days after death) — nearly 30% of lone deaths — with men 3.8× women ([nippon.com summary of 内閣府 and 警察庁, 2026](https://www.nippon.com/ja/articles/-/10455)). Isolation risk creates demand for 見守り (monitoring), 安否確認 (safety confirmation), 配食 (meal delivery), 住環境 (housing adaptation), 終活情報登録, 入退院支援, and 葬儀・手続き services. The national response is institutionalised: the September 2024 高齢社会対策大綱 incorporates support for 身寄りのない高齢者 (elderly without relatives), and model projects in nine municipalities indicate the service categories that will scale ([全社協, 2026](https://www.zenshakyo.org/oyakudachi/?p=3217)). JRI estimates that 7.9 million elderly people in 2024 had no one they could rely on for emergency financial assistance; by 2050, 4.48 million will have no relatives within the third degree of kinship (11% of the elderly), and 8.34 million will have neither children nor a spouse (21.4%) ([JRI リサーチ・フォーカス No.2024-021, 2024-07-23](https://www.jri.co.jp/page.jsp?id=108454)).

### 5.2 Health consciousness

Health consciousness is already a spending driver, not just an attitude. The Cabinet Office's 2022 survey shows a strong link between health practice and outcomes: respondents who reported taking care of their health were more likely to report good health, and those who started young were most likely to be healthy; self-assessed health declines with age, with over 30% of men and about 40% of women aged 80+ rating their health "not good" ([内閣府 高齢社会白書 2023, health special feature](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2023/html/zenbun/s1_3_1.html)). The 2017 iteration found 90.7% of elderly respondents practiced some health activity — nutritional balance (59.4%), regular health checks (53.3%), rest/sleep (53.0%), walking/sports (47.6%), and hobbies (42.9%) — with the 75+ group most likely to prioritize nutrition ([健康長寿ネット summary of 内閣府 2017 survey](https://www.tyojyu.or.jp/net/kenkou-tyoju/tyojyu-shakai/koreisha-kenkoishiki.html)). Social participation correlates with better health and 生きがい (85.6% felt 生きがい in 2017) ([same](https://www.tyojyu.or.jp/net/kenkou-tyoju/tyojyu-shakai/koreisha-kenkoishiki.html)). Spending data corroborate: sports-club usage peaks in the 60s, health-supplement spending peaks in the 70+, and packaged-travel spending peaks in the 60s ([総務省 高齢者の家計](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/topics/topi904.html)).

### 5.3 Continued employment and 生涯現役

65+ employment reached 9.12 million in 2023 (19th consecutive annual rise) and 9.30 million in 2024 (21st consecutive rise; 25.7% employment rate; 13.7% of all employees), both record highs ([総務省 労働力調査](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/topics/pdf/topi146_02.pdf)). Employment rates in 2024 were 53.6% (65–69), 35.1% (70–74) and 12.0% (75+); most elderly work is non-regular (76.9% of 65+ employees) and concentrated in wholesale/retail (1.33 million), medical/welfare (1.15 million), services (1.04 million) and agriculture (0.93 million) ([same](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/topics/pdf/topi146_02.pdf)). Continued employment affects consumption in three ways: it supports income (41.7% of pension-receiving elderly households rely on public pension as their sole income, so work income matters); it compresses time, favoring convenience, ready meals and services over time-intensive home cooking; and it sustains 生涯現役 (lifelong-active) attitudes — about 30% of working 60+ respondents say they want to work "as long as able," and roughly 80% want to work until age 70 or beyond ([内閣府 高齢社会白書 2025](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_2_1.html)). Only 31.9% of companies have the 70-year employment-securement measure in place, so the 65–69 boom is still largely absorbed by non-regular re-employment and community channels such as シルバー人材センター ([same](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/whitepaper/w-2025/html/zenbun/s1_2_1.html)).

### 5.4 Digital adoption and the channel shift

Elderly digital adoption has advanced faster than the "digital divide" stereotype implies, but it remains steeply age-graded.

| Age band | 2020 | 2023 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 65–69 | 78.2% | 87.7% | 88.9% |
| 70–79 | 59.6% | 67.0% | 71.9% |
| 80+ | 25.6% | 36.4% | 37.1% |
| **65+** | **53.9%** | **60.9%** | **62.9%** |
| All persons | 83.4% | 86.2% | 85.7% |

*Source: 総務省 通信利用動向調査, reproduced in [NICT 高齢者のインターネット利用率](https://www.nict.go.jp/info-barrierfree/relate/statistics/elder_net.html).*

The 65–69 band has effectively converged with the national average; the 80+ band has improved but remains below 40%. Smartphone access underpins this: household smartphone ownership reached 91.8% in 2025 and personal ownership 78.9% across all ages ([総務省 令和7年通信利用動向調査](https://www.soumu.go.jp/johotsusintokei/statistics/data/260529_1.pdf)). The モバイル社会研究所's January 2025 survey of 60–84-year-olds puts senior smartphone ownership at 89%, roughly four times its 2015 level; note this excludes the 85+ group ([モバイル社会研究所, 2025-03-24](https://www.moba-ken.jp/project/seniors/seniors20250324.html)).

The channel implication is bifurcation by age band. Among people aged 60+ (survey average age 73.1), only 36.0% use internet shopping even a few times a year and 43.5% never use it, while cashless payment remains minority behaviour — 34.8% never use credit cards and 54.1% never use barcode/QR payments ([内閣府「令和6年度 高齢者の経済生活に関する調査」第4章集計結果](https://www8.cao.go.jp/kourei/ishiki/r06/html/chapter4_1.html), 2024年10月, n=2,188). The 65–74 band is digitally capable, but the 75+ band still depends on physical stores, mobile sales and human assistance. That structural lag is why the "last mile" remains the defining channel problem: verified estimates put shopping-access difficulty (買い物難民) at roughly 6–8.7 million depending on definition, and the market response is mobile supermarkets, community transport, delivery and proxy services rather than pure e-commerce substitution ([MAFF](https://www.maff.go.jp/j/shokusan/eat/access_genjo.html); [消費者庁事例集](https://www.caa.go.jp/policies/policy/consumer_partnerships/consumer_oriented_management/businesses/assets/consumer_partner_cms204_231127_3.pdf)). Supermarket capacity is shrinking — the number of food-retailers fell to about 23,000, down roughly 20% over ten years — while mobile supermarket operator とくし丸 had 1,150 vehicles in 2023, about 80% of users aged 70+, and annual distribution value approaching ¥300億 ([消費者庁白書2023](https://www.caa.go.jp/policies/policy/consumer_research/white_paper/2023/white_paper_example_04.html); [消費者庁事例集](https://www.caa.go.jp/policies/policy/consumer_partnerships/consumer_oriented_management/businesses/assets/consumer_partner_cms204_231127_3.pdf)).

Service design friction points are concrete: among elderly online shoppers, the top concern is personal-information leakage (48.9% of 65–74; 40.0% of 75+), and one-click purchase mechanisms are felt to carry disadvantage by 53.5% of 65–74 users ([消費者庁白書2023](https://www.caa.go.jp/policies/policy/consumer_research/white_paper/2023/white_paper_141.html)). Senior conversion therefore depends on clear cancellation, no dark patterns, simple checkout and a human fallback. Cash acceptance and in-person payment remain baseline requirements for the average 60+ consumer.

### 5.5 From goods to experiences and services

Elderly spending already skews toward experience and services relative to younger households: the 65+ group over-indexes on medical (1.39×), utilities (1.16×) and 交際費/gifts (1.36×) ([総務省 高齢者の家計](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/topics/topi904.html)). The age-band split is the key: 65–74 spend on travel, hobbies and socializing; 75+ shift toward health management, gardening and supplements; 85+ concentrate on medical and care. The experience economy therefore grows with the 65–74 cohort through the 2030s, then hands off to health/wellness and care services as the 75+/85+ share rises toward 2050. Place-making policy reinforces this: 国土交通省's コンパクト・プラス・ネットワーク (compact-plus-network) and 立地適正化計画 explicitly aim to keep elderly residents within reach of medical, welfare and commercial functions via regional public transport ([国土交通省](https://www.mlit.go.jp/toshi/toshi_ccpn_000016.html)).

## 6. Integrated projection, scenarios, and sensitivity

### 6.1 Method

Two mutually exclusive formulas are used, never mixed within a category:

- **Formula A — per-capita:** market size = elderly headcount × per-capita expenditure. Used where the elderly person is the direct consumer: food, clothing, footwear, recreation, and many medical/wellness goods. Denominator is the IPSS 65+ (or age-band) population.
- **Formula B — per-household:** market size = elderly households × per-household expenditure × captured share. Used where the household is the purchasing unit — housing, utilities, communication — or where spending benefits non-elderly people (gifts, family travel). Denominator is the IPSS elderly-household projection.

The two are reconciled by the identity *elderly households × average 65+ persons per household ≈ 65+ population*. For market sizing, use elderly households × per-household expenditure for household-scaled categories (housing, utilities, communication, furniture, food-at-home) and elderly population × per-capita expenditure for individual-scaled categories (medical, clothing, personal care). Mixing the two without labelling misstates the market by tens of percent. Every category is reported as three separate components: (i) household out-of-pocket consumption, (ii) publicly funded share (tax and social insurance), and (iii) private co-pay/premium. Components (i) and (ii) are never summed without an explicit label.

### 6.2 Base-case trajectory

On a household out-of-pocket basis, Japan's 65+ consumption market is estimated at ≈¥57 trillion in 2020 (2025 constant prices), rising to ≈¥58.5 trillion by 2030 and ≈¥62 trillion by 2040, then flattening and edging down to ≈¥60–62 trillion by 2050. The real market peaks around 2040–2043, tracking the 65+ population peak. In nominal terms it keeps growing through 2050 as long as consumer prices rise. The cumulative 2020–2050 out-of-pocket opportunity is on the order of ¥1.7–1.8 quadrillion (2025 prices).

| Category (¥ trillion/yr, 2025 prices) | 2020 | 2030 | 2040 | 2050 | Share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 17.9 | 18.3 | 19.5 | 19.3 | 31.3% |
| Clothing and footwear | 1.4 | 1.4 | 1.5 | 1.5 | 2.4% |
| Housing (incl. utilities) | 9.1 | 9.4 | 10.0 | 9.9 | 16.0% |
| Transport | 5.8 | 5.9 | 6.3 | 6.2 | 10.1% |
| Communication/digital | 2.0 | 2.0 | 2.2 | 2.2 | 3.5% |
| Healthcare (out-of-pocket) | 3.8 | 3.9 | 4.1 | 4.1 | 6.6% |
| Leisure/other | 17.2 | 17.6 | 18.7 | 18.5 | 30.1% |
| **Total** | **57.1** | **58.5** | **62.2** | **61.6** | 100% |

*Method: 65+ population × ¥132,000/month × category share. Category shares from the FIES 2025 65+ non-working household and 60–69 age-class proportions. The 2050 total is sensitive to the population assumption; using 38.0 million instead of 38.88 million gives ≈¥60 trillion.*

The market grows +2.5% in real terms from 2020 to 2030, +6.3% from 2030 to 2040, then −1.0% from 2040 to 2050 — a clear deceleration and plateau as the 65+ count stagnates. Within the total, category growth diverges because the 65+ population is aging: healthcare and housing/care modification grow faster than the total (the 80+ count rises 39.7% from 2020 to 2050), while transport and leisure grow more slowly as the young-old cohort shrinks. Food is the largest and most stable category, anchored by the in-home and 中食 demand of solo-living elderly.

Sensitivity to the population variant: the 65+ count is most sensitive to mortality-improvement assumptions; the IPSS high/low mortality variants shift the 2050 65+ by roughly ±2 million, moving the 2050 market by about ±¥3.2 trillion. Combined with the per-capita range, the 2050 out-of-pocket market spans roughly ¥55–69 trillion, against a central estimate of ≈¥60 trillion.

### 6.3 Scenario analysis

Five behavioural scenarios bracket the spending side; two population overlays bracket the denominator.

| Scenario | Assumption change vs base | Main categories affected | Expected effect on 2050 market |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** | IPSS 2023 medium/medium; per-capita spending on base trajectory | — | Reference |
| **High digital adoption** | Online share, cashless, telehealth and delivery adoption rise faster | Communication/digital, leisure/travel, food delivery; physical retail loses share | Small net effect on total; large compositional shift |
| **Health-conscious longevity** | Higher healthspan; functional food, supplements, wellness, preventive care and barrier-free housing demand rise | Food, health/care, housing | Positive in affected categories; institutional care entry delayed |
| **Mobility decline** | Faster license surrender; car ownership, fuel and driving-related spending fall | Transportation; retail access | Negative for transport; smaller offsetting demand for on-demand and 買い物支援 services |
| **Low economic growth** | Pension benefits compressed, precautionary saving stays high, deflation | All discretionary categories; public-transfer share rises | Negative across the board; nominal market size falls |
| **Population overlay — mortality** | IPSS high- vs low-mortality variants | All categories, via the 65+ count | Direct multiplier on the elderly denominator |
| **Population overlay — fertility** | IPSS high- vs low-fertility variants | Total population and 65+ share; 65+ count almost unchanged | Negligible for the 65+ count; large for the total population and dependency ratio |

Because market size is roughly population × per-capita spending, the population and spending assumptions compound. The diagonal from top-left (fewer survivors + weaker spending) to bottom-right (more survivors + stronger spending) is the planning-relevant range; the spread between the corners is larger than the spread generated by any single assumption.

### 6.4 Sensitivity ranking

| Rank | Assumption | Range considered | Leverage on 2050 market size |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Per-capita spending / willingness to spend | Weak to strong real spending path | **Highest** — a direct multiplier; a 1% change moves the market about 1% |
| 2 | Mortality / longevity variant | IPSS high- vs low-mortality | **High** — direct multiplier on the 65+ count; brackets the 2050 count by several million |
| 3 | Pension benefit level and co-pay policy | Pension indexation; 30% co-pay expansion | **Medium-high** — shifts disposable income and the public/out-of-pocket boundary |
| 4 | Digital adoption rate | High vs low online/cashless/telehealth uptake | **Medium** — compositional; small total effect, large firm-level effect |
| 5 | Mobility and health behaviour | License-surrender speed; healthspan | **Medium** — category-specific, partly offsetting |
| 6 | Migration assumption | ~160,000 vs ~70,000 net inflow/year | **Low-medium** — modest for the 65+ count |
| 7 | Fertility variant | IPSS high vs low fertility | **Low for the 65+ count** — large for the total population and dependency ratio |

### 6.5 Reconciling the headline "¥100 trillion" estimates

Japan's "senior market" is routinely quoted at roughly ¥100 trillion, but that headline conflates incompatible lineages.

| Estimate (source, base year) | Scope and age threshold | Public/insurance-funded included? | Headline value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mizuho Bank Industry Research, FY2023 | 65+ medical + long-term care + "life industry" | **Yes** | ¥96.4T → ¥114.7T (FY2040, real) |
| Mizuho Corporate Bank, 2007→2025 | 65+ medical/pharma + care + living industries | **Yes** | ¥101.3T (2025) |
| METI (via 日本総合研究所), 2020→2050 | Insurance-external healthcare + life-support | **No** | Health ¥18.5T→¥59.7T; life support ¥6.4T→¥16.9T |
| NLI Research Institute, 2012 | 60+ household consumption only | **No** | ~¥100T (2012), growing ~¥1T/yr |
| WEF/World Data Lab, 2026→2036 | Global "silver economy" + 60+ spending | Mixed | Silver economy $4.5T; 60+ spending $19T→$34T |

*Sources: [Mizuho 2025](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/pdf/msif_241.pdf); [Mizuho 2012](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/sangyou/pdf/1039_03_03.pdf); [METI 2025](https://www.kanto.meti.go.jp/seisaku/iryokiki/healthcare/data/20250828_gyousei2.pdf); [Maeda 2013, House of Councillors survey](https://www.sangiin.go.jp/jpn/shiryo/chousa/rippou_chousa/backnumber/2013pdf/20131101141s.pdf); [WEF 2026](https://www.weforum.org/stories/economic-growth/ageing-consumers-reshaping-the-global-economy).*

The most widely cited "¥100 trillion senior market" traces to two different institutions with different definitions. The NLI Research Institute estimated in 2013 that total consumption by people aged 60+ had reached ¥100 trillion in 2012 and would grow by roughly ¥1 trillion per year — household consumption only, no public transfers ([Maeda 2013, House of Councillors survey](https://www.sangiin.go.jp/jpn/shiryo/chousa/rippou_chousa/backnumber/2013pdf/20131101141s.pdf)). The Mizuho lineage is broader: a 2012 Mizuho Corporate Bank report defined the elderly market as 65+ consumption plus social-security transfers across medical/pharmaceutical, long-term care and "living industries," projecting ¥101.3 trillion by 2025 ([Mizuho 2012](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/sangyou/pdf/1039_03_03.pdf)). The two series use different age thresholds (60+ vs 65+), different base years and different treatment of public spending; the fact that both round to ~¥100 trillion is coincidental, not a validated forecast.

Mizuho's current series put the FY2023 senior-related market at ¥96.4 trillion — medical ¥29.0T, long-term care ¥11.7T, life industry ¥55.7T — and project ¥114.7 trillion by FY2040 in real terms ([Mizuho 2025](https://www.mizuhobank.co.jp/corporate/industry/pdf/msif_241.pdf)). Medical + care (¥40.7T, ~42% of the total) is largely insurance-funded, not household out-of-pocket, and patient/care-user co-payments are counted both in household consumption and in the medical/care totals, so ~10% of medical/care spending is double-counted.

The bottom-up out-of-pocket aggregate in §6.2 is smaller than Mizuho's ¥96.4T because Mizuho includes ¥40.7T of largely insurance-funded medical/care; it is smaller than the NLI ~¥100T because NLI uses a 60+ threshold and an earlier base year; and it exceeds the METI ¥30.9T/¥59.7T healthcare-industry figures because METI excludes insurance-funded care. The reconciliation is definitional, not a data error.

## 7. Strategic implications

### 7.1 Two markets, not one

Japan's elderly market is not one market but two. A large young-old (65–74) segment — still growing, still spending, still digitally adopting — behaves like a premium consumer market, while a smaller but faster-growing old-old (75+/85+) segment behaves like a care, medical and service market. The total population will shrink by roughly a fifth by 2050, but the 65+ share will rise from 28.6% toward ~36–37%, and the absolute 65+ count will not peak until 2043. Companies should stop framing Japan as a "shrinking market" and start framing it as a **re-composition market**: the pool of elderly consumers keeps growing for two more decades, but its category mix shifts decisively toward health, care, housing and services. The public-payer boundary — where co-pay, insurance-funded care and 保険外 (non-insurance) services meet — is the single most important strategic frontier.

This dispersion means a single "elderly consumer" strategy will misfire: the premium segment and the low-income segment require fundamentally different value propositions and distribution. Four capabilities determine success: (1) product design — adaptive, easy-dressing, comfort, large-print, simplified UI; (2) distribution — drugstores, mobile stores, mail-order and proxy purchasing by family remain critical, and e-commerce must be paired with cash-on-delivery and human support; (3) digital — simple interfaces, cash acceptance and デジタル活用支援 to close the adoption gap; (4) service and trust — METI finds that companies struggle to reach 保険外 care services because of weak information, trust and price barriers ([METI, 2025-08-28](https://www.kanto.meti.go.jp/seisaku/iryokiki/healthcare/data/20250828_gyousei2.pdf)).

Regional strategy is two-track. In urban areas, the opportunity is high-density service delivery — home-based care, 中食 delivery and digital services — for single-elderly households. In rural areas, the priority is maintaining the physical and mobility infrastructure that allows aging in place: community buses, mobile stores and compact-city consolidation of medical, welfare and retail functions.

### 7.4 The public-payer boundary

The most attractive private-market opportunities sit exactly where public insurance ends. METI targets the non-insurance healthcare/care domestic market at ¥77T by 2050, up from ¥24.9T in 2020 — a ¥52 trillion expansion — spanning preventive services, daily-living support and care technology ([METI, 2025-08-28](https://www.kanto.meti.go.jp/seisaku/iryokiki/healthcare/data/20250828_gyousei2.pdf)). The medical co-pay schedule and the 30 September 2025 expiry of the ¥3,000 monthly mitigation cap raise out-of-pocket exposure from 1 October 2025, shifting additional demand toward private services and price-sensitive alternatives ([MHLW](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/seisakunitsuite/bunya/kenkou_iryou/iryouhoken/newpage_21060.html)). Care-technology (介護ロボット, 見守りセンサー, 福祉用具) and preventive health (特定健診, 保健指導) are the clearest adjacent markets, as is the 保険外 service layer for housing, meals and mobility.

## 8. Data limitations and update triggers

The 2050 elderly market estimate is a bounded range, not a point forecast. Demographic uncertainty is large but quantifiable; measurement uncertainty is structural and cannot be modelled away. Every 2040–2050 market number should carry a labelled range, its numerator/denominator logic and a confidence tag.

**The largest data gaps.**

1. **Single-person elderly households are under-measured.** FIES covers only general households, excludes institutional households and student single-person households, and applies a distribution correction to single-person results because sampling precision is insufficient ([総務省 家計調査 用語の解説](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/kaisetsu.html)). NSFIE covers all households but is conducted only every five years.
2. **Age-of-head vs age-of-members.** Both surveys classify households by the head's age. Spending by 65+ members living in younger-headed or three-generation households is attributed to a younger age class, and per-capita elderly demand is understated.
3. **Public/private boundary.** Medical and long-term-care spending is predominantly insurance-funded; only the co-pay schedule enters household out-of-pocket consumption. Any "elderly medical market" figure that mixes insurance disbursements with household spending is not comparable with FIES/NSFIE consumption totals.
4. **Category gaps.** Elderly housing, mobility services, digital services and end-of-life services have no official category totals; industry estimates fill them but use inconsistent age thresholds (50+/60+/65+) and include B2B or care-insurance spending.
5. **Variant-table extraction.** The exact 2050 65+ counts by IPSS variant are published in downloadable tables; this analysis verified the 2043 peak, the 2070 endpoints and the revision drift, but did not extract the exact 2050 value for every variant. Those values should be read from the IPSS tables before the final 2050 range is printed.
6. **Method reconciliation.** The category-level projections in §4 use household-based formulas (Formula B) that apply couple-household spending to all elderly households, making them upper bounds for food, housing and leisure; the integrated total in §6.2 uses per-capita category shares (Formula A). The two approaches are not additive, and the integrated total is the more conservative estimate.

**Handling the FIES vs NSFIE discrepancy.**

| Survey / cut | Population covered | 2024 monthly consumption (¥/household) |
|---|---|---|
| FIES, 2+ person households | 2+ person general households | 300,243 |
| NSFIE, all households (総世帯) | All households, incl. single-person | 251,242 |
| NSFIE, 2+ person households | 2+ person households | 301,797 |
| NSFIE, single-person households | Single-person households | 174,147 |

*Sources: [FIES 2024](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_gaikyo2024.pdf); [総務省 令和6年全国家計構造調査 要約](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/zenkokukakei/2024/pdf/youyaku1219.pdf); [厚生労働省 令和6年全国家計構造調査](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12002000/001662655.pdf).*

The frequently cited ¥300,243 vs ¥251,242 gap is therefore **not a data error**: the FIES figure covers 2+ person households, while the ¥251,242 figure covers all households including single-person households. The two surveys' 2+ person figures agree within 0.5% (¥300,243 vs ¥301,797), which is the real cross-check. The rule used in this report is: use FIES for 2+ household category detail and monthly dynamics; use NSFIE for single-person households, asset/saving stocks and five-year cross-checks; never sum a FIES 2+ figure and an NSFIE single-person figure into one "elderly consumption" total without labelling the scope.

**Uncertainty communication.** Present the IPSS medium-fertility/medium-mortality variant as the central case, bracket it with the high/low fertility and high/low mortality variants, and tag 2040–2050 estimates as "low confidence." A market-size point estimate without a range should not be quoted.
