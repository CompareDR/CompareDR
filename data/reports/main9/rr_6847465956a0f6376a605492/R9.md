# Technical Report: Experimental Plan to Test Whether a Vision–Language Model Can Replace Human Specialists for Skin-Lesion Image Labeling

**Date:** October 10, 2026  
**Research question:** *Can a modern vision–language model stand in for human specialists when labeling medical images of skin lesions?*

## Executive Summary

A modern dermatology vision–language model (VLM) should **not** be accepted as a replacement for human specialists on the basis of retrospective benchmark accuracy alone. The current evidence supports VLMs and dermatology AI as useful triage or decision-support tools, but it does not yet justify specialist replacement for skin-lesion labeling. The strongest experimental path is a **prospective, locked-model, multi-reader multi-case non-inferiority study** comparing the VLM with board-certified dermatologists on the same cases, using histopathology and expert adjudication as reference standards. The study must include **co-primary safety and labeling endpoints**, prespecified non-inferiority margins, Fitzpatrick skin-type subgroup testing, calibration and abstention evaluation, and post-deployment surveillance triggers.

My concrete position is that the VLM should be considered a possible *conditional labeler* only if it meets all of the following: non-inferior malignant-lesion sensitivity, non-inferior multiclass diagnostic labeling accuracy, no clinically meaningful performance degradation in Fitzpatrick V–VI skin, calibrated uncertainty with appropriate abstention, and reproducible performance under real-world image quality. If any of these fail, the VLM may assist specialists but should not stand in for them.

---

## 1. Background and Rationale

### 1.1 Why this question matters

Dermatology faces a practical access problem: AI skin-lesion systems are increasingly positioned as scalable triage and screening tools because of dermatologist shortages and the need for earlier melanoma detection. However, deployment depends on reliable performance in real-world settings, not just curated retrospective datasets ([Skinive, 2026](https://skinive.com/skinive-accuracy2026/)). The replacement question is therefore stricter than the assistance question. A model that improves workflow may still be unsuitable as a stand-alone labeler if it misses malignancies, performs worse on darker skin, or gives overconfident but unsupported labels.

Recent systems show promise. Dermalyser, a smartphone AI decision-support app using dermoscopic images in Swedish primary care, reported AUROC 0.960, maximum sensitivity 95.2%, and specificity 84.5% across 253 lesions, including 21 melanomas; for invasive melanoma, AUROC was 0.988 with 100% sensitivity and 92.6% specificity ([OncLive, 2026](https://www.onclive.com/view/ai-support-tool-demonstrates-detection-utility-for-melanoma-at-primary-care-practices/)). Yet these results concern a specific dermoscopy-based decision-support tool, not a general VLM replacing specialists for broad medical-image labeling.

The most relevant VLM evidence remains mixed. SkinGPT-4 is a dermatology-specific multimodal LLM aligning a vision transformer with Llama-2-13b-chat, trained on 52,929 skin-disease images with clinical concepts and doctors’ notes, and evaluated on 150 real-life cases with board-certified dermatologists. Its authors explicitly note a major equity limitation: the dataset is primarily Fitzpatrick I–IV, while Fitzpatrick V–VI data are scarce, potentially limiting diagnostic efficacy for darker skin tones ([Zhou et al., 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-50043-3)). This limitation directly affects any replacement claim.

### 1.2 Current evidence: promise, but not replacement-grade

In a realistic 1,117-case TDIV/TODIV-style benchmark including common, rare, and atypical tumors, experienced dermatologists outperformed AI systems on 9-class diagnosis. Physicians averaged 65.9% multiclass accuracy, expert dermatologists with more than 10 years of experience reached 74.2%, and the unimodal PanDerm model reached 72.2%; the multimodal version incorporating clinical photos and metadata reached only 66.3%. For benign-versus-malignant classification, the unimodal model achieved balanced accuracy of 0.82 and AUC of 0.91, outperforming physicians overall on that binary task, but expert dermatologists retained the highest sensitivity for malignancy detection ([LaGrego, 2026](https://ascopost.com/news/july-2026/expert-dermatologists-outperform-ai-foundation-models-in-real-world-skin-cancer-diagnosis/)). This is a critical distinction: binary triage performance does not prove specialist-equivalent multiclass labeling.

A psoriasis-image study similarly shows that top general multimodal LLMs can perform well in controlled settings but still fall short of dermatologists. ChatGPT-5 achieved 93.1% accuracy, 93.3% sensitivity, and 92.9% specificity for psoriasis identification, while three blinded dermatologists achieved 100% diagnostic accuracy against the onsite dermatologist reference standard ([Frontiers in Medicine, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1791488/full)). This supports an experimental premise: high model accuracy is plausible, but replacement requires demonstrating non-inferiority to specialists, not merely strong standalone performance.

## 2. Foundational Experimental Design

### 2.1 Primary objective

The primary objective is to determine whether a locked modern dermatology VLM is **non-inferior to board-certified dermatologists** for labeling skin-lesion medical images.

The target use case should be specified narrowly:

> **Intended use:** Stand-alone labeling of clinical and dermoscopic skin-lesion images into prespecified diagnostic categories, with optional abstention when image quality or diagnostic uncertainty is unsafe.

The experiment should not test an open-ended chatbot in an unconstrained way. It should test a locked model, fixed prompts, fixed label ontology, and prespecified abstention rules.

### 2.2 Study design

The recommended design is a **prospective, fully crossed, paired-case, multi-reader multi-case non-inferiority study**.

The University of Iowa Multi-Reader Sample Size Program is specifically designed for diagnostic reader-performance studies and supports factorial designs in which each reader reads all cases under each modality or test. It also supports nonequivalence and non-inferiority hypotheses, multiple designs, and multiple inference situations, making it directly relevant for planning this study ([University of Iowa, 2024](https://perception.lab.uiowa.edu/software/power-and-sample-size-estimation)).

Established MRMC methods, including Obuchowski–Rockette and Dorfman–Berbaum–Metz frameworks, are appropriate for analyzing diagnostic accuracy and ROC data with multiple readers and multiple cases ([Hillis et al., 2005; MRMCaov, 2026](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/MRMCaov/refman/MRMCaov.html)). If pilot variance estimates are unavailable, the CRAN `MRMCsamplesize` package can estimate sample sizes for fully crossed paired-reader, paired-case designs with AUC or sensitivity endpoints, assuming two modalities or interventions and recommending at least five readers ([Robert, 2026](https://cran.r-project.org/package=MRMCsamplesize)).

### 2.3 Proposed study arms

| Arm | Description | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| **Human specialist arm** | 8–12 board-certified dermatologists independently label all cases | Comparator representing specialist labeling |
| **VLM-alone arm** | Locked VLM labels the same cases using fixed prompt and label ontology | Candidate replacement |
| **Optional VLM-assisted arm** | Dermatologists label cases after seeing VLM output | Secondary analysis for assistive value |
| **Reference standard** | Histopathology where available; otherwise adjudicated expert consensus and follow-up | Ground truth for endpoint scoring |

A paired before/after design is common in AI reader studies. For example, DermaSensor used a web-based MRMC reader study in which primary care clinicians read lesion cases first without and then with device output; aided sensitivity increased, while specificity decreased but met a non-inferiority margin ([DermaSensor, 2026](https://www.dermasensor.com/clinical-evidence-research/)). However, because the present question is replacement rather than assistance, the VLM-alone arm must be the primary experimental arm.

## 3. Dataset and Case Sampling Plan

### 3.1 Case source and composition

A replacement-grade evaluation should use a large, prospectively collected or temporally held-out dataset from multiple centers. It should include:

- Clinical photographs.
- Dermoscopic images.
- Metadata available to clinicians, such as age, lesion location, sex, and relevant history.
- Variable real-world image quality.
- Common, rare, and atypical lesions.
- Strong representation across Fitzpatrick I–VI skin types.

A reasonable initial target is **1,200 cases** read by **8–12 dermatologists**, enriched to include approximately:

| Category | Minimum target |
|---|---:|
| Total lesions | 1,200 |
| Malignant or premalignant lesions | ≥300 |
| Melanomas | ≥100 |
| Rare or atypical tumors | ≥100 |
| Fitzpatrick V–VI cases | ≥250 |
| Poor-quality or borderline-quality images | ≥100 |
| Cases with histopathology | All malignant/premalignant and a representative benign subset |

This scale is justified by real-world benchmarks: the TDIV/TODIV study used 1,117 standardized cases with macroscopic images, dermoscopy, demographics, lesion history, and metadata, and included diagnostically challenging lesions under realistic image-quality conditions ([LaGrego, 2026](https://ascopost.com/news/july-2026/expert-dermatologists-outperform-ai-foundation-models-in-real-world-skin-cancer-diagnosis/)).

### 3.2 Reference standard

The strongest reference standard is histopathology. Prospective validation models such as DermaSensor’s blinded multicenter study strengthened evidence quality by enrolling lesions at 22 primary-care sites, biopsying all enrolled lesions, blinding patients and primary care physicians to device output, and using 2–5 dermatopathologists depending on pathology discordance ([DermaSensor, 2026](https://www.dermasensor.com/clinical-evidence-research/)).

For ethical reasons, not all benign lesions can always be biopsied. In those cases, an adjudicated reference standard should combine dermatologist consensus, clinical follow-up, and repeat imaging. Differential verification must be reported transparently, as real-world skin-cancer AI evaluations commonly face this limitation ([Thomas et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2023.1264846/full)).

## 4. Endpoints and Non-Inferiority Criteria

### 4.1 Co-primary endpoints

Because replacement errors can harm patients, the study should use co-primary endpoints:

1. **Malignant-lesion sensitivity**  
   - Binary malignant/premalignant vs benign classification.
   - Non-inferiority margin: **no more than 5 percentage points below specialist mean sensitivity**.
   - Additional absolute floor: lower 95% confidence bound should not fall below **95% sensitivity** for melanoma-relevant detection, consistent with the high-sensitivity orientation of melanoma tools such as Dermalyser, whose cutoff was selected to correspond to sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 78% ([OncLive, 2026](https://www.onclive.com/view/ai-support-tool-demonstrates-detection-utility-for-melanoma-at-primary-care-practices/)).

2. **Multiclass diagnostic labeling accuracy**  
   - Prespecified ontology, e.g., melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma/keratinocyte carcinoma, melanocytic nevus, seborrheic keratosis, actinic keratosis, dermatofibroma, vascular lesion, inflammatory/other.
   - Non-inferiority margin: **no more than 5 percentage points below specialist mean accuracy**.

The 5-point margin is intentionally stricter than workflow non-inferiority margins such as the 20-percentage-point margin used in an imaging-workflow study of autonomous total-body photography and dermoscopy booth acceptability, because replacing diagnostic labelers is clinically higher risk than replacing an image-acquisition workflow ([ExternalMed, 2026](https://www.externalmed.com/ai-and-tech-in-dermatology)).

### 4.2 Secondary endpoints

| Endpoint | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Specificity | Prevents excessive false positives and unnecessary biopsies |
| AUROC / AUC | Evaluates ranking and threshold behavior |
| Balanced accuracy | Useful under class imbalance |
| Top-3 accuracy | Relevant for differential diagnosis |
| Calibration error | Tests whether confidence is meaningful |
| Abstention appropriateness | Tests whether the model knows when not to label |
| Fitzpatrick subgroup performance | Tests equity and generalizability |
| Image-quality subgroup performance | Tests robustness |
| Reader time | Measures workflow value |
| Error severity | Distinguishes harmless label errors from missed malignancy |

## 5. Equity and Fairness Requirements

Skin-tone bias is central to the experiment. Datasets often overrepresent lighter Fitzpatrick types, and models can perform worse on darker skin, threatening diagnostic equity and global reliability ([Ahmed et al., 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-59316-x)). Fitzpatrick17k provides more than 16,000 clinical dermatology images with Fitzpatrick I–VI labels and is widely used for fairness analysis, but its metadata show that lighter skin types are substantially overrepresented ([Ahmed et al., 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-59316-x)).

The experiment should therefore require:

- Prespecified reporting for Fitzpatrick I–II, III–IV, and V–VI.
- Separate malignant sensitivity for Fitzpatrick V–VI.
- Equalized opportunity and equalized odds analysis.
- Predictive quality disparity across skin-tone groups.
- Interaction tests between model type and skin type.
- Minimum case counts per subgroup before replacement claims are allowed.

This requirement is not optional. SkinGPT-4’s authors explicitly identify Fitzpatrick V–VI scarcity as a limitation and recommend systematic collection and targeted training on darker skin-tone data ([Zhou et al., 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-50043-3)). A VLM that is non-inferior overall but inferior in Fitzpatrick V–VI skin should fail the replacement test.

## 6. Hallucination, Abstention, and Calibration

### 6.1 Why forced-choice labeling is unsafe

Medical VLMs can be systematically overconfident, and common fixes such as prompting or temperature scaling may not fully resolve the problem. Hallucination-aware calibration proposes using vision-grounded hallucination signals—such as comparing outputs from the original image and a weakly perturbed image—to attenuate confidence and improve calibration and ranking ([Byun et al., 2026](https://jiyoungbyun.github.io/vlm-hac/)).

The test should avoid only forced-choice questions. A 2026 CVPR workshop paper on NOTA-style evaluation emphasizes that when the correct answer is missing, forced-choice tests can hide unsafe guessing behavior in medical reasoning models ([Demirci et al., 2026](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/CVPR2026_workshops/Med-Reasoner)). Therefore, the label ontology should include:

- “Insufficient image quality.”
- “Cannot determine safely.”
- “Diagnosis outside supported label set.”
- “Refer to specialist/pathology.”

### 6.2 Abstention as a competence marker

Healthcare LLM abstention should be treated as a calibrated decision policy, not as failure. A recent decision-theoretic review argues that abstention is necessary when uncertainty, missing information, safety, or professional boundaries make answering unsafe; it distinguishes epistemic abstention from normative abstention and emphasizes that both under-refusal and over-refusal are harmful ([Nature Digital Medicine, 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-026-02882-1.pdf)).

The proposed study should therefore report:

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Coverage | Percentage of cases receiving a non-abstained label |
| Accuracy at coverage | Accuracy among non-abstained cases |
| Unsafe non-abstention rate | Malignant or OOD cases labeled confidently but incorrectly |
| Excessive abstention rate | Easy cases abstained despite high specialist agreement |
| Calibration curve | Reliability of confidence estimates |
| Expected calibration error | Quantifies confidence miscalibration |

### 6.3 Uncertainty-aware safety layer

Standard softmax skin-lesion classifiers can be dangerously overconfident, especially on poor-quality or out-of-distribution images. Evidential uncertainty quantification models address this by producing a probability and uncertainty score, with high-uncertainty cases flagged for human review ([Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2026.1859844/full)). The VLM replacement experiment should similarly require uncertainty-based referral. A model that must always answer should not be considered replacement-grade.

## 7. Statistical Analysis Plan

### 7.1 Primary hypothesis

Let \( P_{VLM} \) be VLM performance and \( P_{Derm} \) be the mean specialist-reader performance.

For each co-primary endpoint:

\[
H_0: P_{VLM} - P_{Derm} \leq -\Delta
\]

\[
H_A: P_{VLM} - P_{Derm} > -\Delta
\]

where \( \Delta = 0.05 \) for malignant sensitivity and multiclass accuracy.

### 7.2 MRMC modeling

The analysis should use MRMC methods with random cases and random readers where appropriate. The Obuchowski–Rockette and Dorfman–Berbaum–Metz frameworks are established for diagnostic reader studies with dependent observations ([MRMCaov, 2026](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/MRMCaov/refman/MRMCaov.html)).

The VLM should be treated as a fixed comparator and evaluated against the distribution of specialist readers. Sensitivity analyses should compare:

1. VLM vs average dermatologist.
2. VLM vs lower quartile dermatologist.
3. VLM vs expert dermatologists with more than 10 years of experience.
4. VLM vs consensus panel.

This is important because the TDIV/TODIV evidence suggests AI may outperform less experienced clinicians but remain inferior to expert dermatologists ([LaGrego, 2026](https://ascopost.com/news/july-2026/expert-dermatologists-outperform-ai-foundation-models-in-real-world-skin-cancer-diagnosis/)).

### 7.3 Sample-size planning

Sample size should be finalized after a pilot study of 100–150 cases to estimate reader, case, and reader-by-case variance. The Iowa Multi-Reader Sample Size Program can then provide reader/case combinations for specified power under non-inferiority hypotheses ([University of Iowa, 2024](https://perception.lab.uiowa.edu/software/power-and-sample-size-estimation)). If no pilot data exist, `MRMCsamplesize` can provide planning estimates for AUC or sensitivity endpoints in fully crossed paired-reader, paired-case studies ([Robert, 2026](https://cran.r-project.org/package=MRMCsamplesize)).

## 8. Operational Protocol

### 8.1 Model locking and prompting

Before testing:

- Freeze model weights.
- Freeze preprocessing.
- Freeze prompt templates.
- Freeze label ontology.
- Disable memory across cases.
- Record model version, API date, temperature, and decoding settings.
- Use deterministic decoding where possible.
- Store all model outputs and confidence scores.

### 8.2 Reader procedure

Readers should label cases independently in randomized order. To reduce recall bias, any aided-reader secondary phase should occur after a washout period. Reader interfaces should show the same information available to the VLM unless the study intentionally evaluates image-only or multimodal settings.

The PanDerm comparison is a cautionary example: adding clinical photos and metadata did not automatically improve performance; the multimodal model degraded multiclass accuracy versus the image-only model, likely reflecting distribution shift from more complex real-world imagery ([LaGrego, 2026](https://ascopost.com/news/july-2026/expert-dermatologists-outperform-ai-foundation-models-in-real-world-skin-cancer-diagnosis/)). Therefore, image-only and multimodal VLM performance should be analyzed separately.

### 8.3 Error adjudication

All discordant and high-risk errors should be adjudicated by a blinded panel. Errors should be ranked by clinical severity:

| Severity | Example |
|---|---|
| Critical | Melanoma labeled benign |
| Major | SCC labeled inflammatory |
| Moderate | BCC mislabeled as SCC |
| Minor | Benign nevus mislabeled seborrheic keratosis |
| Non-actionable | Correct benign/malignant triage but wrong subtype |

Replacement should fail automatically if critical false negatives exceed a prespecified safety threshold, even if aggregate accuracy is non-inferior.

---

## 9. Decision Rule

The VLM should be judged suitable to stand in for human specialists **only if all criteria below are met**:

1. Meets non-inferiority for malignant sensitivity.
2. Meets non-inferiority for multiclass accuracy.
3. Meets melanoma sensitivity absolute safety floor.
4. Shows no unacceptable degradation in Fitzpatrick V–VI cases.
5. Is calibrated, with acceptable expected calibration error.
6. Demonstrates appropriate abstention rather than unsafe guessing.
7. Maintains performance on poor-quality and out-of-distribution images.
8. Shows reproducibility across sites.
9. Passes adjudicated error-severity thresholds.
10. Has a post-deployment monitoring plan.

This is a high bar, but it matches the replacement claim. Current evidence suggests many systems could pass selected binary triage endpoints but would likely fail at least one of multiclass accuracy, subgroup fairness, hallucination/abstention, or expert-level sensitivity.

---

## 10. Post-Deployment Surveillance if the Model Passes

If the VLM passes the experimental test, deployment should still begin with human-in-the-loop monitoring. Real-world post-market surveillance of DERM in NHS pathways shows the importance of prospective monitoring, root-cause analysis for false negatives and near misses, subgroup tracking, and performance review before reducing human oversight ([Thomas et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2023.1264846/full)).

Post-deployment monitoring should include:

- Monthly sensitivity and specificity estimates.
- Fitzpatrick subgroup dashboards.
- False-negative root-cause review.
- Drift monitoring for image source and patient population.
- Random second-read auditing.
- Mandatory review of all abstentions and high-uncertainty outputs.
- Public reporting of aggregate performance.

---

## Conclusion

A modern VLM can be experimentally tested as a potential replacement for human skin-lesion labelers, but the correct test is not a simple benchmark comparison. It must be a locked-model, prospective, paired-case, MRMC non-inferiority study with specialist readers, rigorous reference standards, fairness analysis, calibration testing, abstention evaluation, and post-deployment surveillance.

Based on the current evidence, my judgment is that a VLM should **not yet be presumed capable of standing in for human specialists**. The most defensible near-term role is conditional triage or second-reader support. Replacement should be permitted only if the VLM demonstrates expert-level non-inferiority across malignant sensitivity, multiclass labeling, darker-skin performance, uncertainty handling, and real-world robustness.
