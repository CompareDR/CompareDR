# Experimental plan: deciding whether a vision-language model can stand in for human specialists in skin-lesion labeling

**Bottom line.** No modern vision-language model (VLM) currently carries the evidence to stand in for a human specialist labeling skin lesions. The question decomposes into three operational roles—autonomous substitute, second reader/adjunctive support, and triage/referral filter—each with a different comparator, dominant error, and evidence burden. The defensible first test is binary melanoma-vs-benign discrimination against a specialist-derived reference standard, framed as **non-inferiority in sensitivity**, with specificity, number needed to biopsy (NNB), calibration, and abstention as co-primary safety endpoints. This plan specifies the endpoints, reference standard, data, models, reader study, statistics, fairness and robustness checks, governance, and staged go/no-go gates.

## 1. Operational roles: what "stand in" must mean before it can be tested

| Role | VLM output | Human comparator | Dominant error | Evaluation design |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Autonomous substitute | Top-1 diagnosis and/or malignancy probability | Single specialist or consensus panel | False negative (missed melanoma) | Non-inferiority MRMC vs specialist-derived reference |
| Second reader / adjunctive support | Diagnosis plus rationale; flag for disagreement | Specialist, unassisted vs AI-assisted | Missed melanoma plus automation bias | Paired reader study with assisted and unassisted arms |
| Triage / referral filter | Refer / do-not-refer, with abstention on uncertain cases | Consensus panel and human inter-rater distribution | False negative at the referral threshold | Coverage–risk analysis; sensitivity at fixed specificity |

The autonomous substitute role is the only one that licenses "stand in" without qualification; it therefore carries the heaviest evidence burden and is the least defensible initial claim. The second-reader role is the most clinically tractable and has the strongest existing human–AI evidence: PanDerm, a dermatology foundation model pretrained on 2.1 million images from 11 institutions across four modalities, improved clinicians' skin cancer diagnostic accuracy by 11% and early-stage melanoma detection by 10.2% in reader studies ([PanDerm, *Nature Medicine*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03747-y)). The triage role is most forgiving of false positives but least forgiving of false negatives, because its purpose is to decide who gets a biopsy. A fourth mode—the VLM as arbitrator between disagreeing readers or as dataset annotator—should not be used to infer clinical substitutability, because agreement with an expert panel is not a clinical outcome.

The two error types are asymmetric. Localized melanoma has a five-year survival above 99%, compared with roughly 30% for metastatic disease, while a false positive consumes a biopsy slot in systems where NNB already exceeds nine ([Górecki et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12731220/)). The operating threshold, not the AUROC alone, is the clinically decisive quantity, which is why the primary framing is non-inferiority in sensitivity.

Comparator identity must be pre-specified. A single specialist is cheapest but vulnerable to individual bias; a consensus panel reduces variance; the human inter-rater distribution sets an upper bound on achievable model–human agreement and is the most defensible comparator for a substitutability claim. Stakeholder accuracy differs sharply: in a meta-analysis of 100 studies, dermatologists achieved melanoma sensitivity/specificity of 76.9%/89.1% on clinical examination versus 37.5%/84.6% for primary care physicians, with 13.3-fold higher odds of accurate diagnosis using dermoscopy ([Chen et al. 2024](https://www.cancertherapyadvisor.com/news/in-person-exam-with-dermoscopy-yields-more-accurate-skin-cancer-diagnosis/)). Acceptance thresholds must therefore be indexed to a named stakeholder and intended use, not a generic "specialist." Regulatory class follows intended use: software that analyzes a skin image and outputs a diagnosis is Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), whereas software that presents information for a clinician to weigh without offering a diagnosis can fall under the Clinical Decision Support (CDS) exemption ([Singh et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12264609/)).

## 2. Primary endpoint and decision rule

The primary endpoint is **binary melanoma-vs-benign sensitivity at a pre-specified operating threshold**. It maps most directly onto the referral decision, and a false negative is the dominant harm. Specificity, AUROC, AUPRC, balanced accuracy, macro-F1, Cohen's kappa, and calibration are secondary endpoints; multiclass differential quality is downstream of the binary decision.

| Endpoint | Metric | Proposed threshold | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | Melanoma-vs-benign sensitivity | Non-inferiority margin ≤5 pp vs specialist reference | [Salinas 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01103-x): AI vs expert dermatologist sensitivity 86.3% vs 84.2% |
| Secondary | Specificity | Non-inferiority margin ≤5 pp | [Salinas 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01103-x): specificity 78.4% vs 74.4% |
| Secondary | AUC-ROC | Equivalence margin 0.03–0.05 | Protocol proposal |
| Agreement | Cohen's kappa (VLM vs reference) | ≥0.61 and within human inter-rater range | [Viera & Garrett 2005](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~julia/courses/CS6998/Interrater_agreement.Kappa_statistic.pdf) |
| Calibration | Expected calibration error (ECE) | ≤0.05 | [Yu et al. 2026](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9783696/latest.pdf): ECE 0.058→0.036 after temperature scaling |
| Calibration | Brier score | Report; require improvement with temperature scaling | [Yu et al. 2026](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9783696/latest.pdf): 0.381→0.376 |
| Abstention | Coverage–risk | Abstention ≤20%; residual error risk below clinical threshold | [Yu et al. 2026](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9783696/latest.pdf) |

A non-inferiority conclusion requires the confidence interval of the performance difference not to cross the pre-specified margin; equivalence requires the interval to lie within the margin in both directions. The margin should be set by clinical consensus as the maximum acceptable loss. When the reference standard is noisy—a single reader without histopathology—the margin should be widened or reference uncertainty propagated; a consensus panel permits a tighter margin. Kim et al. identify three methodological pillars for defending a margin: the margin itself, the constancy assumption, and assay sensitivity ([Kim et al. 2022](https://www.jidonline.org/article/S0022-202X(22)00370-0/fulltext)); for a VLM the constancy assumption is fragile because the "comparator effect" is noisy specialist performance, so the comparator should be estimated concurrently in the same study.

Aggregate accuracy alone cannot support a triage claim. Temperature scaling improved calibration on HAM10000 without changing top-1 predictions (ECE 0.058→0.036; Brier 0.381→0.376), and selective referral reduced error risk among auto-classified cases; the authors characterize the model as a "calibrated triage-support candidate rather than an autonomous diagnostic system" ([Yu et al. 2026](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9783696/latest.pdf)). CalibPrompt improves calibration of medical VLMs during prompt tuning across four Med-VLMs and five datasets ([Basu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15226)). The protocol should require a coverage–risk curve, prespecified abstention criteria, and multiplicity control with Holm-Bonferroni across models, prompts, and subgroups.

Conformal prediction provides a distribution-free abstention guarantee but its guarantee is marginal, not conditional: it is unreliable under distributional shift, should not be used to select predictions in order to improve accuracy, is unreliable for subsets such as individual classes, and has limited value when the number of classes is small—the common case in medical imaging ([Mehrtens et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18162)). It should be treated as an abstention tool, with per-class and per-subgroup coverage reported. Over-abstention can be reduced by querying structured concepts before deferring; ReCoVERR let BLIP2, InstructBLIP, and LLaVA-1.5 answer up to 20% more questions without accuracy loss ([Srinivasan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15610)).

## 3. Reference standard and specialist disagreement

The reference standard is composite, not a single gold standard. Histopathology anchors biopsied lesions; where biopsy is absent, the standard should be a blinded, adjudicated expert consensus panel supported by clinical follow-up or in-vivo confocal microscopy. HAM10000 operationalizes this: more than 50% of its 10,015 images have pathology confirmation, with the remainder verified by follow-up, expert consensus, or confocal microscopy ([Tschandl et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10417)). The ISIC archive exposes confirmation type per lesion, so the proportion of histologically confirmed cases must be reported, and the primary analysis should stratify by confirmation type.

Specialist disagreement bounds what any VLM can achieve:

| Study | Raters | n lesions | Task | Agreement result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Haggenmüller et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11742048/) | 8 expert pathologists | 792 melanoma-suspicious lesions | Histopathology + clinical metadata | Complete agreement 53.5%; majority (≥5) 90.9%; non-invasive melanoma 10/73; panel disagrees with local pathologists 14.9% and dermatologists 33.5% |
| [Duncan et al. 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8440913/) | 5 dermatopathologists | 60 dysplastic nevi | Histopathologic grading | 77% concordance; Cohen's κ 0.55–0.84 |
| [Codella et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/1902.03368v2) | 3 expert annotators | 100 images (ISIC 2016) | Lesion segmentation | Inter-annotator Jaccard 0.743, 0.754, 0.861 (mean 0.786); thresholded-Jaccard threshold T = 0.65 |

The Haggenmüller panel's raw complete agreement of 53.5% means nearly half of cases lack full consensus; the same study cites prior inter-rater variability of 17.2–26.0% among dermatopathologists differentiating melanoma from nevus. Two figures—Duncan's κ range and Haggenmüller's reported Fleiss' κ range of 0.428–0.807—come from abstract records and should be re-checked at the primary sources before the protocol is locked.

The VLM's agreement should be judged against the human inter-rater distribution, not an absolute label: compute the distribution of pairwise human agreement and compare VLM–reference agreement to its 5th–95th percentile. If the VLM falls inside the human band, it is at the human ceiling; a superiority claim requires exceeding the upper bound. Use Cohen's κ for two raters, Fleiss' κ for a fixed panel, Krippendorff's α when ratings are missing or ordinal, and intraclass correlation for continuous ratings, and report agreement against several reference variants (single adjudicated label, majority vote, consensus), treating the spread as reference-standard uncertainty.

Four biases must be assessed with QUADAS-2: verification bias (only biopsied lesions receive histopathology), partial verification, incorporation bias (the reference incorporates information also available to the VLM), and work-up bias. Adjudication should use majority vote followed by consensus discussion or third-reader arbitration; Haggenmüller et al. recommend at least two pathologists or virtual panels for more consistent ground truth. Reference uncertainty should be propagated by bootstrapping over panel composition and reference-label variants, treating labels without histology as provisional, and pre-specifying the adjudication rule.

## 4. Data sampling frame and leakage control

A defensible experiment needs a multi-source sampling frame. HAM10000/ISIC supplies class coverage and metadata (age, sex, site, lesion ID); DDI, Fitzpatrick17k, and Milk10K supply skin-tone stratification; prospective cohorts (MIDAS, PROVe-AI) supply external validity. Sampling strata should include diagnosis class, modality (dermoscopic vs clinical macroscopic), skin tone (Fitzpatrick I–VI or Monk Scale), age, sex, anatomical site, and image quality, with patient-level grouping wherever identifiers exist.

| Dataset | Scale | Role in experiment |
|---|---|---|
| HAM10000 / ISIC 2018 | 10,015 images, 7 classes | Classification backbone; metadata; benchmark comparability |
| ISIC 2019 | 25,331 training images, 8 classes + OOD | Extended differential; rare-class/OOD testing |
| DDI | 656 biopsy-confirmed images, 570 patients, Fitzpatrick I–VI | Primary skin-tone fairness benchmark |
| Fitzpatrick17k | 16,577 images, 114 conditions | Broad skin-tone coverage; crowd labels |
| MIDAS / PROVe-AI | Prospective, paired dermoscopic + clinical, histopathologic confirmation | External holdout and silent-study cohort |

HAM10000, ISIC 2019, BCN20000, PH2, and Derm7pt lack reliable skin-tone labels, so subgroup fairness on the classification backbone requires supplementation with DDI or Fitzpatrick17k, or explicit label transfer. The stakes are concrete: on DDI, state-of-the-art CNNs' ROC-AUC fell substantially (reported relative drops of roughly 27–40% depending on model and version), all models performed worse on dark skin tones and uncommon diseases, and dermatologists also performed worse on dark skin tones; fine-tuning on DDI closed the light/dark gap ([Daneshjou et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08807)).

Class imbalance is severe: HAM10000 is nevus-dominated (widely cited as ≈67% nevi and ≈11% melanoma, though exact per-class counts were not re-verified at the primary descriptor). Stratified sampling, reweighting, and macro-averaged metrics should be planned accordingly. ISIC 2018 introduced balanced accuracy specifically to reduce prevalence influence, and that metric should be retained.

Leakage control is mandatory. Split at the patient and lesion level, not the image level, using the HAM10000 common lesion identifier; treat derivative datasets as non-independent (DermaBench reuses DDI's images; ISIC-DICM-17K is derived from ISIC); run near-duplicate detection across sources; and reserve a genuinely external prospective cohort for final validation. Cassidy et al. found duplicates within and between ISIC releases from 2016–2020, including across training and test sets; removing 14,310 duplicate images yielded a best-model AUC of 0.80 on the ISIC 2020 test set, presented as a more reliable benchmark than uncurated measures ([Cassidy et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34852988/)).

## 5. Label schema and elicitation formats

The clinically defensible primary target is the binary melanoma-vs-benign decision, supported by criterion-level algorithms; the HAM10000/ISIC multiclass schema is the dataset label schema used for training and benchmarking. Label granularity is not cosmetic—it determines achievable agreement, the metric set, and the error profile.

| Schema | Output | Primary metrics | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melanoma vs benign | Binary | Sensitivity, specificity, PPV/NPV, AUROC, AUPRC, NNB | Asymmetric error costs; misses non-melanoma skin cancer |
| HAM10000 / ISIC multiclass | 7 classes (MEL, NV, BCC, AK, BKL, DF, VASC; ISIC 2019 adds SCC) | Macro-F1, per-class F1, balanced accuracy, one-vs-rest AUROC | Single-label; class imbalance; AK merges AK/Bowen |
| 7-point checklist | Ordinal 0–10; ≥3 suggests melanoma | Sensitivity/specificity at threshold | Semiquantitative weighting |
| Malignancy probability | Continuous 0–1; threshold ≥0.5 | Brier, ECE, reliability diagram, AUROC | Threshold-dependent; needs calibration |

Top-k scoring matters as much as schema. On dermoscopic images, GPT-5's top-1 diagnosis was only modest, while its top-3 differential reached sensitivity >93%, specificity >86%, accuracy ≥92%, and F1 >91%; yet the same model retained a tendency to misclassify melanoma as benign ([Wang et al. 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/23/3052)). Those results rest on small samples (100 ISIC Archive and 500 HAM10000 images) without subgroup analysis, so the top-1/top-3 gap should be re-measured on the full prespecified test set. The protocol must still pair top-k output with an explicit malignancy decision, calibration, and abstention.

The 7-point checklist weights three major criteria (atypical pigment network, blue-white veil, atypical vascular pattern) at 2 points each and four minor criteria at 1 point each, with a score ≥3 suggesting melanoma and reported sensitivity 95%/specificity 75% in the origin study ([Argenziano et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9875194/)); the exact rubric should be confirmed against the primary source before preregistration. Continuous probability can be elicited on a 0–1 scale with an explicit threshold: in Winkler et al., dermatologists graded suspect melanocytic lesions by probability of malignancy on exactly this scale, and a market-approved CNN produced scores on the same scale, though only 125 of 228 lesions (54.8%) had histopathologic confirmation ([Winkler et al. 2023](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=37133847&retmode=text&rettype=abstract)).

Outputs must be schema-constrained, not assumed: mandate JSON-schema-constrained decoding or strict post-hoc parsing with validation, define the allowed class vocabulary exactly, and reject or flag out-of-schema outputs. An explicit "unknown/indeterminate" class should be defined; ambiguous or multi-label cases adjudicated by consensus; the frequency of "unknown" selections reported, and never silently collapsed into the majority class.

## 6. Candidate VLMs, prompting, and lightweight adaptation

Verified performance varies by roughly an order of magnitude across candidate VLMs, driven less by intrinsic model quality than by task formulation, prompting strategy, sample size, dataset, modality, and whether clinical history is supplied.

| Model | Access | Verified skin-lesion performance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-4V | Closed API | 85% accuracy vs 45% for LLaVA on 60 image–prompt pairs | [Cirone 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10973960/) |
| GPT-4o | Closed API | Accuracy 57.7%; sensitivity 96.8%, specificity 18.4% on 500 HAM10000 images | [Sattler 2025](https://derma.jmir.org/2025/1/e67551/PDF) |
| GPT-5 | Closed API | Top-3 differential: sensitivity >93%, specificity >86%, accuracy ≥92%, F1 >91% | [Wang 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/23/3052) |
| Claude Sonnet 4 | Closed API | 24.1% diagnostic accuracy on 87 clinical images (images only) | [Mijares 2026](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/2bb1/bd195c6afe5a4d5cd0034bc708234f822126.pdf) |
| LLaVA | Open | 45% accuracy on 60 image–prompt pairs | [Cirone 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10973960/) |
| PanDerm | Research | +11% clinician dermoscopy accuracy; +10.2% early melanoma; +16.5% nondermatologist differential across 128 conditions | [PanDerm 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03747-y) |
| SkinVL (MM-Skin) | Research/open | Outperforms general and medical VLMs across 8 datasets after LoRA tuning on ~10k image–text pairs and 27k+ VQA samples | [MM-Skin 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06152) |

Two design constraints follow. First, include at least one closed-API frontier model, one open generalist, and one specialist model to span the access–reproducibility trade-off; closed APIs raise data-governance concerns and version drift, while open models require GPU infrastructure. Second, specialist status alone is not a selection criterion: MedVLMBench found that lightly adapted generalist VLMs are generally competitive with specialists, frequently matching or exceeding adapted specialists in cross-domain settings, with specialist advantages strongest only when target modalities match pretraining scope ([MedVLMBench 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.17337v5)). PanDerm's reader studies were conducted by the model's developers—an independence caveat that the blinded MRMC design must address.

Version control is essential. For closed APIs, record the dated model string (for example `gpt-4o-2024-08-06`, not the alias), endpoint, and inference parameters (temperature, top_p, seed). For open models, archive weights, configuration, and a container hash. Store every raw output with prompt version, timestamp, and model ID, and delete chat context before each prompt to prevent cross-image contamination ([Cirone 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10973960/)).

Prompting and adaptation materially change reliability. Cirone 2024 showed that color-manipulation prompts induced systematic misclassification—darkened benign lesions were classified as melanoma—and that LLaVA's reasoning was disrupted by color changes. Jin et al. showed that GPT-4V achieved 81.6% multiple-choice accuracy on NEJM Image Challenges versus 77.8% for physicians, yet 35.5% of its correct answers carried flawed rationales, most prominently in image comprehension (27.2%); aggregate accuracy concealed systematic reasoning failures ([Jin 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08396)). This means abstention cannot rely solely on confidence in the final label.

Lightweight adaptation can close much of the gap. Knowledge distillation from GPT-4V-generated structured pseudo-reports into LLaVA-v1.5-13B outperformed Claude 3.7 Sonnet, the original LLaVA, and a retrieval-augmented variant ([Chakrabarti 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12443370/)); LoRA instruction tuning of SkinVL outperformed general and medical VLMs across eight datasets ([MM-Skin 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06152)); and a training-free concept pipeline that predicts ABCDE-type concepts before diagnosis outperformed traditional concept bottleneck models, though few-shot exemplar selection introduces leakage risk ([Patricio et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05609v1)). Remaining gaps: no fetched study systematically varies temperature, top_p, or seed for skin-lesion VLMs, tests self-consistency, or quantifies API version drift.

## 7. Reader-study design

A VLM cannot be validated against a single "human specialist" benchmark. The appropriate design is a **multi-reader multi-case (MRMC)** study, in which both readers and cases are treated as random effects, spanning the clinicians who would use or be replaced by the model.

| Reader type | Melanoma baseline (sens/spec) | Role in MRMC |
|---|---|---|
| Board-certified dermatologist | 76.9% / 89.1% ([Chen 2024](https://newsroom.uw.edu/blog/who-will-you-contact-about-that-worrisome-skin-lesion)) | Expert comparator; substitution target |
| Dermatopathologist | Not separately quantified | Reference-standard adjudicator (2–5 per case in DERM-SUCCESS) |
| Dermatology resident | Not separately quantified | Training-gradient arm |
| GP / primary care physician | 37.5% / 84.6% ([Chen 2024](https://newsroom.uw.edu/blog/who-will-you-contact-about-that-worrisome-skin-lesion)) | Target user for triage; DERM-SUCCESS comparator |
| Nondermatologist provider | Not separately quantified | Lower-bound comparator; generalizability stress test |
| AI-assisted specialist | — | Decision-support arm; within-reader aided-vs-unaided contrast |

Dermatology residents, dermatopathologists, and nondermatologist providers are not separately quantified in the reviewed studies; their inclusion captures the training gradient and adjudication perspective, but baseline values remain an evidence gap.

Three comparator options carry different implications. A single expert dermatologist is most permissive and appropriate only if the VLM is intended to reproduce a specific expert's read. A consensus panel is more conservative, because aggregated panel accuracy typically exceeds any single reader. The human inter-rater distribution is the most principled comparator for a substitution claim and is recommended as primary.

For the primary substitution comparison, a fully crossed design—every reader reads every case under unaided and VLM-assisted conditions—is preferred because the paired difference is the estimand of interest; a balanced incomplete-block design is acceptable for subgroups. DERM-SUCCESS provides the regulatory-grade template: 108 primary care physicians evaluated 100 skin lesion cases (50 malignant) in a two-phase within-reader design with a ≥2-hour washout, different randomized case order, and a prospectively locked algorithm referenced to 2–5 dermatopathologists per case ([DERM-SUCCESS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12126654/)). Its results illustrate the trade-off: diagnostic sensitivity rose from 71.1% to 81.7% (*P* = .0085) and referral sensitivity from 82.0% to 91.4% (*P* = .0027), while diagnostic specificity fell from 60.9% to 54.7% (*P* = .1896) and referral specificity from 44.2% to 32.4% (*P* = .0256). The sensitivity–specificity trade-off must be pre-specified as acceptable for the intended use.

Readers must be blinded to AI outputs during unaided reads and to each other throughout; AI assistance should be conditionally randomized so readers do not know whether a given case is aided until the aided phase begins, with re-randomized case order to mitigate anchoring. Automation bias is documented and persistent: erroneous LLM recommendations significantly degraded physicians' diagnostic performance even among AI-trained physicians ([Alizai et al., NEJM AI](https://www.ovid.com/journals/neai/fulltext/10.1056/aioa2501001~automation-bias-in-large-language-modelassisted-diagnostic)). The AI-assisted arm must therefore measure both correct reliance (accepting accurate AI output) and rejection of erroneous AI output, because the VLM's error profile, not just its mean accuracy, determines whether automation bias is net harmful. Secondary endpoints should include per-case reader confidence, reading time, and confidence calibration assessed separately for unaided and aided conditions; in DERM-SUCCESS, AUC in the low-confidence subgroup increased from 0.567 to 0.682 with assistance, aligning with a selective-referral deployment model.

## 8. Statistical analysis, power, and bias control

The primary framing is non-inferiority, not superiority. The margin must be prespecified and justified by clinical consensus, for example through a Delphi panel of dermatologists identifying the largest difference they would accept before withholding the VLM. Menzies et al. provide a prospective non-inferiority precedent in melanoma AI; indexed text reports a 10-percentage-point non-inferiority margin and a 0.5-percentage-point superiority threshold, though the full text was not accessible for independent verification and the specific margin should be confirmed before pre-registration ([Menzies et al. 2023](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(23)00130-9/fulltext)).

Power should target the lower one-sided 97.5% confidence bound of the VLM–specialist sensitivity difference, computed by simulation using MRMC variance components rather than a simple two-sample formula. As an illustrative calculation: with comparator sensitivity 84.2%, a 10-percentage-point margin, and expected VLM sensitivity 86.3% ([Salinas 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01103-x)), a naive single-reader binomial calculation suggests roughly 30–40 positive cases, but reader variability and case clustering inflate this substantially. The DERM-SUCCESS precedent used 100 cases and 108 readers, indicating that a VLM comparison should plan for at least 100–200 cases and 10–20 readers in a fully crossed design, with more if the margin is tightened or the expected difference is smaller. Target 80–90% power at one-sided α = 0.025 and report assumed effect sizes, margin, and variance components.

Pairing and clustering must be respected at every step. Use McNemar's test for paired binary accuracy, DeLong's test for paired ROC curves, and Wilcoxon signed-rank for ordinal outcomes such as reader confidence or malignancy probability. For MRMC variance estimation and hypothesis testing, use the Obuchowski-Rockette and Dorfman-Berbaum-Metz methods, with bootstrap confidence intervals resampling cases and readers and mixed-effects or cluster-robust standard errors to avoid anti-conservative p-values. Apply Holm-Bonferroni correction across candidate models, prompts, and subgroups, with gatekeeping for secondary endpoints. Prespecify an intention-to-diagnose population (counting abstained or indeterminate outputs according to the abstention policy) and an evaluable population, with missing-data rules registered before unblinding.

Risk of bias should be assessed with QUADAS-2 and its comparative extension QUADAS-C for the reader study, and PROBAST+AI for the VLM as a prediction model ([Moons et al. 2025](https://www.latitudes-network.org/tool/probastai/)). The benchmark literature underscores the stakes: 25 of 38 studies in a recent melanoma AI meta-analysis were at high risk of bias, primarily from patient selection ([Nadour 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12875-025-03073-9)).

## 9. Error analysis and failure modes

VLM labeling errors are not randomly distributed. Verified evidence concentrates them in three measurable failure modes—overconfidence on out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs, flawed visual rationale accompanying correct labels, and systematic degradation in darker skin tones and non-specialist settings—organized here with explicit stop rules. A critical limitation is that most quantitative evidence comes from general medical VQA or CNN-based dermatology AI, not VLMs labeling dermoscopic skin lesions; the VLM-specific error profile must be measured directly.

| Failure mode | Evidence | Detection method | Stop-rule trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overconfidence on OOD inputs | [Ju et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01020v1); [Byun et al. 2026](https://jiyoungbyun.github.io/vlm-hac/) | OOD detection AUROC; ECE; overconfidence gap | OOD AUROC below floor; ECE above threshold |
| Flawed rationale / hallucination | [Jin 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08396): 35.5% of correct answers had flawed rationales; 27.2% image comprehension | Expert rationale review; vision-grounded hallucination scores | Hallucination rate above threshold; any melanoma-related hallucination |
| Subgroup disparity (skin tone) | [Tjiu & Lu 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/1648-9144/61/12/2186): AUROC 0.82 dark vs 0.89 light (CNN-based) | Stratified AUROC/sensitivity by skin tone | Subgroup AUROC gap exceeds margin |
| Rare/atypical class error | [Frasier et al. 2025](https://www.jdermis.com/full-text/the-blind-spots-of-artificial-intelligence-in-skin-cancer-diagnosis) | Per-class confusion matrix; per-class F1 | Per-class sensitivity below floor for melanoma or any malignant class |
| Modality-specific artifact | [Ju et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01020v1) | Cross-modality evaluation; covariate-shift detection | Accuracy drop > margin on non-target modality |
| Missed melanoma (false negative) | Clinical consequence | Binary sensitivity; NNB | Sensitivity below non-inferiority margin |

Byun et al. evaluated eight open-source VLM configurations on three pooled medical VQA benchmarks and found overconfidence gaps of .192–.338 and ECE of .202–.340, with larger models achieving higher accuracy but uniformly high gaps regardless of scale ([Byun et al. 2026](https://jiyoungbyun.github.io/vlm-hac/)). Ju et al. found that advanced OOD detection methods tend to fail on covariate-shifted inputs and that medical VLMs are prone to overconfident predictions on OOD samples, which "can lead to severe outcomes such as misdiagnosis" ([Ju et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01020v1)). For skin imaging, covariate shifts—a dermoscopic model receiving a clinical macroscopic image, or a fair-skin model receiving a dark-skin image—are the most deployment-relevant failure mode.

Errors should be stratified by diagnosis class, skin tone, image quality (blur, illumination, artifacts), modality, and OOD status. Stop rules must be pre-registered: a missed melanoma exceeding the non-inferiority margin; a subgroup disparity exceeding a pre-specified limit; overconfidence on OOD inputs exceeding a calibration threshold; or a hallucination rate on visual rationale exceeding a threshold. Any of these halts autonomous labeling, requires human oversight, and triggers re-evaluation.

## 10. Subgroup fairness

Skin-tone annotation is methodologically fragile. The Fitzpatrick skin type (FST) scale was developed for sun reactivity, not image-based color assessment, and Fitzpatrick17k's creators noted the subjectivity of annotating Fitzpatrick type on diseased skin; its labels were produced by commercial annotation services, not dermatologists ([Fitzpatrick17k documentation](https://github.com/mattgroh/fitzpatrick17k)). The Monk Skin Tone scale is a more inclusive alternative for darker skin tones; Milk10K uses an ordinal 0–5 variable inspired by both frameworks. Ethnicity is frequently reported instead of skin type, but the two are not interchangeable ([Alipour 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343783/)).

Verified evidence on subgroup disparities:

| Study | Model type | Dataset | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Daneshjou 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2111.08006v1) | CNN | DDI: 656 images, pathology-confirmed | ROC-AUC drops 29–40% vs original results; ModelDerm 0.64 (FST I–II) vs 0.55 (V–VI); DeepDerm 0.61 vs 0.50; F1 0.20–0.37 |
| [Frederickson 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1816102/full) | VLM (GPT-5.2) | Milk10K: 460 lesions, histopath-confirmed | Broadly consistent accuracy, F1, and Cohen's κ across skin-tone groups; no systematic decline in darker tones; per-group values not released |
| Cirone 2024 | VLM (GPT-4V, LLaVA) | Not verified | Primary source inaccessible; skin-tone-specific breakdown unverified |

CNN-based results show a consistent pattern: higher ROC-AUC on lighter skin tones, with absolute gaps of 0.09–0.11 between FST I–II and V–VI. In contrast, GPT-5.2 on Milk10K showed broadly consistent performance across skin-tone groups, though overall performance was moderate and per-group numerical values were not released in the accessible abstract. The directly relevant VLM-specific skin-tone study could not be verified from its primary source and remains an evidence gap; no claim about VLM skin-tone parity should be made until it is closed.

Mitigation techniques such as resampling, cost-sensitive learning, over-sampling, and ensemble methods help balance datasets, but Daneshjou et al. found that GroupDRO, CORAL, and CDANN "cannot correct for these biases without diverse training data," making diverse data collection the primary solution rather than algorithmic correction alone. Fine-tuning on DDI closed the light/dark gap, though it must be validated prospectively. Observed disparities may reflect broader systemic inequities in data collection and healthcare access, not solely algorithmic bias ([Alipour 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343783/)).

When labels are absent, prioritize DDI and Milk10K as primary fairness benchmarks; stratify AUROC, sensitivity, and calibration by skin tone where labels exist; compute **equal opportunity difference** (the gap in true positive rate between groups) as a primary fairness metric, since missed melanoma in darker skin tones is the dominant harm; assess per-group calibration; and report proxy measures such as self-reported race/ethnicity only with explicit caveats.

## 11. External validation and robustness stress tests

Retrospective benchmark performance systematically overstates real-world generalizability. Three mechanisms drive the gap: dataset-level leakage, data-level distribution shift, and the near-absence of prospective validation. An executable external-validation plan must address all three.

A genuinely external test set must come from a different institution, device, or population. MIDAS is suitable: the first prospectively recruited, systematically paired dermoscopic-and-clinical dataset, with patient-level metadata (Fitzpatrick skin type, age, sex, site, lesion size), histopathologic diagnoses by three board-certified dermatopathologists, and a consensus conference for intermediate melanocytic tumors ([Stanford AIMI](https://aimi.stanford.edu/datasets/mra-midas-Multimodal-Image-Dataset-for-AI-based-Skin-Cancer)). The ISIC 2018 test set shares source datasets with its training set (HAM10000, BCN20000, MSK) and is therefore not truly external.

Domain-shift factors to quantify include population/skin tone, institution/device, prevalence/case mix, temporal drift, and device-specific bias. Evidence anchors: Daneshjou et al.'s DDI degradation; Young et al.'s finding that common transformations produced false predictions for 6.5–22% of lesions across test datasets (CNN-based; full text not retrieved, so re-verify before setting thresholds) ([Young et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-020-00380-6)); and Marchetti et al.'s prospective validation, which was limited to 96% White participants with Fitzpatrick I–IV only ([Marchetti et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-023-00872-1.pdf)).

The PROVE-AI study provides the prospective template: consecutive enrollment of 435 patients with 603 lesions (95 melanomas), all biopsied, with a predefined 95% sensitivity threshold. ADAE achieved sensitivity 96.8% and specificity 37.4% at that threshold; its AUC was 0.857 versus 0.780 for dermatologists (*P* = .007), and dermatologist AUC improved after exposure from 0.7798 to 0.8161 (*P* = .042). Limitations for generalization: 96% White, Fitzpatrick I–IV only, five dominant dermatologists enrolling 97% of lesions, single institution, and a biopsy-enriched population.

A **silent study** extends this design for the labeling use case: the VLM runs in parallel with clinical care without influencing decisions, and agreement with the eventual reference standard is measured retrospectively. This assesses real-world agreement and drift without the automation bias introduced by real-time exposure.

Robustness stress tests should cover:

| Test | Deployment risk addressed | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-modality transfer (clinical ↔ dermoscopic ↔ dermatopathology) | Model deployed on a modality outside its validation set | [PanDerm](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03747-y) unified 4-modality pretraining, 28 benchmarks; [MedVLMBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.17337) shows adapted generalists match specialists on unfamiliar modalities |
| Image corruption (blur, noise, contrast, rotation, compression) | Real-world images lower quality than curated benchmarks | [Young et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-020-00380-6): 6.5–22% flipped predictions (abstract; re-verify); corruption templates exist but have not been applied to skin-lesion VLMs |
| Rare-class lesions (DF, VASC, SCC, AK) | Missed rare malignancies | PanDerm evaluated rare conditions; per-class figures not retrieved |
| OOD detection / abstention | Overconfident wrong labels on unfamiliar lesions | [Ju et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01020v1); conformal prediction leads exist but direct skin-VLM evidence is thin |
| Saliency / attribution review | Spurious reliance on rulers, ink, lighting | Prospective blinded dermatologist review required; no skin-VLM-specific evidence retrieved |
| ABCDE hallucination | Misleading structured labels | [Jütte et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11484268/) methodological lead; direct hallucination rates not retrieved |

The evidence base for VLM-specific robustness in skin lesions is thin: most stress-testing predates VLMs, and the multimodal evaluations report aggregate rather than per-corruption figures. Until a skin-VLM-specific corruption and cross-modality battery is run, robustness should be treated as an open risk rather than a verified property.

Post-deployment monitoring should track input and output distributions, calibration drift (Brier score, ECE), and prediction frequencies. The FDA's Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) framework, finalized in August 2025, describes planned modifications, the methodology to develop and validate them, and an impact assessment; modifications within the PCCP bounds can proceed with pre-specified evidence, while those outside require new review ([FDA PCCP guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/marketing-submission-recommendations-predetermined-change-control-plan-artificial-intelligence)). For closed API models, any version update must trigger re-validation against the same test set before clinical use.

## 12. Governance: regulatory, ethics, data, and liability

A VLM that labels skin lesions is regulated by its intended use, not its architecture. Under FDA rules, software for one or more medical purposes that performs those purposes without being part of a hardware device is SaMD, while software that cannot operate separately from a device is Software in a Medical Device; software that does not acquire, process, or analyze a medical image, supports a clinician without offering a diagnosis, and enables independent review can fall under the CDS exemption—a contested, fact-specific boundary ([Singh 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12264609/)). In the EU, software intended for diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, or treatment is Medical Device Software under the MDR, with the "intended purpose" taken from the label, instructions for use, promotional material, and clinical evaluation ([MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_en_0.pdf)).

Because the report's safety constraint rules out fully autonomous diagnosis, the defensible intended use is adjunctive labeling or triage with human oversight. That choice fixes the premarket pathway, evidence burden, and liability split. Prohibited uses include autonomous diagnosis, operation outside the validated population/modality/lesion spectrum, substitution for histopathology where biopsy is indicated, and use without human oversight or uncertainty-triggered referral. Lifecycle governance rests on the FDA's Good Machine Learning Practice principles, a PCCP, and a Total Product Lifecycle approach, alongside ISO 13485 (quality management), ISO 14971 (risk management), IEC 62304 (software lifecycle), IEC 62366-1 (usability), and IEC 81001-5-1 (cybersecurity).

Data privacy and ethics require HIPAA/GDPR compliance: retrospective use of de-identified public datasets generally does not require individual consent, while institutional or prospective data require IRB approval and informed consent or a waiver; cloud/API inference may require de-identification before transmission and a business associate or data-processing agreement. Bias audits and subgroup performance checks are a governance requirement, and transparency/explainability documentation is expected. Liability is split: physicians and health systems face malpractice and negligence exposure, algorithm designers may face products liability but are unlikely to face negligence claims, and the reading clinician retains the duty to independently apply the standard of care regardless of AI output; the allocation remains unsettled ([Milbank 2021](https://www.milbank.org/quarterly/articles/artificial-intelligence-and-liability-in-medicine-balancing-safety-and-innovation/)). Evidence gaps: the UKCA conformity route, primary HIPAA/GDPR/IRB text, and generative-AI-specific FDA guidance were not verified in this material and should be confirmed with the institutional privacy officer and IRB before data access.

## 13. Reporting, pre-registration, and reproducibility

Reporting obligations map to distinct AI-specific guidelines:

| Guideline | Study component |
|---|---|
| STARD-AI | Retrospective diagnostic-accuracy benchmark and MRMC reader study |
| CLAIM (2024) | AI in medical imaging companion checklist |
| DECIDE-AI | Prospective/silent live clinical evaluation |
| CONSORT-AI / SPIRIT-AI | Subsequent randomized trial and protocol |
| TRIPOD+AI / TRIPOD-LLM | Prediction-model development/validation, including calibrated malignancy probability |
| QUADAS-2 / QUADAS-C | Risk of bias in diagnostic accuracy studies |

Pre-registration in a public registry (for example OSF or ClinicalTrials.gov) before data analysis prevents selective reporting. It should lock the primary endpoint and non-inferiority margin; the operating threshold and its clinical rationale; pinned model versions/snapshots and exact prompt templates; inference settings (temperature, top_p, seed); reader-panel composition and MRMC design; analysis populations and missing-data rules; pre-specified subgroup analyses with multiplicity correction; abstention and calibration targets; and stop rules. Changes after registration should be handled through a Transparent Changes document or a new registration.

The reproducibility package should release prompt templates and version identifiers; model names, API snapshots, and inference parameters; inference and analysis code; de-identified per-case VLM outputs and reader labels; dataset datasheets; and model cards. Each candidate VLM should ship a model card reporting intended use, evaluation procedures, and benchmarked performance across relevant demographic and phenotypic subgroups, including intersectional groups such as Fitzpatrick skin type × sex ([Mitchell et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03993)), with skin-tone-stratified and intersectional performance, calibration, abstention behavior, and known failure modes. Each dataset should be accompanied by a datasheet documenting motivation, composition, collection process, class distributions, skin-tone label availability, de-identification status, and known leakage risks ([Gebru et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010)). Conflict-of-interest disclosure applies across all study components.

## 14. Staged go/no-go pathway

No modern VLM currently meets the evidence bar for autonomous substitution. Pooled meta-analytic evidence shows AI algorithms achieving clinically comparable performance to expert dermatologists—AI sensitivity 86.3% versus 84.2% and specificity 78.4% versus 74.4% ([Salinas 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01103-x))—yet 25 of 38 studies in a recent meta-analysis were at high risk of bias, primarily patient selection, and no prospective non-inferiority trial with pre-specified margins has confirmed substitution-grade performance ([Nadour 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12875-025-03073-9)). The defensible path is a staged evidence pathway with explicit gates, where AI-assisted decision support is the fallback if autonomous substitution is not demonstrated.

| Stage | Design | Primary endpoint | Go/no-go gate | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Retrospective benchmark | Public datasets; locked model version; lesion-level splits | AUROC, sensitivity, specificity for melanoma vs benign | AUROC ≥0.90; sensitivity ≥85% | Dataset leakage, overfitting, class imbalance |
| 2. Blinded MRMC reader study | Readers in unaided and VLM-assisted arms; fully crossed or incomplete design; blinding | AUC or sensitivity; non-inferiority vs specialist or within human inter-rater distribution | Non-inferiority on primary endpoint with pre-specified margin | Automation bias, reader contamination |
| 3. Prospective/silent study | Real-world deployment; VLM labels collected but not acted upon; histopathologic or expert-panel reference | Real-world sensitivity and specificity vs specialist reference | Performance within pre-specified bounds; no safety signal | Spectrum bias, verification bias |
| 4. Clinical trial | Prospective non-inferiority trial vs specialist panel | Binary melanoma-vs-benign sensitivity/specificity with safety margin | Non-inferiority demonstrated with pre-specified margin | Generalizability, sample size |

Stop rules must be pre-registered before unblinding: fail non-inferiority on the primary endpoint; demonstrate unacceptable subgroup disparity; fail calibration or abstention thresholds; or fail robustness tests on external validation or stress tests. Any of these stops advancement or falls back to decision support only.

If the VLM fails autonomous substitution, the fallback is an MRMC study comparing VLM-assisted with unassisted specialists, following the DERM-SUCCESS template. If the VLM fails in a subgroup—for example darker skin tones or rare lesion classes—the indication should be restricted to subgroups where non-inferiority was demonstrated, or the VLM should remain a triage tool rather than a diagnostic aid for that subgroup. Post-market monitoring under a PCCP is a condition of use, not an afterthought; the NHS DERM pilot reflects this staged approach in practice, deploying AI as a screening layer with sensitivity exceeding 90% and safety monitoring tracking false negatives rather than as a replacement.

The evidence package should include a pre-registered protocol and statistical analysis plan; an MRMC reader-study report with Obuchowski-Rockette or Dorfman-Berbaum-Metz analysis; a regulatory brief classifying the VLM as SaMD with its intended use and indications; a decision memo with go/no-go rationale, stop-rule outcomes, and subgroup findings; and a model card documenting version, training data, performance, limitations, and intended use. Governance should include an independent adjudication committee for reference-standard construction, a data safety monitoring board for interim safety signals particularly false-negative rates, and conflict-of-interest disclosure for all investigators and readers. The main threats to validity—spectrum bias, verification bias, automation bias, domain shift, API version drift, and publication bias—must be disclosed in the decision memo and regulatory brief.

Remaining evidence gaps that materially affect interpretation: the Menzies 2023 non-inferiority margin could not be verified from accessible content; the prospective meta-analysis figures cited via a secondary summary require primary-source verification; the Cirone 2024 skin-tone breakdown is inaccessible; no VLM-specific dermatology confusion matrices or image-quality–error correlations exist; robustness stress-testing largely predates VLMs; and CALM-VLM and MedUncertainVLM full texts were inaccessible, so their figures should not be used as design targets until verified.
