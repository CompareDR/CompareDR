# Experimental Plan for Evaluating Vision-Language Models as Substitutes for Human Specialists in Labeling Skin Lesion Medical Images

**Abstract**

Modern vision-language models (VLMs) exhibit a paradoxical profile in dermatological image labeling: GPT-4o reaches 68.1% accuracy on the NEJM Image Challenge versus a human mean of 49.6%, yet GPT-4V identifies primary skin lesion diagnoses from images alone only 53.7% of the time — far below its 89% accuracy when textual clinical context is provided. Meanwhile, PanDerm, a multimodal foundation model trained on 2.1 million skin images, surpasses dermatologists by 10.2% in early melanoma monitoring. The most surprising finding is that VLMs currently function more as clinical reasoning engines than as visual interpreters — their "seeing" of skin lesions lags behind their "reasoning" about them. This report proposes a prospective, multi-reader, STARD-AI-compliant experimental design with predefined non-inferiority thresholds, stratified fairness audits across Fitzpatrick skin types, and a decision framework grounded in Cohen's kappa agreement, sensitivity-specificity parity, and regulatory SaMD (Software as a Medical Device) compliance to determine whether VLMs can responsibly substitute for human specialists in skin lesion labeling.

## Background and Basic Concepts

Skin cancer is among the most common malignancies worldwide, and early detection significantly improves prognosis. Dermatoscopy (also called dermoscopy) — a non-invasive imaging technique using a magnifying lens and polarized light — improves diagnostic accuracy for pigmented skin lesions compared to unaided visual examination. The standard clinical workflow requires a trained dermatologist to visually inspect and classify lesions, typically using the ABCDE rule (Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Color variegation, Diameter >= 6 mm, Evolution) for melanoma screening.

A vision-language model (VLM) is an artificial intelligence system that processes both visual (image) and textual (language) inputs to generate diagnoses, descriptions, or classifications. Unlike traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs) — which excel at binary classification tasks (e.g., melanoma vs. nevus) but cannot provide textual reasoning — VLMs can generate natural-language diagnoses, explain their reasoning, and engage in multi-turn clinical dialogue. This capability makes them theoretically attractive as automated labeling tools for medical image annotation, a task traditionally performed by board-certified dermatologists.

The central question is not whether AI can diagnose skin lesions — CNNs have matched dermatologist-level performance on binary melanoma classification since Esteva et al. (2017) published in Nature. The question is whether the newer class of VLMs, which combine visual understanding with language reasoning, can serve as direct substitutes for human specialists in the broader task of multi-class skin lesion labeling. This matters because expert annotation is a bottleneck: dermatologist time is expensive and scarce, and inter-rater agreement among specialists is imperfect (Cohen's kappa ranging from 0.57 to 0.93 for malignancy classification). If VLMs could reliably replicate specialist-level labels, they could accelerate dataset creation, enable large-scale screening programs, and reduce annotation costs — but only if their performance meets rigorous, prospectively defined standards of agreement, safety, and fairness.

## Vision-Language Models for Medical Image Labeling

### The VLM Landscape: From General-Purpose to Dermatology-Specific

Current VLMs applicable to dermatological image labeling form a tiered landscape. At the top are proprietary frontier models: GPT-4o (OpenAI, estimated ~1.8 trillion parameters), which achieved 68.1% accuracy on the NEJM Image Challenge compared to a human mean of 49.6% [1]; and Med-Gemini (Google DeepMind), which leverages Gemini's multimodal capabilities for medical reasoning. These models are general-purpose but can be instructed to perform medical tasks through prompting.

The second tier comprises open-source, medical-specific VLMs. LLaVA-Med (Microsoft, NeurIPS 2023 Spotlight) combines a Vision Transformer (ViT) visual encoder with a Vicuna language model, trained on PMC-15M — a dataset of 15 million biomedical image-text pairs from PubMed Central. It was trained in under 15 hours on 8 x A100 GPUs using a curriculum learning strategy that first teaches biomedical concept alignment, then task-specific instruction tuning [2](https://baike.baidu.com/item/LLaVA-Med/68803828). MedGemma (Google, 2025), based on the Gemma 3 architecture with 4B and 27B parameter variants, extends this paradigm with open-weight availability for medical research.

Dermatology-specific models represent the third tier. SkinGPT-4 combines a ViT with Llama-2-13b-chat, fine-tuned on 52,929 skin lesion images; it achieved 80.63% diagnostic correctness/relevance and 93.73% consistency in evaluations published in Nature Communications (2024) [3]. SkinVL, trained on the MM-Skin dataset (~10,000 image-text pairs and 27,000+ visual question answering items), outperformed both general-purpose and medical VLMs across eight dermatology datasets [3]. Most recently, PanDerm (Nature Medicine, 2025) — a multimodal dermatology foundation model pretrained via self-supervised learning on 2.1 million skin disease images from 11 clinical institutions across 4 imaging modalities — achieved state-of-the-art performance across 28 diverse benchmarks and surpassed clinicians by 10.2% in early-stage melanoma detection [4](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/2001754174753366913).

### Prompting Strategies and Training Paradigms

VLM performance on medical tasks is heavily influenced by prompting strategy. Zero-shot prompting (providing instructions with no examples) tests inherent capability but typically yields lower performance. Few-shot or in-context learning — providing several labeled examples in the prompt — can significantly improve accuracy: a Nature Communications (2024) study demonstrated that GPT-4V with in-context learning matched or surpassed task-specific neural networks in cancer pathology image classification with minimal examples [5](https://blog.csdn.net/m0_59164520/article/details/145399905). Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning prompts the model to verbalize diagnostic steps, potentially improving both accuracy and interpretability.

A critical development is the shift from supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms. The Med-R1 framework uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) instead of SFT to encourage diverse reasoning paths, improving cross-domain generalization and interpretability — directly addressing the "black box" problem of SFT-based medical VLMs that may achieve high accuracy without generalizable or clinically meaningful reasoning [6](https://blog.csdn.net/Python_cocola/article/details/146590017). This distinction matters for a labeling substitution experiment: a VLM that arrives at correct labels through generalizable visual reasoning is more trustworthy than one that exploits spurious correlations.

### Key Performance Data and Limitations

A striking finding comes from a 2024 medRxiv preprint evaluating GPT-4V's dermatology capabilities. In image-only mode, GPT-4V correctly identified the primary diagnosis for only 29 of 54 images (53.7%). With text scenario information alone or combined with images, accuracy reached 89%. The authors noted a "notable bias towards textual data over visual data" — the model relies more on clinical text than on the image itself [7](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.01.24.24301743). This has a profound implication for labeling substitution: if a VLM's visual understanding of skin lesions is only moderate, its labeling performance on image-alone tasks may be insufficient.

Conversely, specialized models show stronger visual capabilities. PanDerm improved specialist diagnostic accuracy by 11% and non-specialist accuracy by 16.5% across 128 skin conditions, and achieved state-of-the-art performance using only 10% of labeled data in transfer learning settings [4](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/2001754174753366913). The bifurcation between massive foundation models (PanDerm, 2.1M images) and general-purpose VLMs adapted via prompting (GPT-4V) represents a fundamental design choice for any experimental plan: whether to test the best available general-purpose VLM or the best available dermatology-specific model.

## Human Specialist Labeling: Standards and Baselines

### Ground Truth Construction in Dermatology Datasets

The HAM10000 dataset (Scientific Data, 2018) — the most widely used benchmark for skin lesion classification — contains 10,015 dermoscopic images across 7 diagnostic categories covering more than 95% of pigmented lesions encountered in clinical practice. Ground truth was established through a four-tier hierarchy: (1) histopathology (>50% of lesions, confirmed by specialized dermatopathologists); (2) follow-up (nevi showing no change over 1.5 years or 3 follow-up visits, accepted as evidence of biologic benignity); (3) expert consensus (two experts independently giving the same unequivocal benign diagnosis); and (4) confirmation by in-vivo reflectance confocal microscopy [8](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018161). This multi-tier approach reveals a fundamental tension: even the "gold standard" is not uniformly gold — pathology confirmation is available for only half of cases, and the remainder rely on varying levels of certainty.

The ISIC (International Skin Imaging Collaboration) archive provides the standard repository, but ISIC datasets are biased toward melanocytic lesions (12,893 of 13,786 images are nevi or melanomas in the 2018 archive) [8](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018161). The ISIC-DX consensus terminology project (JEADV, 2025) — a Delphi study — sought to standardize lesion diagnostic labeling across studies using hierarchical mapping [9](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/221393), addressing the fragmentation that makes cross-study comparison difficult.

### Inter-Rater Variability Among Specialists

Human specialist labeling is far from perfectly reproducible. The IMA++ dataset (MICCAI 2025) — the largest multi-annotator skin lesion segmentation dataset — demonstrated a statistically significant association (p < 0.001) between inter-annotator agreement (IAA, measured by Dice coefficient) and lesion malignancy: malignant lesions with ambiguous boundaries (e.g., spiculated or infiltrative nodules, irregular borders per the ABCD rule) are prone to greater annotator disagreement [10](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.09381v1). This means that clinically important cases — the ones where accurate labeling matters most — are precisely where human labelers disagree most.

An international teledermoscopy study found Cohen's kappa ranging from 0.57 to 0.93 for distinguishing malignant from benign lesions among dermatologists A-D, but dermatologist E diverged from the group with more frequent diagnoses of melanoma and atypical nevus — attributed to lack of consensus guidelines, unfamiliarity with the specific patient population, and "diagnostic drift" [11](https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/openlb/publication/OLB-PM-20795998). The BRAAFF checklist for acral melanoma (Dermatology, 2024) showed inter-rater agreement ranging from fair to moderate (alpha = 0.31-0.46), with the lowest agreement for parallel ridge and fibrillar patterns (alpha = 0.31) and highest for asymmetry of colors and structures (alpha = 0.46). Experience level correlated positively with both agreement and diagnostic accuracy [12](https://doi.org/10.1159/000541591).

These findings establish that any experimental comparison of VLMs to human labelers must use multiple human raters and report inter-rater agreement, because a single specialist's label is not an infallible reference. The VLM's performance should be benchmarked against the distribution of human labels, not against a single "ground truth" that may itself be contested.

### Human Diagnostic Accuracy Benchmarks

A JAMA Dermatology meta-analysis (2025) of 11 prospective studies involving 2,500+ patients and 50 dermatologists found pooled sensitivity of 78.6% (95% CI: 67.5%-88.1%) and specificity of 75.2% (95% CI: 63.3%-84.3%) for dermatologist melanoma diagnosis from dermoscopic images. AI systems (all CNN-based) achieved pooled sensitivity of 80.9% (95% CI: 63.6%-94.5%) and specificity of 75.6% (95% CI: 64.5%-85.6%) — statistically equivalent performance [13]. The PROVE-AI study (npj Digital Medicine, 2023) — a prospective clinical trial of 435 participants with 603 lesions (95 melanomas, all biopsy-confirmed) — found the ADAE algorithm achieved AUC 0.857 versus dermatologist AUC 0.780 (p = 0.007), with sensitivity of 96.8% at a 95% sensitivity threshold and specificity of 37.4% [14](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-023-00872-1). Critically, dermatologist performance improved after AI exposure (AUC 0.7798 -> 0.8161, p = 0.042), supporting an augmentation rather than substitution paradigm.

## Experimental Design

### Study Objectives and Hypotheses

**Primary Objective**: To determine whether a modern VLM can achieve non-inferior agreement with a panel of board-certified dermatologists on multi-class skin lesion labeling, compared to inter-rater agreement among the dermatologists themselves.

**Primary Hypothesis (H1)**: The VLM's Cohen's kappa with the majority-vote specialist label is not inferior to the mean pairwise specialist-specialist kappa by more than a predefined non-inferiority margin (delta = 0.05).

**Secondary Hypothesis (H2)**: VLM labeling performance does not vary significantly across Fitzpatrick skin types I-VI (difference in sensitivity across skin type strata < 10 percentage points).

**Exploratory Hypothesis (H3)**: VLM labeling with few-shot in-context learning outperforms zero-shot labeling by >= 5 percentage points in overall accuracy.

### Study Population and Dataset

The study should use a prospective, multicenter design enrolling consecutive patients presenting with pigmented skin lesions at 3-5 dermatology clinics across diverse geographic and demographic settings. Enrollment criteria:

- Adult patients (>= 18 years) with at least one pigmented skin lesion scheduled for biopsy or referred for specialist evaluation
- All Fitzpatrick skin types represented (target: >= 15% of lesions from skin types IV-VI to enable subgroup analysis)
- Minimum enrollment: 1,000 lesions (informed by sample size calculation, see below)

Each lesion is photographed using standardized dermoscopic imaging protocols (polarized dermoscopy, consistent lighting, resolution >= 1024 x 768 pixels) and a clinical close-up image. Patient metadata (age, sex, anatomical site, lesion history) is collected but withheld from the VLM in the image-only labeling condition and provided in a separate multimodal condition.

### VLM Configuration and Prompting Protocol

The experimental plan should test VLMs at three levels of task-specificity to capture the full landscape:

| Tier | Model | Rationale |
|------|-------|-----------|
| General-purpose VLM | GPT-4o (or latest available) | Tests frontier model zero/few-shot capability |
| Medical-specific VLM | MedGemma-27B or LLaVA-Med | Tests domain-adapted but not dermatology-specific models |
| Dermatology-specific | PanDerm (if accessible) or SkinGPT-4 | Tests best-in-class dermatology model |

Each model is evaluated under three prompting conditions: (1) zero-shot (instruction only); (2) few-shot (5 labeled examples per class provided in-context); and (3) chain-of-thought (model instructed to verbalize diagnostic reasoning before outputting a label). This 3 x 3 factorial design (9 conditions per lesion) enables disentangling model capability from prompting strategy.

### Human Rater Panel and Blinding

A panel of >= 5 board-certified dermatologists (>= 5 years post-certification experience) independently labels each lesion from dermoscopic images under three conditions: (1) image only; (2) image + brief clinical context (age, sex, site); (3) image + full clinical context (including lesion history). Raters are blinded to VLM outputs and to each other's labels. A second rating session after >= 4 weeks (washout period) enables intra-rater reliability assessment.

### Reference Standard

Histopathology serves as the reference standard for all excised lesions (target: >= 50% of enrolled lesions). For non-excised benign lesions, ground truth is established by: (1) >= 1.5 years of digital dermoscopic follow-up showing no change (for nevi); (2) expert consensus of >= 3 dermatologists for typical benign lesions; (3) reflectance confocal microscopy where available. Ambiguous or contested diagnoses are adjudicated by a panel of 3 dermatopathologists.

### Study Procedures

The study follows a five-step procedure. First, enrollment and imaging: consecutive enrollment with standardized dermoscopic and clinical images captured per protocol. Second, VLM labeling: each model-prompting combination labels all lesions; confidence scores and generated reasoning (for CoT condition) are recorded. Third, human labeling: dermatologist panel labels all lesions in 3 conditions, in randomized order, blinded to VLM outputs. Fourth, reference standard adjudication: biopsy results collected; follow-up scheduled for non-excised lesions. Fifth, adjudication of disagreements: a separate expert panel reviews cases where VLM and majority human labels disagree by > 1 diagnostic category, without knowing which label is the VLM's.

### Bias Mitigation and Quality Control

Several bias mitigation measures are implemented. Selection bias is addressed through consecutive enrollment at multiple sites to avoid cherry-picking easy cases. Spectrum bias is addressed by enrolling across the full range of lesion types (all 7 HAM10000 categories plus additional non-pigmented lesions). Image quality bias is addressed by including images of varying quality (blurry, hair-obstructed, gel artifacts) to reflect real-world conditions. Data leakage is prevented through strict separation of any few-shot examples from the test set; if using a pretrained VLM, verification that no test images appeared in the model's training data is performed. Reviewer bias is addressed by randomizing presentation order and using a digital platform that prevents rater comparison.

## Evaluation Metrics and Analysis Plan

### Primary Endpoints

The primary endpoint is **Cohen's kappa between the VLM label and the majority-vote specialist label**, compared against the **mean pairwise specialist-specialist kappa** using a non-inferiority framework:

- Non-inferiority margin: delta = 0.05 kappa units
- If the lower bound of the 95% CI for (kappa_VLM-specialist - kappa_specialist-specialist) exceeds -delta, non-inferiority is declared
- This design acknowledges that no single human label is infallible; the VLM need not match any individual specialist, but should match the panel consensus as well as specialists match each other

### Secondary and Exploratory Endpoints

| Metric | Purpose | Target |
|--------|---------|--------|
| Sensitivity (per class) | Malignancy detection rate | >= 90% for melanoma |
| Specificity (per class) | False positive rate | >= 70% for melanoma |
| AUC (per class) | Discrimination | >= 0.85 |
| Negative predictive value (NPV) | Rule-out capability | >= 95% for malignancy |
| Per-class accuracy | Multi-class performance | >= 75% for each of 7 classes |
| Calibration (ECE) | Confidence reliability | ECE <= 0.10 |
| Subgroup sensitivity by skin type | Fairness audit | Difference < 10 pp across Fitzpatrick I-VI |
| Subgroup sensitivity by image quality | Robustness | Difference < 15 pp between high/low quality |
| Intra-rater reliability (VLM) | Consistency | kappa >= 0.85 (VLMs should be perfectly consistent on re-testing) |
| Human-AI collaboration delta | Augmentation assessment | Dermatologist + VLM >= dermatologist alone |

### Sample Size Justification

Assuming a mean specialist-specialist kappa of 0.70 (based on the teledermoscopy literature range of 0.57-0.93) and a non-inferiority margin of 0.05:

- Using the formula for non-inferiority testing of kappa (based on the asymptotic variance of kappa), with alpha = 0.025 (one-sided), power = 80%, and assuming the VLM-specialist kappa is equivalent to specialist-specialist kappa
- Estimated sample size: approximately 1,000 lesions with complete reference standard data
- This accounts for ~10% attrition (incomplete follow-up, inadequate images, reference standard ambiguity)
- Subgroup analyses (skin type, disease class) require >= 150 lesions per stratum for adequate power

### Statistical Analysis Plan

The statistical analysis follows a structured plan. The primary analysis conducts a non-inferiority test of VLM-specialist kappa versus specialist-specialist kappa, using bootstrap 95% CI (10,000 resamples) to account for the paired, multiclass structure. Sensitivity analyses include: (a) restricting to pathology-confirmed lesions only; (b) excluding the most ambiguous lesions (lowest quartile of specialist agreement); (c) per-protocol analysis excluding images with technical artifacts.

Subgroup analyses are pre-specified by Fitzpatrick skin type, lesion class, image quality, anatomical site, prompting condition, and model tier. Fairness analysis computes absolute and relative performance differences across skin type strata; if > 10 pp, this is flagged as a fairness failure requiring mitigation before deployment. Calibration analysis computes Expected Calibration Error (ECE) by binning VLM confidence scores into 10 deciles. Error analysis: all false negatives (missed malignancies) undergo blinded review by the adjudication panel to categorize error types (visual misinterpretation, knowledge gap, confidence miscalibration, ambiguous reference standard).

### Reporting Standards Compliance

The study will be designed and reported in compliance with:

- **STARD-AI** (Nature Medicine, 2025): 18 new/modified items beyond STARD 2015, addressing dataset practices, AI index test specification, and algorithmic bias reporting [15](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41591-025-03953-8)
- **TRIPOD+AI** (BMJ, 2024): 27 core items for clinical prediction model reporting, covering development, validation, and fairness auditing [16](https://www.bmjchina.com.cn/News/Companynews/2025/1231/2135.html)
- **CONSORT 2025**: 30-item checklist for randomized trials (if the study advances to a comparative-effectiveness RCT phase) [17]
- **QUADAS-2 and QUADAS-C**: Risk of bias and applicability assessment for diagnostic accuracy studies

## Ethical, Regulatory, and Practical Considerations

### Data Privacy and De-identification

Under HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act), de-identified patient information — with all personally identifiable information (PHI) removed — may be used for research without authorization. For dermatological images, de-identification requires removal of: (1) EXIF metadata from image files; (2) potentially identifiable background content (garments, jewelry, tattoos); (3) any embedded patient identifiers in image filenames or folder structures. The HAM10000 dataset employed automated neural-network-based screening, multiple manual reviews, and EXIF data clearing to achieve anonymization [8](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018161). The study must obtain IRB (Institutional Review Board) approval at all participating sites before enrollment, with informed consent for prospective image acquisition and biopsy.

### Algorithmic Fairness and Skin Tone Bias

The most concerning finding for VLM substitution is the documented performance gap across skin tones. At the AAD 2026 conference, Lipoff reported that while AI melanoma detection accuracy exceeds 80% overall, it drops to approximately 17% for dark skin individuals — a catastrophic failure attributable to training data underrepresentation [18]. The Fitzpatrick skin type classification (I-VI) provides the standard stratification framework. Any experimental plan must:

- Prospectively record Fitzpatrick skin type for each enrolled patient
- Ensure >= 15% representation of skin types IV-VI (the "dark skin" category)
- Report stratified performance metrics with confidence intervals
- Treat a > 10 percentage-point gap in sensitivity between light and dark skin as a fairness failure that disqualifies the VLM from substitution regardless of overall performance

### Regulatory Framework: SaMD Classification

AI systems that perform medical diagnosis fall under the Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) regulatory framework. In the United States, the FDA's AI/ML-Based SaMD Action Plan governs such devices, with the Pre-Cert (Pre-Certification) pilot program shifting regulatory focus from per-product review to organization-level total product lifecycle (TPLC) oversight [19]. DermaSensor (January 2024) became the first FDA-authorized AI device for primary care skin cancer detection, establishing a regulatory precedent [20](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01161-1).

A critical regulatory challenge is that VLMs — especially large general-purpose models — may be updated or retrained by their developers, invalidating prior performance assessments. The regulatory framework requires that the specific model version evaluated in the experiment be documented with version identifiers, and that any subsequent model updates trigger revalidation.

In Australia, a review of 21 dermatology AI-based SaMD mobile applications found that none fully complied with common labeling recommendations — highlighting that regulatory frameworks have not yet caught up with deployment realities [21](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajd.14269). The EU MDR (Medical Device Regulation) combined with the AI Act requires Notified Body involvement for high-risk AI SaMD, with GDPR Article 9 governing health data processing legitimacy and mandating Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIA). China's NMPA classifies AI medical software under a three-tier system, and the 2026 AI Medical Imaging Research Ethical Guidelines mandate that AI outputs shall not serve as final diagnoses — physicians retain ultimate decision-making authority [22](https://www.digitalchina.gov.cn/2026/english/dn/202609/t20260903_5367508.htm).

### VLM Failure Modes and Risk Mitigation

Three failure modes identified in the literature demand specific mitigation.

**Textual bias**: GPT-4V's 53.7% image-only accuracy versus 89% multimodal accuracy reveals that VLMs may "reason" rather than "see" [7](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.01.24.24301743). Mitigation: evaluate image-only labeling performance separately from multimodal; image-only must meet non-inferiority threshold independently.

**Overconfidence miscalibration**: A documented case described a VLM labeling an early melanoma as "benign nevus, 96% confidence" — the model was confidently wrong, the most dangerous failure mode for a labeling tool. Mitigation: require ECE <= 0.10; implement a confidence threshold below which VLM labels are flagged for human review.

**Distribution shift**: The "evidence desert" critique — thousands of accuracy studies but few RCTs — reveals that lab performance on standardized datasets does not translate to real-world settings with out-of-distribution images, variable quality, and missing clinical context [23]. Mitigation: enroll consecutive real-world cases with varying image quality; report performance separately on in-distribution vs. out-of-distribution images.

### Decision Framework: Substitution Criteria

Based on the synthesized evidence, the following criteria must all be satisfied for a VLM to be deemed capable of substituting for human specialists in skin lesion labeling:

| Criterion | Threshold | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Non-inferiority in kappa | VLM-specialist kappa >= specialist-specialist kappa - 0.05 | VLM must match the agreement level specialists have with each other |
| Malignancy sensitivity | >= 90% for melanoma | Missing a melanoma is clinically catastrophic |
| NPV for malignancy | >= 95% | A labeling tool used for triage must safely rule out malignancy |
| Skin tone fairness | < 10 pp gap across Fitzpatrick I-VI | Algorithmic bias is ethically unacceptable |
| Confidence calibration | ECE <= 0.10 | Overconfidence is a known VLM failure mode |
| Uncertainty flagging | VLM must identify low-confidence cases for human review | No substitution system can operate without a safety valve |
| Regulatory compliance | Meets SaMD labeling and transparency requirements | Deployable systems require regulatory authorization |
| Reporting standards | Study reported per STARD-AI and TRIPOD+AI | Peer review and replication require transparent reporting |

If all criteria are met, the VLM may be considered for supervised deployment as a "first-pass" labeler with human review of flagged uncertain cases. If any criterion fails, the VLM should be restricted to an augmentation role (assisting, not replacing, human labelers).

## Conclusion

The evidence synthesized in this report reveals a field at a critical inflection point. On the one hand, dermatology-specific foundation models like PanDerm — trained on 2.1 million images across 4 imaging modalities — already surpass human dermatologists by 10.2% on specific tasks, and CNN-based AI systems show pooled sensitivity (80.9%) and specificity (75.6%) statistically equivalent to dermatologist performance (78.6% and 75.2%) in prospective meta-analyses. On the other hand, general-purpose VLMs like GPT-4V demonstrate a troubling disconnect: their visual understanding of skin lesions (53.7% image-only accuracy) lags far behind their clinical reasoning capability (89% multimodal accuracy), suggesting that current VLMs "reason" about dermatology better than they "see" it.

The proposed experimental design addresses this tension through a prospective, multicenter, multi-reader study that benchmarks VLM labeling against a panel of >= 5 board-certified dermatologists using a non-inferiority framework on Cohen's kappa. The design incorporates critical safeguards absent from much of the existing literature: stratified fairness audits across Fitzpatrick skin types (addressing the 17% vs. 80% dark-skin performance gap), confidence calibration assessment (addressing overconfidence failure modes), image-only evaluation (addressing textual bias), and compliance with STARD-AI reporting standards (addressing the "evidence desert" of transparent, reproducible studies).

The decision framework requires satisfaction of 8 simultaneous criteria spanning agreement, safety, fairness, calibration, uncertainty management, regulatory compliance, and reporting transparency. This multi-gate approach reflects the reality that substituting a machine for a human specialist in medical image labeling is not merely a technical performance question — it is a safety, equity, and governance question that demands evidence from rigorous prospective studies, not retrospective benchmarks on curated datasets. Until a VLM passes all eight gates in a properly powered, STARD-AI-compliant prospective trial, its role should be augmentation, not substitution: the dermatologist remains the ultimate accountable entity, and the VLM serves as a high-throughput first-pass labeler with mandatory human review of uncertain cases.
