# Managing Oil Wealth to Avoid the Resource Curse

This report synthesizes public information and academic work published up to and including 2020 on how oil-dependent countries manage resource wealth. It defines the core concepts and their economic and political impacts, explains the three main fiscal rules, examines the two central revenue-management tools (natural resource funds and public investment management systems), and analyzes Norway as the benchmark case.

## 1. Core Challenges: The Resource Curse and Dutch Disease

The "resource curse" is a strong recurrent tendency—not an iron law—for countries rich in point-source natural resources to grow more slowly, suffer greater macroeconomic volatility, and show weaker governance and development outcomes than resource-poor countries. Dutch disease is its central real-side mechanism: an oil windfall appreciates the real exchange rate and reallocates labour and capital toward the resource sector and non-tradables, squeezing manufacturing and other tradable sectors.

### 1.1 The resource curse: definition and transmission channels

Richard Auty coined the term "resource curse" in 1993 to describe the observed association between resource abundance and disappointing economic development; he treated it as a "strong recurrent tendency" rather than a deterministic outcome ([Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001, World Bank](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content); [Auty, *Sustaining Development in Mineral Economies*](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.4324/9780203422595/sustaining-development-mineral-economies-richard-auty)). Its empirical foundation was laid by Sachs and Warner, whose cross-country regressions showed that economies with a high ratio of natural-resource exports to GDP in 1971 subsequently grew more slowly over 1971–1989, even after controlling for initial income, trade policy, government efficiency and investment rates ([Sachs & Warner 1995, NBER Working Paper 5398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5398)). The Natural Resource Governance Institute (NRGI) defines the curse as "the significant social, economic and political challenges that are unique to countries rich in oil, gas and minerals," noting that such countries are on average more authoritarian, more prone to conflict and less economically stable ([NRGI, *Primer: The Resource Curse*, 2015](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/nrgi_Resource-Curse.pdf)).

A key conceptual distinction is between **resource abundance** and **resource dependence**. Abundance refers to a physical endowment—oil reserves, mineral deposits or natural capital per capita—whereas dependence measures how large a share of exports, fiscal revenue or GDP those resources account for. Sachs and Warner proxied abundance by resource exports relative to GDP, while Gylfason used the share of natural capital in national wealth and found that growth since 1965 varied inversely with it ([Sachs & Warner 1995](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5398); [Gylfason 2001, *European Economic Review* 45:847–859](https://gylfason.hi.is/en/natural-resources-education-and-economic-development-2/)). The distinction matters because a country can be resource-abundant but not resource-dependent if other sectors are large, and the two concepts carry different policy implications.

The curse has an **economic face** (slow growth, revenue volatility, de-industrialization, fiscal mismanagement) and a **political face** (rentier-state behaviour, weak institutions, corruption, patronage, conflict). Ross's (1999) review essay consolidated the political resource curse as a distinct field, synthesizing rentier-state and institutional arguments ([Ross 1999, *World Politics* 51:297–322](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/political-economy-of-the-resource-curse/EBEA5E178E7534C4BA38EE23D25322E0)). Ross (2001) then showed, using pooled data for 113 states from 1971 to 1997, that oil exports are strongly associated with authoritarian rule; that the effect is not limited to the Middle East; and that other mineral exports have a similar antidemocratic effect while other commodity exports do not. He found limited support for three mechanisms: a **rentier effect** (low taxes and patronage dampen democratic pressures), a **repression effect** (resource wealth funds internal security), and a **modernization effect** (oil-led growth fails to produce the social change that typically accompanies democratization) ([Ross 2001, *World Politics* 53:325–361](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/does-oil-hinder-democracy/67665D8D240C8F43CD4A2DCB35894071)). A **rentier state** is one that derives a large share of its revenue from external rents rather than domestic taxation, weakening the fiscal contract between state and citizens.

Mehlum, Moene and Torvik (2006) sharpened the institutional argument: countries rich in natural resources are both "growth losers and growth winners," and the difference is institutional quality—more resources reduce income when institutions are "grabber friendly" but raise it when institutions are "producer friendly" ([Mehlum, Moene & Torvik 2006, *Economic Journal* 116:1–20](https://www.prio.org/publications/3514)). Karl's *The Paradox of Plenty* used case material from Venezuela, Iran, Nigeria, Algeria and Indonesia to show how petroleum dependence produces disproportionate fiscal reliance on petrodollars and public spending "at the expense of statecraft," with oil booms destabilizing regimes and weakening state capacity ([Stanford CDDRL summary of Karl 1997](https://cddrl.fsi.stanford.edu/publications/the_paradox_of_plenty_oil_booms_and_petrostates)).

The principal transmission channels are:

- **Revenue price volatility and boom–bust cycles.** Resource revenues are exposed to world price swings, encouraging excessive spending during booms and drastic cuts when they end ([Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content)).
- **Dutch disease and crowding-out of tradables.** Windfalls appreciate the real exchange rate and draw resources toward non-tradables, contracting manufacturing and other tradable sectors ([Gylfason 2001](https://gylfason.hi.is/en/natural-resources-education-and-economic-development-2/); [Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content)). This is one mechanism within the economic curse, not the whole curse; its mechanics are set out in Section 1.2.
- **Rent-seeking and corruption.** Abundant rents create opportunities for corruption and rent-seeking—the pursuit of resource rents rather than productive activity—which divert resources from productive uses ([Mehlum, Moene & Torvik 2006](https://www.prio.org/publications/3514); [Gylfason 2001](https://gylfason.hi.is/en/natural-resources-education-and-economic-development-2/); [Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content)). Leite and Weidmann's IMF Working Paper 99/85, *Does Mother Nature Corrupt?*, is the canonical cross-country test of the corruption channel ([Leite & Weidmann 1999, IMF WP 99/85](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/1999/wp9985.pdf)). **Evidence note:** the full text of that paper could not be retrieved, so its characterization here rests on the paper's title, working-paper metadata and the consistent rent-seeking findings in the sources above.
- **Institutional erosion and rentier politics.** As above, a large external-rent share weakens the fiscal contract and accountability ([Ross 2001](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/does-oil-hinder-democracy/67665D8D240C8F43CD4A2DCB35894071); [Karl 1997](https://cddrl.fsi.stanford.edu/publications/the_paradox_of_plenty_oil_booms_and_petrostates)).
- **Conflict.** Collier and Hoeffler find that "political and social variables that are most obviously related to grievances have little explanatory power," while economic variables—more obviously related to the viability of rebellion—explain considerably more; natural resources are a source of extortion that finances rebellion ([Collier & Hoeffler 2004, *Oxford Economic Papers* 56:563–595](https://academic.oup.com/oep/article-abstract/56/4/563/2361902)).
- **Education and human-capital neglect.** Gylfason shows that public education spending, expected years of schooling for girls and secondary enrolment are all inversely related to the share of natural capital in national wealth: "natural capital appears to crowd out human capital, thereby slowing down the pace of economic development" ([Gylfason 2001](https://gylfason.hi.is/en/natural-resources-education-and-economic-development-2/)).
- **Enclave economies and low linkages.** The boom sector often has weak forward and backward linkages with the rest of the economy, limiting diversification and employment spillovers ([Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content)).

Oil is a **point-source** resource: production is geographically concentrated and the rents accrue to a small number of firms and to the state rather than being dispersed across a large population of producers, as with diffuse agricultural resources. This concentration makes the rents easy to capture and centralizes the revenue flow—which is why Ross (2001) finds antidemocratic effects for oil and mineral exports but not for other commodity exports ([Ross 2001](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/does-oil-hinder-democracy/67665D8D240C8F43CD4A2DCB35894071)), and why Sarraf and Jiwanji emphasize the boom sector's low linkages and associated rent-seeking ([Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content)). Point-source character is a conditioning feature, not a separate curse.

**The empirical foundation is contested.** Sachs and Warner themselves state that "empirical support for the curse of natural resources is not bulletproof, but it is quite strong," and find little support for bias from omitted geography or unobserved growth determinants ([Sachs & Warner 2001](https://www.earth.columbia.edu/sitefiles/file/about/director/pubs/EuroEconReview2001.pdf)). Sarraf and Jiwanji report that "some studies find that the resource curse phenomenon is not widespread" and that there is "no clear consensus on the measurement of resource abundance," citing export dependence, per capita land area, export orientation and population size as competing metrics ([Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content)). Mehlum, Moene and Torvik mount a sharper critique: if institutions are decisive, pooling resource-abundant countries without conditioning on institutional quality will misstate the average effect ([Mehlum, Moene & Torvik 2006](https://www.prio.org/publications/3514)). The "resource blessing" counter-literature therefore does not deny that poorly governed resource exporters can fail; it argues that abundance itself is not destiny and that the measured curse is sensitive to how abundance is measured and which countries and institutions are included.

### 1.2 Dutch disease: mechanism and channels

Corden defines Dutch disease as "the adverse effects on Dutch manufacturing of the natural gas discoveries of the nineteen sixties, essentially through the subsequent appreciation of the Dutch real exchange rate," tracing the first printed use to *The Economist* of 26 November 1977 ([Corden 1984, *Oxford Economic Papers* 36(3):359–380](https://www.depfe.unam.mx/actividades/12/desarrollo-comparado/Corden_OEP_1984.pdf)). Corden and Neary formalize it as "the phenomenon whereby a boom in one traded goods sector squeezes profitability in other traded goods sectors, both by directly bidding resources away from them and by placing upward pressure on the exchange rate" ([Corden & Neary 1982, IIASA CP-82-58](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/2060/1/CP-82-058.pdf)).

The Corden–Neary model divides a small open economy into three sectors: a **booming resource sector (B)**, a **lagging non-resource tradable sector (L)** producing manufacturing and agricultural goods at given world prices, and a **non-tradable sector (N)** producing services and construction at domestically determined prices. The real exchange rate is the price of non-tradables relative to tradables. A boom can arise from a technical improvement, a resource discovery or an exogenous rise in the world price of the export ([Corden & Neary 1982](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/2060/1/CP-82-058.pdf); [Corden 1984](https://www.depfe.unam.mx/actividades/12/desarrollo-comparado/Corden_OEP_1984.pdf); [Brahmbhatt, Canuto & Vostroknutova 2010, World Bank PREM Economic Premise No. 16 / VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)).

**The two classic effects.**

- **Spending effect.** Part of the extra income in B is spent, whether directly by factor owners or through taxes and government expenditure. If the income elasticity of demand for non-tradables is positive, the price of N relative to tradables must rise—a **real appreciation**. This draws resources out of B and L into N and shifts demand away from N toward B and L ([Corden & Neary 1982](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/2060/1/CP-82-058.pdf); [Corden 1984](https://www.depfe.unam.mx/actividades/12/desarrollo-comparado/Corden_OEP_1984.pdf)). In manufacturing, prices are fixed internationally, so profits are squeezed by rising economy-wide wages and demand is increasingly met from imports ([Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)).
- **Resource-movement effect.** The boom raises the marginal product of labour in B, increasing the demand for labour there and inducing movement of labour out of L and N into B ([Corden & Neary 1982](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/2060/1/CP-82-058.pdf); [Corden 1984](https://www.depfe.unam.mx/actividades/12/desarrollo-comparado/Corden_OEP_1984.pdf)). Corden labels the movement of labour out of L into B **"direct de-industrialisation"** because it lowers L output without requiring an appreciation of the real exchange rate.

**Flow sequence.** Oil windfall → higher factor incomes in B → (*spending effect*) higher spending on N → P<sub>N</sub>/P<sub>tradables</sub> rises (real appreciation) → resources drawn into N and L squeezed; simultaneously (*resource-movement effect*) higher marginal product of labour in B → labour and capital move from L and N into B → direct de-industrialisation. The joint outcome is a fall in the output share of non-resource tradables relative to non-tradables and a real exchange-rate appreciation ([Corden & Neary 1982](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/2060/1/CP-82-058.pdf); [Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)).

| Sector | Direction | Primary mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Oil / resource (B) | Expands | Higher marginal product of labour and higher rents draw in factors ([Corden & Neary 1982](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/2060/1/CP-82-058.pdf)) |
| Non-resource tradables (manufacturing, agriculture, L) | Contracts | Resource-movement effect (direct de-industrialisation) plus spending effect (real appreciation, wage squeeze, import competition) ([Corden 1984](https://www.depfe.unam.mx/actividades/12/desarrollo-comparado/Corden_OEP_1984.pdf); [Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)) |
| Non-tradables (services, construction, N) | Expands | Spending effect raises relative price and output ([Corden & Neary 1982](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/2060/1/CP-82-058.pdf); [Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)) |
| Economy-wide | Real appreciation; falling non-resource tradable share; export concentration | Joint outcome of both effects ([Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)) |

The two real effects are complemented by monetary and liquidity channels analysed in Neary and van Wijnbergen's (1986) edited volume, the standard reference for extending the framework beyond relative prices and factor allocation. **Evidence gap:** the volume could not be accessed, so the liquidity effect is named as a pre-2020 concept but not formalized here.

**Empirical evidence.** Cross-country work finds relatively robust evidence that terms-of-trade increases cause real appreciation in resource-rich countries (Spatafora & Warner 1995, cited in [Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)); that a 10% increase in an oil windfall is associated with a 3.4% fall in manufacturing value added, with larger effects in economies more open to capital flows and with less capital-intensive manufacturing (Ismail 2010, cited in [Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)); and that resource-rich countries' tradables sector averaged about 15% of GDP below the Chenery–Syrquin norm over 1975–2005 ([Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)). Benjamin, Devarajan and Weiner (1989) applied the framework to Cameroon, the standard developing-country case; the full text was not retrieved, so its specific estimates are not reported here ([Benjamin, Devarajan & Weiner 1989, *Journal of Development Economics* 30:71–92](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0304387889900515)). Fardmanesh (1991) applies Dutch-disease reasoning to developing oil exporters; again, the full text was not retrievable ([Fardmanesh 1991, *World Development* 19(6):711–717](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/wdevel/v19y1991i6p711-717.html)). Corden identifies Forsyth and Kay (1980) as the key article in the British North Sea discussion, but detailed UK estimates were not retrieved ([Corden 1984](https://www.depfe.unam.mx/actividades/12/desarrollo-comparado/Corden_OEP_1984.pdf); [Corden & Neary 1982](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/2060/1/CP-82-058.pdf)).

**Automatic or policy-dependent?** The sources agree that the real-side reallocation is an equilibrium tendency, not an inherent "disease": non-resource tradables decline and non-tradables expand, but average incomes can still be higher after the boom. At the same time, real exchange-rate appreciation "is in principle an equilibrium phenomenon that reflects improved fundamentals," but agents may overestimate the permanence of the improvement, producing overvaluation ([Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease)). The policy-dependence view is explicit: "excessive spending appears to be at the heart of economic mismanagement in the wake of natural resource booms," and fiscal policy is the main instrument for dealing with the negative impacts ([Brahmbhatt et al. 2010](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease), citing Gelb and Associates 1988). Mitigation channels therefore include sound fiscal rules that constrain the spending effect; smoothing expenditure and tilting its composition toward investment rather than consumption; accumulating resource revenues abroad in sovereign wealth funds; and sterilised foreign investment as a monetary-policy response—the last noted here but not detailed, because the retrieved sources emphasize fiscal policy.

### 1.3 Specific negative impacts and the scope for escape

Oil dependence produces a cluster of interlocking macroeconomic, governance and social pathologies rather than a single outcome. The recurrent channels are slower long-run growth, manufacturing decline and export concentration, revenue-driven boom–bust fiscal cycles, real exchange-rate overvaluation and inflation, institutional erosion and rent-seeking, and heightened risk of civil conflict.

**Macroeconomic impacts.** The foundational cross-country result is slower growth for resource-dependent economies ([Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content); [NRGI 2015](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/nrgi_Resource-Curse.pdf)). Oil revenues swing with prices and production, and governments tend to raise spending on legacy projects during booms and make painful cuts when revenues fall; over-borrowing against temporarily high revenues produces debt crises when prices collapse. NRGI documents this for Mexico, Nigeria and Venezuela in the 1980s, and its Venezuela series shows expenditure volatility tracking revenue volatility ([NRGI 2015](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/nrgi_Resource-Curse.pdf)). *Pro-cyclical fiscal policy*—spending that rises in booms and falls in busts—is the operative concept; it converts revenue volatility into output and employment volatility. De-industrialization follows the Dutch-disease mechanism: NRGI documents stunted or declining manufacturing in Iran, Russia, Trinidad and Tobago and Venezuela, while noting that Chile, Indonesia, Norway and the UAE "largely managed to overcome Dutch disease" ([NRGI 2015](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/nrgi_Resource-Curse.pdf)). Export concentration follows, because oil dominates export earnings and a price swing moves the whole external account.

**Governance and social impacts.** Oil wealth makes governments less dependent on citizen taxation, weakening the fiscal contract that underpins accountability; resource-rich states have been more likely to become or remain authoritarian over recent decades ([NRGI 2015](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/nrgi_Resource-Curse.pdf); [Ross 1999](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/political-economy-of-the-resource-curse/EBEA5E178E7534C4BA38EE23D25322E0)). Large, volatile and often secret rents create opportunities for rent-seeking and patronage ([Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content); [Leite & Weidmann 1999](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/1999/wp9985.pdf)). On conflict, NRGI reports that since 1990 oil-producing countries have been roughly twice as likely to experience civil war as non-oil producers, citing the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the Niger Delta, Iraq, Libya and Angola ([NRGI 2015](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/nrgi_Resource-Curse.pdf)); the greed-and-grievance framework of Collier and Hoeffler is the standard academic anchor for the resource-rents-to-conflict channel ([Collier & Hoeffler 2004](https://academic.oup.com/oep/article-abstract/56/4/563/2361902)). Finally, oil's point-source, capital-intensive, site-specific, export-oriented character creates **enclave economies**—extraction sectors with limited employment and weak backward linkages—and rents that elites capture for **patrimonialism**, patronage-based rule ([NRGI 2015](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/nrgi_Resource-Curse.pdf); [Karl 1997](https://cddrl.fsi.stanford.edu/publications/the_paradox_of_plenty_oil_booms_and_petrostates)).

Oil combines high rent, low employment density, capital intensity, export orientation, price volatility, non-renewability and secrecy ([NRGI 2015](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/nrgi_Resource-Curse.pdf)). These features mean revenues are large but geographically and socially concentrated, easily captured by governments, and unstable—intensifying both the economic channel (volatility, Dutch disease) and the political channel (rent-seeking, weak accountability, conflict).

**Counterexamples and escape conditions.** Botswana is the canonical escape case: sound economic policies and good windfall management—budget discipline, accumulation of international reserves, exchange-rate management and diversification—allowed it to sustain growth despite its mineral boom ([Sarraf & Jiwanji 2001](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5a9b3ed5-2111-5216-b598-4ba7586592bd/content)). NRGI identifies Chile, Indonesia, Norway and the UAE as having largely overcome Dutch disease, and notes that damage can be minimized where governments have the absorptive capacity to convert revenues into productive investment, invest in non-resource growth, or place revenues in foreign assets ([NRGI 2015](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/nrgi_Resource-Curse.pdf)). The implication is that the curse is conditional on institutions and policy, not on geology. The country-specific estimates for Cameroon and for a six-economy comparison of Algeria, Ecuador, Trinidad and Tobago and Indonesia are part of the literature but are not reproduced here; the synthesis above rests on cross-country and multi-country sources.

## 2. Fiscal Policy Strategies

Three fiscal rules dominate the academic and policy discussion: the **Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH) rule**, the **bird-in-hand rule**, and the **structural/non-oil balance rule**. They differ chiefly in whether they treat oil still in the ground as government wealth and in how tightly they bind spending. The PIH rule counts the net present value (NPV) of reserves as part of total wealth and spends a permanent annuity; the bird-in-hand rule waits for extraction and spends only realized returns on accumulated above-ground assets; structural/non-oil balance rules ignore underground wealth and delink the budget from resource revenue using a reference price or trend output. No single rule dominates, and in practice the rules are usually combined with Natural Resource Funds rather than used in isolation.

### 2.1 Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH) rule

The PIH rule treats oil wealth—resources still underground as well as accumulated financial assets—as part of total government wealth, computes the NPV of that wealth, and spends only the **permanent income** (the expected real return on total wealth) each year, preserving the real value of the principal for future generations. In formula form:

> **Permanent income = r\* × W**, where **W = NPV of expected future petroleum revenues + accumulated financial assets**.

Operationally, the government estimates recoverable reserves and the production profile, projects the resource rent (price minus extraction cost), discounts the rent stream at the real rate to obtain W, and spends r\* × W each year while saving all current petroleum revenue in the fund. Because the underground component of W falls as extraction proceeds and the above-ground component rises by the same amount, total real wealth—and therefore the spending ceiling—is held constant in real terms. The rule adapts Friedman's (1957) household consumption theory to government budgeting, grounded in the exhaustible-resource literature (Solow 1974; Stiglitz 1974) ([Segura 2006, IMF Working Paper 06/183](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imfwpa/2006-183.html)). Van der Ploeg and Venables describe the mechanism precisely: "The horizontal line is the PIH, giving a constant and permanent increase. This involves saving whilst revenue is flowing and building up a [sovereign wealth fund] large enough for interest on the fund to maintain the consumption increment in perpetuity" ([van der Ploeg & Venables 2011, OxCarre Research Paper 09](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:a7f82051-5f06-4c5e-92bf-d234c3f66ffa/files/sv118rd977)).

The accounting foundation is El Serafy (1989), who argued that the surplus from a depletable resource comprises two values: a true income component that can be consumed, and a separate depletion (capital) cost that must be reinvested to compensate future generations. As described in the peer-reviewed literature, "El Serafy (1989) and Mikesell (1994) argue that this surplus value is composed of two values, a true income and the depletion cost" ([Santopietro 1998, *Resources Policy*](https://www.cbd.int/financial/values/argentina-estimaterents.pdf)). This capital/income split is exactly the logic the PIH applies at the budget level.

The real-return (discount-rate) assumption is the linchpin: a higher assumed r raises permanent income and licenses more spending; a lower r does the reverse. The IMF's foundational volume on fiscal policy in oil producers stresses that oil price volatility makes the wealth estimate uncertain and devotes attention to the statistical properties of oil prices and the calculation of government wealth ([Davis, Ossowski & Fedelino eds. 2003, IMF](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/nft/2003/fispol/)). Spending current oil revenues instead makes fiscal expenditure as volatile as oil prices; the PIH smooths this by delinking the annual spending ceiling from current revenue.

| Country | Instrument | PIH element | Operationalization | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Timor-Leste | Petroleum Fund (Law No. 9/2005, amended by Law No. 12/2011) | Estimated Sustainable Income (ESI) = 3% of total Petroleum Wealth | Petroleum Wealth = fund balance + NPV of expected future petroleum revenue; transfers guided by the ESI; investment objective of a 3% real return; external auditor certifies the calculation | [IFSWF, Timor-Leste Petroleum Fund](https://www.ifswf.org/members/timor-leste); [Petroleum Fund Annual Report 2020](https://www.laohamutuk.org/Oil/PetFund/2022/220411MF_PFAR20en.pdf) |
| São Tomé and Príncipe | PIH-based oil wealth management framework | PIH fiscal rule applied to the oil sector | Segura (2006) is the canonical IMF treatment of PIH-based oil wealth management, using São Tomé and Príncipe as its case | [Segura 2006, IMF WP 06/183](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imfwpa/2006-183.html) |
| Alaska (United States) | Alaska Permanent Fund (constitutional amendment approved 1976) and Permanent Fund Dividend | Principal preserved; income (real return) available for spending | At least 25% of certain mineral revenues paid into principal; principal used only for income-producing investments; dividend funded from fund income | [Alaska Permanent Fund Corporation, Our History](https://apfc.org/about/history/) |
| Botswana | Pula Fund / diamond wealth | PIH analysis of diamond revenue | Botswana's reliance on diamond revenue analyzed through the PIH lens (Basdevant 2008, listed in van der Ploeg & Venables 2011) | [van der Ploeg & Venables 2011, RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/oxf/wpaper/543.html) |

**Critiques and suitability.** The scholarly critiques are well defined. First, **over-optimistic return assumptions**: if the assumed real return exceeds what the fund actually earns, the rule licenses spending that erodes wealth. Second, **dependence on uncertain reserve and price estimates**: the NPV of resource wealth depends on reserve estimates, production profiles and oil price assumptions, all subject to revision ([Davis, Ossowski & Fedelino eds. 2003](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/nft/2003/fispol/)). Third, **absorptive-capacity constraints**: a smooth, permanent consumption increment may not fit poor countries with large unmet infrastructure needs; van der Ploeg and Venables conclude that "incremental consumption should be skewed towards present generations, relative to those in the far future" and that savings should fund domestic capital formation ([van der Ploeg & Venables 2011](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:a7f82051-5f06-4c5e-92bf-d234c3f66ffa/files/sv118rd977)). Fourth, **political myopia and governance**: the PIH assumes a benevolent planner able to commit to a smooth path; in practice, political-economy pressures favour current spending and over-consumption during booms ([Davis, Ossowski & Fedelino eds. 2003](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/nft/2003/fispol/); [van der Ploeg & Venables 2011](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:a7f82051-5f06-4c5e-92bf-d234c3f66ffa/files/sv118rd977)). The verdict is therefore conditional: the PIH rule suits high-capacity, capital-abundant producers (Norway, Kuwait, Alaska), but its informational, institutional and political preconditions are often absent in low-capacity developing economies. **Evidence gap:** the full texts of Segura (2006) and the Adam FES policy paper were not retrievable, so claims about them are limited to titles, topics and reference lists.

### 2.2 Bird-in-hand rule

The bird-in-hand rule is the most conservative of the three. Its core logic is to **exclude underground oil wealth from the spending base**: petroleum revenues are saved as they are extracted, and only after they have been converted into above-ground financial assets does the government permit spending—and then only up to the **realized real return** on those accumulated assets. Iacono (2012) characterizes it as "the conservative Bird in Hand rule," an ad-hoc rule designed to reduce spending discretion and the macroeconomic risks that arise when governments overestimate revenues ([Iacono 2012, NTNU Working Paper No. 3/2012](https://www.svt.ntnu.no/iso/WP/2012/3_Ultima%20bozza.pdf)).

| Rule | Spending formula | Spending base |
|---|---|---|
| Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH) | S<sub>t</sub> = r\* × W | Expected real return r\* applied to the NPV W of **total** resource wealth (underground reserves + above-ground financial assets) |
| Bird-in-hand | S<sub>t</sub> = r<sub>t</sub> × A<sub>t−1</sub> | **Realized** real return r<sub>t</sub> applied only to accumulated above-ground financial assets A |

The bird-in-hand rule is more conservative than the PIH rule for four reasons. It **ignores underground wealth**, removing the need to forecast uncertain reserve volumes and future prices when setting the spending ceiling. It uses **realized rather than expected returns**, providing a certainty-equivalent spending limit that mitigates the risk governments spend on the basis of over-optimistic return assumptions—a risk Frankel (2010) emphasized when he noted that governments "have often overestimated revenues and dangerously relied on the inflated version of their budget constraints" (cited in [Iacono 2012](https://www.svt.ntnu.no/iso/WP/2012/3_Ultima%20bozza.pdf)). It **preserves the principal** of accumulated financial assets, giving the rule a strong intergenerational-equity property. And it **reduces boom-time over-spending**, because the ceiling is delinked from oil prices and expectations about future revenues—precisely the pro-cyclical pattern Villafuerte and Lopez-Murphy (2010) documented across 31 oil-producing countries in 2000–2008 (cited in [Iacono 2012](https://www.svt.ntnu.no/iso/WP/2012/3_Ultima%20bozza.pdf)).

For **macroeconomic stabilization**, the realized-return ceiling is less volatile than oil prices, so it can dampen expenditure volatility. Landon and Smith (2012) show that rule-based stabilization funds reduce expenditure volatility, with the best-performing design in their Alberta simulations depositing 50% of resource revenues and withdrawing 25% of assets, cutting volatility by almost 30% ([Landon & Smith 2012, "Government Revenue Stabilization Funds"](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~slandon/CPP2012StabilizationFunds.pdf)). However, they also find that "stabilization funds that accumulate large asset stocks and, thus, generate low levels of current government services, generally yield low welfare"—a direct caution for bird-in-hand-type rules that may over-accumulate. Iacono (2012) links the rule to the **precautionary savings** motive, noting that Bems and Filho (2010) find a positive and significant precautionary savings motive across resource-rich economies ([Iacono 2012](https://www.svt.ntnu.no/iso/WP/2012/3_Ultima%20bozza.pdf)).

**Country applications.** Norway's statutory framework is formally a structural non-oil balance rule (analyzed in Section 4.1), not a statutory "bird-in-hand" rule; but its operational ceiling—the expected real return on accumulated Government Pension Fund Global assets, set at 4% and revised to 3% in 2017—means the government spends only the return on **already accumulated** assets, which is bird-in-hand-like in effect. Harding and van der Ploeg's comparative study examines Norway against both permanent-income and bird-in-hand rules ([Harding & van der Ploeg, SSB Discussion Paper 676](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ssb/dispap/676.html)); the classification is analytic, not statutory. Kazakhstan's National Fund of the Republic of Kazakhstan (NFRK), established in 2000, combines a stabilization function with a savings function and explicitly assigns itself a "savings function to address inter-generational equity from the oil windfall," investing most oil revenue inflows abroad. Its rules include a non-oil deficit anchor progressively reduced to 7% of GDP by 2020 and 6% by 2025, a minimum asset floor of 30% of GDP, a requirement that privatization revenues flow into the fund, and a cap requiring general government and state-owned-enterprise debt not to exceed NFRK assets ([ADB, "National Fund of the Republic of Kazakhstan"](https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/linked-documents/54188-001-sd-05.pdf)). This is often characterized as a conservative, bird-in-hand-type asset-accumulation rule, though its stabilization mandate and guaranteed/targeted transfers make it a hybrid rather than a pure bird-in-hand arrangement. Timor-Leste's Petroleum Fund is frequently discussed alongside conservative fund-based rules, but its withdrawal rule permits spending above the estimated sustainable income under specified conditions, so it is not a strict bird-in-hand rule.

**Critiques and scholarly debate.** Maliszewski (2009) finds the bird-in-hand rule suboptimal relative to the PIH rule in welfare terms, a result cited by Iacono (2012). Landon and Smith (2012) reinforce the over-accumulation concern: funds accumulating large asset stocks generate low welfare because they suppress current government services. By permitting spending only from realized returns, the rule may also under-invest in infrastructure, education and health—sectors with high social returns in resource-dependent developing countries. The counter-argument is that, despite its conservatism, the relative welfare loss from adopting the bird-in-hand rule is approximately null; Iacono's calibration leads him to conclude: "No, it is not that much worse with a Bird in Hand policy" ([Iacono 2012](https://www.svt.ntnu.no/iso/WP/2012/3_Ultima%20bozza.pdf)). This suggests the rule's cost may be smaller than critics claim, while its insurance value against forecast errors and political myopia remains significant. The rule is most appropriate for countries with strong institutions, credible asset management and capital-market access. **Evidence gap:** the formal derivation of the bird-in-hand formula rests here on secondary discussions (Iacono 2012; JICA 2016), because IMF Working Paper 06/183 and the ECB occasional paper treating PIH and bird-in-hand as alternative anchors were not accessible.

### 2.3 Structural balance / non-oil balance rule

The third main fiscal anchor is the *structural (cyclically adjusted) or non-oil balance rule*. Instead of spending current resource revenue, the government targets a budget balance that excludes resource revenue and is adjusted for the economic cycle and for a long-run reference price of the resource. Its core logic is to delink the budget from the commodity price cycle: booms should not become permanent spending commitments, and busts should not force abrupt cuts. Chile is the pioneer and most fully developed case, operating a structural surplus rule since 2001 and pairing it, since the 2006 Fiscal Responsibility Law, with a Pension Reserve Fund and an Economic and Social Stabilization Fund.

A *structural balance* is the actual budget balance adjusted for the economy's cyclical position and, in resource producers, for the gap between the current resource price and a long-run reference price. A *non-oil* or *non-resource balance* goes further and excludes resource-related revenue and expenditure altogether. Ossowski (2013) calls the first concept the nonresource fiscal balance (NRB) and the second the nonresource primary balance (NRPB), and argues that the NRPB is the main sustainability indicator for resource producers because resource revenue is "more like financing—a transformation of assets from exhaustible resource reserves in the ground to other assets" ([Ossowski 2013, IDB-DP-290](https://webimages.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Fiscal-Rules-and-Resource-Funds-in-Nonrenewable-Resource-Exporting-Countries-International-Experience.pdf)). The macroeconomic case is to "decouple public spending as much as possible from volatile and uncertain resource revenue streams in the short term."

Operationally, the rule works in four steps: (1) estimate trend/potential output and a long-run reference price for the resource; (2) compute **structural revenue**—what revenue would be at trend output and the reference price; (3) set expenditure equal to structural revenue minus the target structural balance; and (4) save actual revenue above structural revenue and finance actual revenue shortfalls from accumulated assets. Because expenditure follows *structural* rather than *actual* revenue, the budget is insulated from price cycles.

Chile's implementation is the clearest example. "From 2001 the fiscal policy in Chile has been based on a structural surplus rule," under which "the government is committing itself to maintain its expenditures equal to the structural revenue minus the target for the structural surplus, which is expressed in terms of the GDP. The calculation of the structural balance depends on trend output, the long-run copper price and the structural income from Codelco's molybdenum sales" ([Danmarks Nationalbank, Pedersen 2008](https://www.nationalbanken.dk/en/news-and-knowledge/publications-and-speeches/archive-publications/2008/the-chilean-fiscal-rule)). For oil producers the analogous mechanism uses a long-run reference oil price; Chile's copper-based system shows how the reference price is typically operationalized. Chile's long-run copper price and trend output are set by "panels of independent experts, who meet once a year," while the molybdenum reference price is "determined as the average of past prices"; delegating these key parameters to independent experts separates the estimates from short-term political pressure ([Danmarks Nationalbank, Pedersen 2008](https://www.nationalbanken.dk/en/news-and-knowledge/publications-and-speeches/archive-publications/2008/the-chilean-fiscal-rule)).

**How it differs from the PIH and bird-in-hand rules.** Those rules are *wealth-based*: they ask how much of total oil wealth—underground plus above-ground—can be spent sustainably. The structural/non-oil balance rule is a *flow-based* budget rule: it does not directly target wealth preservation, and intergenerational equity operates only indirectly through the anchor and any linked savings funds. Norway's statutory rule illustrates the overlap: it is formally a structural non-oil balance rule whose operational ceiling (the expected real return on the Government Pension Fund Global) makes it bird-in-hand-like in practice.

The structural rule also differs from a *simple balanced-budget rule*. A simple balanced-budget rule targets the overall balance, which includes resource revenue, so it is procyclical: it allows spending to rise in booms and forces cuts in busts. NRGI explicitly advises resource-dependent countries to "avoid overall budget balance rules; or just setting a debt ceiling as a percentage GDP," and instead to use "expenditure rules, non-resource balance or structural balance rules," with targets set in absolute numbers or as a percentage of non-resource GDP ([NRGI, Mihalyi & Fernandez](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/documents/firscal-rules-commodity-crash-summary.pdf)).

**Chile's benchmark design has three layers.** First, the 2001 structural surplus guideline committed the government to expenditure equal to structural revenue minus a surplus target ([Danmarks Nationalbank, Pedersen 2008](https://www.nationalbanken.dk/en/news-and-knowledge/publications-and-speeches/archive-publications/2008/the-chilean-fiscal-rule)). Second, the 2006 Fiscal Responsibility Law formalized the framework and created or regulated two sovereign wealth funds—the Pension Reserve Fund (FRP) and the Economic and Social Stabilization Fund (ESSF/FEES)—complemented by an informal defence fund ([WIDER](https://www.wider.unu.edu/publication/copper-sector-fiscal-rules-and-stabilization-funds-chile)). Third, the rule was embedded in a wider macro framework of a flexible exchange rate and an autonomous central bank ([WIDER](https://www.wider.unu.edu/publication/copper-sector-fiscal-rules-and-stabilization-funds-chile)). The ESSF was established on 6 March 2007 with an initial contribution of US$2.58 billion, mostly transferred from the old Copper Stabilization Fund; under the Fiscal Responsibility Law it receives "each year the positive balance resulting from the difference between the effective fiscal surplus and the contributions to the Pension Reserve Fund and to the Central Bank of Chile, discounting the payment of public debt and advances made the year before," and may finance fiscal deficits, the annual contribution to the FRP and debt amortization ([Chile Ministry of Finance](https://www.hacienda.cl/english/work-areas/international-finance/sovereign-wealth-funds/economic-and-social-stabilization-fund)). The rule and the funds are therefore complementary: the rule determines the surplus, and the funds allocate it between stabilization, pension liabilities and debt reduction.

**Other adopters and variation.** Within Latin America, Chile was long the only country targeting cyclically adjusted indicators, while Colombia was "also taking that approach" and Mexico's rule had some stabilization properties; Argentina, Brazil and Peru applied numerical rules targeting the overall or primary balance and/or public spending, and Venezuela's framework was "diluted" after introduction ([Berganza 2012, Banco de España](https://www.bde.es/f/webbde/SES/Secciones/Publicaciones/PublicacionesSeriadas/DocumentosOcasionales/12/Fich/do1208e.pdf)). Peru is notable for invoking "a well-defined and justified escape clause" during the 2015–16 commodity price crash, while Ecuador used "questionable practices to modify or disregard" its rules ([NRGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/documents/firscal-rules-commodity-crash-summary.pdf)). Mongolia has been reported to operate a structural deficit ceiling, but its design details could not be verified from accessible sources; this is a specific evidence gap.

**Institutional requirements.** The structural rule imposes heavier institutional demands than a simple balance rule: (1) independent expert committees or fiscal councils to estimate trend output and long-run prices (Chile's annual expert panels are the model); (2) a competent statistics office and methodology for cyclical adjustment; (3) a linked savings or stabilization fund with clear deposit and withdrawal rules; and (4) a well-specified escape clause. Oversight is critical: only about one-third of countries with fiscal rules have a national oversight organization monitoring compliance, most commonly supreme audit institutions, fiscal councils or parliamentary budget offices ([NRGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/documents/firscal-rules-commodity-crash-summary.pdf)). NRGI recommends that such bodies have independence, a legal mandate and resources, and that escape clauses explicitly include commodity shocks above a certain size, with operational guidance to prevent abuse.

**Critiques and performance.** The rule's weaknesses cluster around estimation and commitment. *Forecasting errors*: the structural balance depends on estimates of trend output and long-run prices, which are inherently uncertain and can bias the target ([Danmarks Nationalbank, Pedersen 2008](https://www.nationalbanken.dk/en/news-and-knowledge/publications-and-speeches/archive-publications/2008/the-chilean-fiscal-rule)). *Complexity*: it requires expert panels, models and data, and NRGI counsels that rules be "simple to calculate and easy to monitor and enforce, especially in countries where there is no established oversight actor with strong technical capacity" ([NRGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/documents/firscal-rules-commodity-crash-summary.pdf)). *Political manipulation of reference prices and methodology*: WIDER's review of Chile finds "frequent revisions in the methodology that affects the fiscal rule and the level of the structural balance by the authorities, thereby reducing its anchoring role on expectations and policy predictability" ([WIDER](https://www.wider.unu.edu/publication/copper-sector-fiscal-rules-and-stabilization-funds-chile)). *Procyclicality if poorly designed*: NRGI finds that in many resource-dependent countries the rules "allowed expenditures to rise pro-cyclically during the boom, and then forced abrupt fiscal adjustments" (e.g., Nigeria), and that funds often have "clear rules for accumulating resources at good times but no rules for using them at bad times." *Over-accumulation*: WIDER cautions against a "possible bias to over-accumulation of resources in SWFs without paying attention to the opportunity cost" of forgone social spending ([WIDER](https://www.wider.unu.edu/publication/copper-sector-fiscal-rules-and-stabilization-funds-chile)). On performance, Chile's framework is "associated (causality is another matter) with reasonably good macro outcomes," but the same review warns against "a blind endorsement of macro rules as the cornerstone for good macro management." Cross-country compliance is weak: in 2015–16, governments fully adhered to their rules in only six of 34 countries with rules (Botswana, Colombia, Indonesia, Liberia, Malaysia and Norway), while 25 suspended, modified or disregarded them ([NRGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/documents/firscal-rules-commodity-crash-summary.pdf)).

### 2.4 Comparing the three rules and their country applications

The organizing distinction among the three rules is *where the anchor sits*: PIH anchors spending to an estimated stock of total wealth, bird-in-hand anchors it to realized financial returns, and structural balance anchors it to a cyclically adjusted flow of non-resource revenue. This drives the trade-off between intergenerational saving (strongest under bird-in-hand and PIH) and macroeconomic stabilization (strongest under structural balance and bird-in-hand), and it explains the different institutional demands.

|---|---|---|---|
| Anchor | Permanent income from total oil wealth (NPV) | Realized real return on accumulated financial assets | Cyclically adjusted non-oil budget balance |
| Treatment of underground wealth | Counted in NPV; annuity spent | Ignored until extracted | Not a wealth rule; focuses on the budget flow |
| Treatment of resource revenue | Spent as part of permanent income | Spent as realized return | Excluded from the anchor; budget delinked from it |
| Stabilization logic | Intergenerational consumption smoothing | Spending limited to realized returns | Countercyclical: spending follows structural revenue |
| Intergenerational equity | Explicit (preserve real wealth) | Strong (preserve principal) | Indirect, via the anchor and linked funds |
| Key institutional demand | NPV, reserve and price estimates | Accumulated-asset accounts; realized-return measurement | Trend-output and long-run price estimates; expert panels; funds |
| Main critique | Over-optimistic return assumptions; absorption constraints | Underinvestment / oversaving | Forecasting errors; complexity; manipulation of reference prices |
| Representative cases | São Tomé and Príncipe; Timor-Leste (PIH-like) | Norway (bird-in-hand-like in practice) | Chile; Colombia |

**Determinants of country choice.** Optimal policy is country-specific: what suits a high-income, capital-abundant economy such as Norway or Kuwait is unlikely to suit a low-income, capital-scarce economy such as Ghana or Uganda ([van der Ploeg & Venables 2011](https://tonyvenables.weebly.com/uploads/1/1/5/4/115449811/harnessing2.pdf)). For capital-scarce economies, incremental consumption should be skewed toward present generations and savings directed to domestic private and public capital rather than foreign assets, departing from the standard PIH prescription. This creates a trade-off for low-capacity producers between the simplicity of a bird-in-hand or structural-balance rule and the development need to spend on domestic investment. Resource horizon and absorptive capacity reinforce the choice: a short horizon or weak project-appraisal capacity argues for spending smoothing and saving abroad, while a long horizon with strong institutions can support a more complex PIH-type rule.

**Interaction with NRFs.** Rules are rarely used in isolation. Norway combines a fiscal guideline with the Government Pension Fund Global: the guideline specifies the desired trajectory of the non-resource deficit financed by transfers from the fund, so fund and rule are two parts of one framework ([Ossowski 2013](https://webimages.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Fiscal-Rules-and-Resource-Funds-in-Nonrenewable-Resource-Exporting-Countries-International-Experience.pdf)). Chile pairs its structural balance rule with the ESSF stabilization fund and the FRP pension reserve fund. The non-resource primary balance is the corresponding sustainability indicator, because resource revenue is treated as financing—a transformation of assets from reserves in the ground into other assets—rather than as ordinary fiscal income ([Ossowski 2013](https://webimages.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Fiscal-Rules-and-Resource-Funds-in-Nonrenewable-Resource-Exporting-Countries-International-Experience.pdf)). Strong rules can reinforce fund governance but are not a substitute for it.

**Performance, failures and escapes.** The evidence is mixed and country-specific. Chile's structural balance rule was instrumental in saving a large part of the 2005–08 copper windfall, and government net assets rose above 20% of GDP in 2008; but during the 2009 recession the rule was "essentially abandoned" as authorities implemented a fiscal expansion beyond the rule's prescription—real spending rose 18% year-on-year, a fiscal impulse of 3% of GDP ([Engel et al.](https://www.eduardoengel.com/papers/66%20Engel_Neilson_Valdes_Vol17_393_425.pdf)). In a normative evaluation calibrated to Chile, the structural balance rule captures only 18% of the welfare gains of an optimal rule, a Friedman-type PIH rule 20%, and the best linear rule 74%; adding an escape clause improves outcomes ([Engel et al.](https://www.eduardoengel.com/papers/66%20Engel_Neilson_Valdes_Vol17_393_425.pdf)). This illustrates both the stabilization value of rules and their vulnerability to political pressure during downturns. At the cross-country level, NRGI finds that in many resource-dependent countries rules allowed expenditures to rise pro-cyclically during the boom and then forced abrupt fiscal adjustments, and that funds often lack rules for using resources in bad times ([NRGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/documents/firscal-rules-commodity-crash-summary.pdf); [WIDER](https://www.wider.unu.edu/publication/copper-sector-fiscal-rules-and-stabilization-funds-chile)).

**Related variants.** Beyond the three core rules, the fiscal-rules literature distinguishes four main types by the budgetary aggregate they constrain: budget balance, revenue, expenditure and debt rules. Budget-balance and debt rules have been the most prevalent among commodity exporters since the 1990s. Related sub-variants include the "golden rule" (balancing the current budget while allowing borrowing for investment) and constant or spending rules that cap the expenditure path. Debt ceilings are among the most common rule types globally and decentralized countries often impose debt limits on subnational governments to manage common-pool problems; they control the debt stock but do not directly address the flow of resource revenue into spending, and can be weakly binding—NRGI cites Liberia as a case where a debt ceiling "made compliance trivial" ([NRGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/documents/firscal-rules-commodity-crash-summary.pdf); [Berganza 2012](https://www.bde.es/f/webbde/SES/Secciones/Publicaciones/PublicacionesSeriadas/DocumentosOcasionales/12/Fich/do1208e.pdf)). These are complements to a resource-revenue anchor, not substitutes for it. **Evidence gap:** Davis, Ossowski and Fedelino (2003), Iacono (2017) and Bova et al. (2016) could not be verified in full, so claims about the average effect of fiscal rules on fiscal discipline should be treated as provisional.

## 3. Revenue Management Tools

Oil-dependent countries manage revenue through two complementary instruments. **Natural Resource Funds (NRFs)** determine how much revenue is saved, stabilized or invested abroad; **Public Investment Management (PIM) systems** determine whether the portion spent at home buys durable, high-return capital or is dissipated in white elephants and low-return construction. A fiscal rule sets the ceiling on spending, an NRF sets the saving/stabilization function, and PIM sets the return on domestic spending—the three are complements.

### 3.1 Natural Resource Funds: functions, types and governance

An NRF is "sovereign wealth funds financed by natural resource revenues" ([NRGI & Vale Columbia Center 2014](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_RWI_Complete_Report_EN.pdf)). The broader SWF category covers "special-purpose investment funds or arrangements that are owned by the government mainly for macroeconomic purposes" ([World Bank 2018](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/908941526042996775/pdf/125967-OUO-9-Note-on-stabilization-funds-04-13-2018-FINAL-for-Print.pdf), citing the International Working Group of Sovereign Wealth Funds). Three distinctions matter. **NRF vs. central bank reserves:** reserves are held for balance-of-payments and monetary purposes and managed for liquidity, whereas NRFs are separate vehicles with explicit stabilization or savings mandates ([World Bank 2018](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/908941526042996775/pdf/125967-OUO-9-Note-on-stabilization-funds-04-13-2018-FINAL-for-Print.pdf)). **NRF vs. public pension funds:** pension reserve funds are earmarked for future public pension liabilities and classified separately ([Sharma 2017, UN background paper](https://www.un.org/esa/ffd/high-level-conference-on-ffd-and-2030-agenda/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2017/11/Background-Paper_Sovereign-Wealth-Funds.pdf)). **NRF vs. stabilization accounts:** a treasury or central-bank stabilization account may be a simple liquidity buffer, whereas an NRF stabilization fund is a distinct institution with an investment policy and, typically, a reference-price or revenue rule ([Davis, Ossowski, Daniel & Barnett 2001, IMF OP 205, ch. 3](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/ch03.xml)).

The core purpose is to address two structural features of resource dependence—revenue volatility and exhaustibility. The policy literature identifies three main functions: (1) **budget stabilization**—smoothing expenditure when commodity revenues fall; (2) **intergenerational savings**—preserving wealth for future generations; and (3) **development/strategic investment**—channeling resources into development projects or strategic assets ([World Bank 2018](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/908941526042996775/pdf/125967-OUO-9-Note-on-stabilization-funds-04-13-2018-FINAL-for-Print.pdf); [Sharma 2017](https://www.un.org/esa/ffd/high-level-conference-on-ffd-and-2030-agenda/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2017/11/Background-Paper_Sovereign-Wealth-Funds.pdf)). These map onto three fund types, with pension reserve funds and hybrid/multiple-objective funds as variants.

| Type | Primary objective | Typical horizon | Investment / risk profile | Withdrawal rule | Representative funds |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Savings / intergenerational** | Preserve resource wealth for future generations; earn long-run real returns | Long (decades) | Higher risk tolerance; equities and fixed income, with alternatives over time | Realized real return, or discretionary transfers subject to parliamentary approval | Norway GPFG; Kuwait RFFG; Alaska Permanent Fund; Timor-Leste Petroleum Fund; Nigeria Future Generations Fund ([OP 205](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/ch03.xml); [World Bank 2009](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/791151468015000339/pdf/479900BRI00ENG10Box338877B01PUBLIC1.pdf); [IFSWF](https://www.ifswf.org/what-is-a-sovereign-wealth-fund/multiple-objectives-funds)) |
| **Stabilization** | Smooth budget revenue volatility; finance counter-cyclical spending | Short (1–3 years) | Conservative; liquid money-market and sovereign bonds | Contingent on a reference price or revenue; transfers to the budget | Chile ESSF; Chile Copper Stabilization Fund; Oman SGRF; PNG MRSF ([IFSWF](https://www.ifswf.org/what-is-a-sovereign-wealth-fund/stabilisation-funds); [OP 205](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/ch03.xml)) |
| **Development / strategic** | Finance development projects and strategic investment | Medium–long | Flexible; domestic projects, private equity, foreign securities | Discretionary or project-based, typically via the budget | Oman Oil Fund; Azerbaijan SOFAZ; Nigeria Infrastructure Fund ([OP 205](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/ch03.xml); [World Bank 2009](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/791151468015000339/pdf/479900BRI00ENG10Box338877B01PUBLIC1.pdf); [IFSWF](https://www.ifswf.org/what-is-a-sovereign-wealth-fund/multiple-objectives-funds)) |
| **Pension reserve** *(variant)* | Prefund future public pension liabilities | Long | Similar to savings funds | Linked to pension deficits/liabilities | Chile Pension Reserve Fund ([IFSWF](https://www.ifswf.org/what-is-a-sovereign-wealth-fund/stabilisation-funds)) |
| **Hybrid / multiple objective** *(variant)* | Combine stabilization, savings and development | Mixed | Mixed; often ring-fenced sub-portfolios | Mixed | Botswana Pula Fund; Azerbaijan SOFAZ; Nigeria NSIA ([World Bank 2018](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/908941526042996775/pdf/125967-OUO-9-Note-on-stabilization-funds-04-13-2018-FINAL-for-Print.pdf); [IFSWF](https://www.ifswf.org/what-is-a-sovereign-wealth-fund/multiple-objectives-funds)) |

The three types differ systematically in deposit rules, withdrawal rules, horizon, risk tolerance and asset allocation. OP 205 records that savings funds use accumulation rules tied to a share of resource revenue (Alaska's 50% of certain mineral revenues; Kuwait's 10% of all government revenue) and preserve principal, whereas stabilization funds accumulate according to a reference price or revenue rule and release assets to the budget when receipts fall short ([OP 205, Table 3.1](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/ch03.xml)). The UN classification notes that stabilization funds "have a more conservative risk appetite and therefore are usually restricted to lower-risk, passive investments," while savings, pension reserve and reserve investment funds "may have longer-term liabilities and more flexibility to invest in illiquid, higher-risk and longer-term assets"; development funds show "a greater proportion of private market investment" ([Sharma 2017](https://www.un.org/esa/ffd/high-level-conference-on-ffd-and-2030-agenda/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2017/11/Background-Paper_Sovereign-Wealth-Funds.pdf)). The IFSWF likewise observes that stabilization funds "tend to hold a large proportion of their assets in liquid investments so that they have access to capital at short notice" ([IFSWF](https://www.ifswf.org/what-is-a-sovereign-wealth-fund/stabilisation-funds)).

**Do the functions conflict?** Yes, potentially: stabilization requires liquidity and low risk; savings requires a long horizon and higher risk; development requires domestic investment, which can conflict with the sterilization objective of investing abroad. Hybrid funds are "particularly common in developing economies in sub-Saharan Africa," and "locking away capital for future generations is clearly inappropriate for countries with high levels of poverty or pressing infrastructure-development needs" ([IFSWF](https://www.ifswf.org/what-is-a-sovereign-wealth-fund/multiple-objectives-funds)). This tension explains why many funds adopt ring-fenced sub-portfolios rather than a single mandate.

**Governance requirements.** The literature converges on a clear conclusion: the *existence* of an NRF matters far less than its *governance*. IMF OP 205 found that in several countries fund rules "have been bypassed or changed and they do not themselves seem to have effectively constrained spending," and that "the integration of the fund's operations with overall fiscal policy has often proven problematic" ([Davis, Ossowski, Daniel & Barnett, IMF OP 205, 2001](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/9781589060197.xml)). Effective governance rests on the following interlocking elements.

| Governance element | Operational requirement | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Statutory objective | Purpose defined in law (stabilization, savings, development) | Determines deposit/withdrawal rules and investment horizon ([Santiago Principles, IWG 2008](https://www.ifswf.org/sites/default/files/santiagoprinciples_0_0.pdf); [IMF OP 205](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/9781589060197.xml)) |
| Deposit rule | Explicit inflow formula (share of revenue or excess reserves) | Prevents discretionary, pro-cyclical accumulation ([World Bank 2018](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/908941526042996775/pdf/125967-OUO-9-Note-on-stabilization-funds-04-13-2018-FINAL-for-Print.pdf)) |
| Withdrawal rule | Explicit conditions/ceilings for outflows | Turns objective into a spending constraint; protects principal ([IMF OP 205](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/9781589060197.xml)) |
| Investment policy / strategic asset allocation | Mandate, permitted assets, risk limits, benchmark | Matches portfolio to horizon; controls risk ([Santiago Principles 2008](https://www.ifswf.org/sites/default/files/santiagoprinciples_0_0.pdf); [Truman 2007](https://www.piie.com/sites/default/files/publications/papers/truman1007swf.pdf)) |
| Separation of ownership, management, oversight | Owner, operational manager and oversight body distinct | Checks and balances; curbs political interference ([World Bank 2018](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/908941526042996775/pdf/125967-OUO-9-Note-on-stabilization-funds-04-13-2018-FINAL-for-Print.pdf)) |
| Transparency | Published audits, holdings, returns, transactions | Enables accountability; reduces corruption ([Linaburg-Maduell Transparency Index](https://www.swfinstitute.org/research/linaburg-maduell-transparency-index); [Truman 2007](https://www.piie.com/sites/default/files/publications/papers/truman1007swf.pdf)) |
| Accountability to parliament/public | Legislative reporting, external audit, public disclosure | Democratic control of resource wealth ([Truman 2007](https://www.piie.com/sites/default/files/publications/papers/truman1007swf.pdf); [NRGI 2017 RGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/publications/2017-resource-governance-index)) |
| Ethical/responsible investment | Ethical guidelines, negative screening, active ownership | Prevents politically motivated or harmful holdings ([Truman 2007](https://www.piie.com/sites/default/files/publications/papers/truman1007swf.pdf)) |

Three voluntary benchmarks define "good" governance in operational terms. The **Santiago Principles** (IWG, October 2008) comprise 24 voluntary principles covering legal framework, objectives and coordination with macroeconomic policies; institutional framework and governance structure; and investment and risk-management framework, underpinned by four objectives including investing on economic and financial risk-return grounds and maintaining "a transparent and sound governance structure" ([Santiago Principles, IWG 2008](https://www.ifswf.org/sites/default/files/santiagoprinciples_0_0.pdf); [World Bank 2018](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/908941526042996775/pdf/125967-OUO-9-Note-on-stabilization-funds-04-13-2018-FINAL-for-Print.pdf)). The **Truman SWF scoreboard**, first developed in 2007, evaluates funds against structure, governance, transparency and accountability, and behaviour; its "fiscal treatment" sub-category scores whether funding sources are specified, whether use of principal and earnings is clear, whether these are integrated with the budget, and whether guidelines are followed without frequent adjustment ([Truman 2007](https://www.piie.com/sites/default/files/publications/papers/truman1007swf.pdf)). The **Linaburg-Maduell Transparency Index** rates funds on a 10-point scale, with ten transparency principles each adding one point; the SWFI recommends a minimum score of 8 to claim adequate transparency ([Linaburg-Maduell Transparency Index, SWFI](https://www.swfinstitute.org/research/linaburg-maduell-transparency-index)). A complementary diagnostic is the **NRGI Resource Governance Index**, whose 2017 edition measured resource governance in 81 countries producing 82% of the world's oil and 78% of its gas, capturing the wider governance environment within which NRFs operate ([NRGI 2017 RGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/publications/2017-resource-governance-index)).

**Deposit and withdrawal rules as operational constraints.** Botswana's Pula Fund (established 1994) operates two accounts—an excess-reserves account owned by the Bank of Botswana (BoB) and a Government Investment Account owned by the Ministry of Finance and Development Planning—with two concurrent deposit rules, a BoB Board that sets general investment policy but must consult the Ministry on strategic decisions, and a dual savings-and-short-term-stabilization mandate ([World Bank 2018](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/908941526042996775/pdf/125967-OUO-9-Note-on-stabilization-funds-04-13-2018-FINAL-for-Print.pdf)). This "dual-key" arrangement is a concrete separation-of-powers model. Libya's Investment Authority shows a more formalized variant: under Law No. 13 of 2010 it has a Board of Trustees chaired by the Prime Minister and a seven-member Board of Directors with three sub-committees (nomination and compensation, governance, and audit and risk) ([Libyan Investment Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libyan_Investment_Authority)). Yet OP 205's cross-country evaluation warns that such rules are often weak in practice, and that a rule on paper does not become an operational constraint unless it is embedded in the budget process, enforced by an oversight body and supported by transparency ([IMF OP 205, 2001](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/9781589060197.xml)).

**Common failure modes.**

| Failure mode | Mechanism | Country example | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opacity / weak disclosure | Operations, holdings and returns not published; no external audit | Libya, Equatorial Guinea and Qatar ranked among the lowest-scoring funds on the 2012 SWF scoreboard | [Bagnall & Truman, PB13-19, 2013](https://ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu/pbei/iie/0028994/f_0028994_23542.pdf) |
| Political interference | Fund controlled directly by the head of state; decisions bypass institutions | Azerbaijan's SOFAZ under direct presidential control | [Yücesoy 2013](https://ojs.library.carleton.ca/index.php/rera/article/view/223/160) |

The Azerbaijan case is instructive: SOFAZ was founded in 1999 with stated goals of reducing oil dependence and preserving wealth, yet direct presidential control and the absence of civil-society consultation made corruption easier, and oil money was repeatedly used for salary increases, pensions and debt payments rather than diversification—leaving the country *more* oil-dependent than before the fund's creation ([Yücesoy 2013](https://ojs.library.carleton.ca/index.php/rera/article/view/223/160)). The Kuwait and Libya episodes show that even long-established funds with formal governance structures can suffer large losses when investment mandates and oversight are weak ([Kuwait Investment Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuwait_Investment_Authority); [Libyan Investment Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libyan_Investment_Authority)).

**Transparency in practice remains uneven.** On the 2012 scoreboard, the highest-scoring funds were those of Norway, New Zealand, Chile, the United States (Alaska) and Ireland, while the lowest scorers included Qatar, Libya and Equatorial Guinea—three of the five lowest-ranking funds were nonetheless IFSWF members, indicating that membership in the Santiago Principles forum does not guarantee adherence ([Bagnall & Truman, PB13-19, 2013](https://ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu/pbei/iie/0028994/f_0028994_23542.pdf)). The NRGI RGI confirms that the wider governance environment—not just fund-level disclosure—is the binding constraint in most producing countries ([NRGI 2017 RGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/publications/2017-resource-governance-index)).

**Performance and interaction with the fiscal rule.** The evidence on whether NRFs change fiscal behavior is mixed. IMF OP 205's econometric comparison found that, following a 50% increase in nonrenewable-resource prices, government expenditure *fell* in Chile (−6.2%), Kuwait (−0.2%) and Norway (−1.7%), suggesting counter-cyclical stabilization, but *rose* in Oman (+24.2%) and Papua New Guinea (+3.0%), and also rose in most countries without funds (e.g., Venezuela +10.3%, Saudi Arabia +11.5%) ([IMF OP 205, ch. V](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589060197/ch05.xml)). NRGI's study of 22 funds found that funds "have benefited citizens in a number of countries and subnational jurisdictions, as in Chile, Norway, some Persian Gulf countries and several U.S. states," yet about half were too opaque to study comprehensively, and some were "created simply to act as slush funds for ruling regimes, serving as channels for patronage and corruption" ([NRGI & Vale Columbia Center 2014](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_RWI_Complete_Report_EN.pdf)). A World Bank review concluded that "petroleum funds can be effective under the right circumstances, but they can also contribute to, rather than mitigate, the resource curse" ([World Bank 2009](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/791151468015000339/pdf/479900BRI00ENG10Box338877B01PUBLIC1.pdf)). The fiscal rule and the fund are complements, not substitutes: a strong rule can give a well-governed fund a credible spending ceiling, but it cannot rescue a fund whose governance is captured by political interests. **Evidence gap:** Kazakhstan's National Fund and Ghana's Petroleum Funds are frequently cited but their design details could not be verified against primary sources; the NRGI fund census and the IMF OP 205 landing page were also inaccessible, so figures rest on the OP 205 chapter text, World Bank and IFSWF case material, and the NRGI/Vale Columbia study.

### 3.2 Public Investment Management Systems: rationale and stages

A Public Investment Management (PIM) system is the institutional machinery that converts oil revenue into productive capital. For oil-dependent countries it is arguably as consequential as the fiscal rule or the NRF: the fiscal rule determines how much revenue may be spent, and an NRF determines how much is saved or parked abroad, but PIM determines whether the portion spent at home buys durable, high-return assets or is dissipated in **white elephants**—completed but underused, overpriced or abandoned projects—and in low-return construction that worsens Dutch disease. The IMF estimates that the average country loses about 30% of the value of its public investment to inefficiencies in PIM processes, and that the most efficient investors obtain roughly twice the growth "bang" for their investment "buck" than the least efficient ([IMF, *Public Investment Management Assessment: Review and Update*, May 2018](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Knowledge-Hub/Publications/pubdocuments/PublicInvestmentManagementAssessment_Review&Update_May2018.pdf)). For oil exporters, weak PIM destroys wealth on two fronts: it lowers the return on the financial surplus and, by channelling spending into non-tradables, intensifies real-exchange-rate overvaluation and de-industrialization.

A resource windfall creates a large, temporary surge in revenue and intense political pressure to spend it quickly. Without strong PIM, projects are selected for political rather than economic reasons; feasibility and cost-benefit analysis are weak; costs escalate during implementation; procurement is corrupted; and spending concentrates in non-tradable construction and services, raising the real exchange rate and crowding out manufacturing. Dabla-Norris et al. (2011) construct an index of public investment efficiency across four stages—appraisal, selection, implementation and evaluation—for 71 countries (40 of them low-income), and find that "weaknesses in public investment management have resulted in inadequate returns to public and private investment in many low-income countries," with low returns arising from "poor selection and implementation of projects due to limited information, waste and leakage of resources, and weak technical expertise." Critically, they warn that "a substantial scaling-up of public investment in a weak institutional environment runs the risk of potentially undermining its growth benefits as well as prospects for fiscal and debt sustainability" ([Dabla-Norris et al., IMF Working Paper 11/37, 2011](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/downloadpdf/view/journals/001/2011/037/article-A001-en.pdf)). Berg et al. (2013) formalize the oil-specific trade-off in a dynamic stochastic small-open-economy model: investing natural-resource revenues at home can raise productive capacity, but only if public investment is efficient and if the economy can absorb the investment without triggering Dutch disease; where **absorptive capacity** is limited and investment inefficient, the macroeconomic gains are small while the real-exchange-rate costs are large ([Berg, Portillo, Yang & Zanna, *IMF Economic Review* 61(1), 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/imfer.2013.1)).

**The seven-stage PIM cycle.** An effective system covers the full project life cycle, from identification through operation and maintenance, with feedback to future planning. The World Bank's *Public Investment Management Reference Guide* (2020) and Rajaram et al.'s (2010) diagnostic framework are the standard reference points; the IMF's PIMA operationalizes the same functions ([World Bank, *Public Investment Management Reference Guide*, 2020](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/99c14858-9bf8-53e3-8fa3-67bd9b46610f); [Rajaram et al., *A Diagnostic Framework for Assessing Public Investment Management*, World Bank, 2010](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/f5a8025c-e0d7-580f-be96-7b5cba4ea2f7)).

| Stage | Key activities | Primary responsible institutions | Common failure points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Identification & needs assessment | Identify infrastructure and service gaps; align with national/sector strategies; preliminary screening | Line ministries; planning ministry; Ministry of Finance | Politically driven project lists; no systematic needs assessment; unfunded pipeline |
| 2. Preparation & appraisal | Feasibility studies; **cost-benefit analysis**; financial and economic appraisal; risk assessment | Line ministries (project owners); central PIM unit/MoF (methodology); external consultants | Weak or optimistic appraisal; no standard methodology; risks ignored |
| 3. Selection & prioritization / portfolio management | Central review of appraisals; apply published selection criteria; maintain an appraised pipeline; portfolio oversight | MoF or central PIM unit; cabinet/parliament; planning ministry | Non-transparent criteria; selection on political grounds; projects not compared against one another |
| 4. Budgeting & financing | Multiyear budgeting; appropriation of total project outlays; reliable cash-flow forecasts; integration of capital and recurrent budgets | MoF; parliament; line ministries | Off-budget spending; virement from capital to current; funding not secured |
| 5. Implementation & procurement | Project management; open and transparent procurement; contract management; physical monitoring | Line ministries; procurement agency; MoF | Cost escalation; delays; procurement corruption; weak project management |
| 6. Monitoring & evaluation | Physical and financial monitoring; ex post reviews; audit; adjustment of procedures | MoF; supreme audit institution; line ministries | No ex post evaluation; monitoring data unused; audit findings not followed up |
| 7. Operation, maintenance & asset management | Asset registers; routine maintenance funding; major improvements; depreciation accounting | Line ministries; MoF | Neglected maintenance; no asset register; assets deteriorate |

The stages are not purely sequential: stage 7 feeds back into stages 1 and 2, and stage 6 (ex post review) should inform future appraisal methodology and selection criteria. The World Bank's independent evaluation arm found that PIM reform support was "necessary" but "not sufficient" on its own: meaningful results also required investment in PIM offices and institutional capacity, linking public investment programmes with budgets and project selection, ex ante and ex post evaluation tools and methodologies, and project-management and monitoring manuals—plus careful sequencing ([IEG, *Building Public Investment Management Capacity*](https://ieg.worldbankgroup.org/evaluations/public-financial-and-debt-management/chapter-4-building-public-investment-management)).

**The IMF PIMA: three phases and 15 institutions.** The IMF's Public Investment Management Assessment evaluates **15 institutions across three key stages**—planning of sustainable investment, allocation of investment to the right sectors and projects, and implementation of projects to deliver productive and durable public assets. Each institution is assessed on institutional strength (design, or "de jure") and effectiveness ("de facto"), and three cross-cutting factors—the legal and regulatory framework, staff capacity and IT systems—condition overall performance ([IMF, "What is PIMA"](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/PIMA/Home/PimaTool/What-is-PIMA.html); [IMF 2018](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Knowledge-Hub/Publications/pubdocuments/PublicInvestmentManagementAssessment_Review&Update_May2018.pdf)). A consistent finding across assessments is that countries score higher on institutional design than on effectiveness—rules exist on paper that are not applied in practice ([IMF 2018](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Knowledge-Hub/Publications/pubdocuments/PublicInvestmentManagementAssessment_Review&Update_May2018.pdf)).

| IMF PIMA phase | PIMA institutions (5 per phase) | Corresponding PIM stage(s) |
|---|---|---|
| **Planning** | 1. Fiscal Targets and Rules; 2. National and Sectoral Planning; 3. Coordinating Between Entities; 4. Project Appraisal; 5. Alternative Infrastructure Financing | 1. Identification & needs assessment; 2. Preparation & appraisal |
| **Allocation** | 6. Multiyear Budgeting; 7. Budget Comprehensiveness and Unity; 8. Budgeting for Investment; 9. Maintenance and Funding; 10. Project Selection | 3. Selection & prioritization / portfolio management; 4. Budgeting & financing (institution 9 also feeds stage 7) |
| **Implementation** | 11. Procurement; 12. Availability of Funding; 13. Portfolio Management and Oversight; 14. Management Project Implementation; 15. Monitoring of Public Assets | 5. Implementation & procurement; 6. Monitoring & evaluation; 7. Operation, maintenance & asset management |

The mapping is approximate because several PIMA institutions span more than one stage: "Maintenance and Funding" sits in the allocation phase but directly serves the operation-and-maintenance stage, and "Monitoring of Public Assets" sits in the implementation phase but supports monitoring and evaluation. The resource-rich application follows the same logic; the IMF has applied PIMA-type analysis to oil exporters (a November 2014 report, *Making the Most of Public Investment in MENA and CCA Oil-Exporting Countries*, is listed in the IMF's capacity-development resources), and the framework's fiscal-targets-and-rules and multiyear-budgeting institutions directly address the revenue volatility and absorptive-capacity problems that oil windfalls create ([IMF Institute for Capacity Development / Middle East and Central Asia, Resources](https://www.imfigeur.org/content/IGEUR/Home/Resources.html)). The published PIMA structure does not contain a separately branded "resource-rich module"; resource-rich features are captured through the standard 15 institutions. This is an evidence gap: the operational guidance oil exporters most need—how to calibrate the investment rate to absorptive capacity—is implied by the framework rather than codified in a distinct module.

**Institutional arrangements.** An effective PIM system requires a clear division of labour. The **Ministry of Finance or a central PIM unit** sets the appraisal methodology, conducts the central review of major project appraisals before budget inclusion, publishes selection criteria and maintains the project pipeline. **Line ministries** act as project owners for identification, preparation and implementation. The **planning ministry** prepares national and sectoral strategies. **Parliament** appropriates total project outlays and provides oversight. The **supreme audit institution** conducts ex post audits, and a **procurement agency** runs open and transparent procurement. These arrangements rest on three supporting pillars that PIMA treats as cross-cutting: a legal and regulatory framework, staff capacity and IT systems ([IMF, "What is PIMA"](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/PIMA/Home/PimaTool/What-is-PIMA.html)). The World Bank's Infrastructure Prioritization Framework provides a methodology for ranking projects against strategic objectives, costs and risks during selection ([World Bank, *Infrastructure Prioritization Framework*](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/844631461874662700-0100022016/original/160423InfrastructurePrioritizationFrameworkFinalVersion.pdf)).

**Links to fiscal rules and NRFs.** PIM, fiscal rules and NRFs are complements. A strong fiscal rule without strong PIM can still leak money through white elephants and low-return projects; a strong PIM without a fiscal rule can be overwhelmed by boom-time spending pressure and absorptive-capacity limits. Berg et al. (2013) make the interaction explicit: if public investment is inefficient and absorptive capacity is constrained, saving revenue abroad (as under a bird-in-hand or PIH-type rule) can dominate domestic investment; conversely, improving PIM efficiency shifts the trade-off toward domestic investment because each dollar spent at home yields a higher return ([Berg et al. 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/imfer.2013.1)).

**Country evidence.** Chile's National Investment System (*Sistema Nacional de Inversiones*, SNI), established in 1975 and jointly managed by the Ministry of Planning and the Ministry of Finance, is the earliest and most consolidated investment-appraisal system in Latin America; its objective is to improve the quality of public investment by selecting projects with the largest social net present value, and the IDB describes it as "a model of consistent and transparent investment appraisal" ([Gómez-Lobo 2012](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/jbcacn/v3y2012i1n1.html); [IDB, *Public Investment for Sustainable Development in Chile*](https://publications.iadb.org/en/public-investment-sustainable-development-chile-building-national-investment-system); [World Bank, *Appraisal of Public Investment: Chile*](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/666151468216003237/pdf/406180CL0Publi1b1OFFICIAL0USE0ONLY1.pdf)). Timor-Leste separates petroleum revenue inflows from government spending through its Petroleum Fund and has received World Bank support for PIM capacity in a post-conflict setting ([Timor-Leste Petroleum Fund Law](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Timor-Leste-Petroleum-Fund-Law.pdf); [NRGI, *Investing for the Future: Timor-Leste's Petroleum Fund*](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/documents/investing-for-the-future-timor-lestes-petroleum-fund.pdf); [World Bank, *Timor-Leste: Public Investment Management from Post-Conflict*](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/0a696e7f-c7b6-5e0f-a678-eed4f67a7721)). Ghana's petroleum revenue management framework has been supported by World Bank prior actions for "the adoption of a methodology and a legal framework to appraise, select, and approve projects above a defined monetary value" ([IEG, *Building Public Investment Management Capacity*](https://ieg.worldbankgroup.org/evaluations/public-financial-and-debt-management/chapter-4-building-public-investment-management)). Botswana's Pula Fund is funded by mineral export earnings and managed by the Bank of Botswana; Botswana's PIM system is generally regarded as stronger than the regional average, although public documentation of its stages is thinner than Chile's ([NRGI, *Botswana* country profile, 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Botswana_July2013.pdf)). **Evidence gaps:** the full texts of the World Bank's 2020 *PIM Reference Guide* and Rajaram et al.'s 2010 diagnostic framework were not retrievable, so the seven-stage cycle is presented from the IMF's operationalization of the same functions, the World Bank IEG's summary, and secondary descriptions; the exact numbering of the World Bank's must-haves should be verified against the original documents. Berg et al.'s numerical results were not independently verified. The evidence on whether PIM reform actually improves outcomes is suggestive rather than definitive: the World Bank's IEG found that PIM support was necessary but not sufficient, and that results depended on complementary investment in capacity, systems and sequencing.

## 4. Benchmark Case Study: Norway

Norway's oil wealth framework—a structural non-oil fiscal rule paired with a large, transparent, externally invested sovereign wealth fund—achieved its core objectives: it delinked government spending from volatile petroleum revenue, avoided the Dutch disease that afflicted many oil producers, preserved substantial intergenerational savings, and maintained strong institutions and fiscal discipline. The literature is nevertheless clear that the model is not directly transferable: its success depended on a conjunction of preconditions (high income, capital abundance, strong institutions) that most low-capacity producers lack, and the permanent-income/bird-in-hand prescription that Norway approximates is welfare-reducing for capital-scarce economies.

### 4.1 Norway's fiscal rule: the 4% benchmark and its operation

Norway's fiscal rule, adopted in 2001, is a **structural non-oil balance rule**: the central government's cyclically adjusted non-oil budget deficit may not exceed the expected real return on the Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG). The return assumption was set at **4%** at inception and reduced to **3%** in 2017. Because all petroleum revenues are transferred into the GPFG and invested abroad, the rule operates as a spending ceiling that phases only the expected return into the budget, leaving the principal—and any excess returns—saved for future generations. In operation it is bird-in-hand-*like*: it spends the expected return on already-accumulated financial assets rather than on estimated underground wealth.

Norway's framework evolved in response to the 1969 North Sea oil discovery and the recognition that petroleum revenues were finite, volatile and potentially damaging to the non-oil economy.

The statutory core is a structural non-oil balance rule. The reference variable is the central government's budget deficit *excluding petroleum revenues*, adjusted for the position in the business cycle; the ceiling is the expected long-run real return on the GPFG's assets. In formula form:

> **Structural non-oil budget deficit ≤ r\* × W<sub>GPFG</sub>**, where r\* = expected real return on the GPFG (4% from 2001 to 2017; 3% from 2017), and W<sub>GPFG</sub> = the fund's value.

Three operational principles make the rule function as a wealth-preserving spending ceiling ([Norges Bank 2010](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/news/Speeches/2010/02-11-2010-Perspectives-on-managing/)): (1) **all petroleum revenues are transferred to the fund**, consisting of the net cash flow from petroleum activities plus the return on the fund's own capital ([Government Pension Fund Act, sections 2–3](https://natlex.ilo.org/dyn/natlex2/natlex2/files/download/73096/NOR73096%20ENG.pdf)); (2) **only the expected real return is phased into the budget**, with petroleum revenues above the benchmark remaining in the fund and invested abroad, and fund capital usable only for transfers to the central government budget pursuant to a resolution by the Storting ([Government Pension Fund Act, section 5](https://natlex.ilo.org/dyn/natlex2/natlex2/files/download/73096/NOR73096%20ENG.pdf)); and (3) **the capital is invested abroad**, since fund assets may not be invested in Norway ([Norges Bank 2010](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/news/Speeches/2010/02-11-2010-Perspectives-on-managing/); [NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). The institutional division of labour is: the **Ministry of Finance** owns the fund on behalf of the Norwegian people and sets the investment mandate; **Norges Bank Investment Management (NBIM)** manages the assets operationally; and the **Storting** approves the budget and all withdrawals, with the structural non-oil deficit derived from data produced by **Statistics Norway** ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf); [Government Pension Fund Act](https://natlex.ilo.org/dyn/natlex2/natlex2/files/download/73096/NOR73096%20ENG.pdf)).

In 2017 the Ministry of Finance reduced the expected real-return assumption from 4% to 3%, tightening the spending ceiling, reflecting a downward reassessment of the fund's long-run expected real return. The Norges Bank Governor acknowledged in 2018 that some observers judged the expected-return estimates optimistic, making the assumption and its periodic review a focal point of debate ([BIS 2018](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20180327-does-norway-save-too-much); [IMF 2017](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2017/182/article-A001-en.xml)).

**Effectiveness.** The evidence supports the rule's effectiveness as a stabilization and savings device, though rigorous causal attribution is limited. The fund "has acted as a buffer between widely fluctuating oil revenues and public expenditure," allowing the annual spending decision to be made independently of petroleum revenues; capital outflows also stabilize the krone when oil revenues rise ([Norges Bank 2010](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/news/Speeches/2010/02-11-2010-Perspectives-on-managing/)). By 2010 the fund had reached NOK 3 trillion (about 120% of GDP), of which NOK 600 billion were returns in foreign currency—enough to fund roughly NOK 120 billion of annual spending without eroding real value ([Norges Bank 2010](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/news/Speeches/2010/02-11-2010-Perspectives-on-managing/)). By investing abroad and phasing in only the expected return, the framework was designed to shield the non-oil economy from the spending and resource-movement effects identified by Corden and Neary; the IMF reported that the 2013–15 krone depreciation and low wage growth had "helped reverse some of the long-term deterioration in Norway's cost competitiveness," with real effective exchange rates around their two-decade averages ([IMF 2017](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2017/182/article-A001-en.xml)). The OECD and IMF have commended the framework, and Norway is widely treated as having escaped the resource curse ([BIS 2018](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20180327-does-norway-save-too-much); [Norges Bank 2010](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/news/Speeches/2010/02-11-2010-Perspectives-on-managing/)). **Criticisms and limitations.** Because the ceiling is defined as an average over the cycle, individual years can deviate; the 2010 Norges Bank speech observed that the budget bill had called for spending *below* 4% only once before 2007, indicating the ceiling was generally binding at or near its limit rather than used counter-cyclically in booms ([Norges Bank 2010](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/news/Speeches/2010/02-11-2010-Perspectives-on-managing/)). During the 2014–16 oil price fall, mainland growth slowed to 0.9% in 2016 and non-oil exports declined, illustrating exposure of the wider economy to petroleum cycles ([IMF 2017](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2017/182/article-A001-en.xml)). The rule also depends on estimating the cyclical position and on an expected-return assumption that must be periodically revised, and there is ongoing debate about whether spending is too high given future pension liabilities ([BIS 2018](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20180327-does-norway-save-too-much)). Some economists argue the rule is excessively conservative—Gagnon (2018) contended that Norway "has saved too much" and that spending should follow a modified permanent-income principle ([BIS 2018](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20180327-does-norway-save-too-much)). Analytically, Norway's rule is close to the bird-in-hand rule because it spends only the expected return on accumulated financial assets; the Norges Bank Governor stated explicitly that "the Norwegian fiscal rule implies a more cautious spending path than the permanent income rule" because "the government spends the expected return on its current financial assets" ([BIS 2018](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20180327-does-norway-save-too-much)).

### 4.2 GPFG governance, investment mandate, and institutional arrangement

The GPFG is run through a layered, legally anchored architecture that deliberately separates ownership, management and oversight and subjects the fund to unusually strong transparency and ethical constraints. At end-2020 the fund was worth **NOK 10,914 billion** and held roughly **1.5% of all listed shares globally** ([Norges Bank Annual Report 2020](https://www.norges-bank.no/contentassets/4f5b42f6218843968c078c34dfd5d3e6/annual-report-2020.pdf?v=24022021181045&ft=.pdf); [NBIM, "About the fund"](https://www.nbim.no/en/about-us/about-the-fund/)).

This separation is the core of the model: the owner sets the rules, a professionally managed central-bank unit executes them, and multiple independent bodies audit and supervise. The fund is fully integrated into the government's annual budget, and parliament must approve all withdrawals ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). A new Central Bank Act that entered into force on 1 January 2020 reorganized Norges Bank's governance, while the Executive Board retained responsibility for GPFG management ([Norges Bank Annual Report 2020](https://www.norges-bank.no/contentassets/4f5b42f6218843968c078c34dfd5d3e6/annual-report-2020.pdf?v=24022021181045&ft=.pdf)).

**Investment mandate, strategic benchmark and asset allocation.** The Ministry of Finance delegates operational management to Norges Bank within a mandate covering investment guidelines, ethical management, risk management and internal control; the equity portfolio is benchmarked against the FTSE Global All Cap Index and fixed income against a corresponding bond benchmark ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). The statutory objective is to achieve the highest possible return—"dependent upon sustainable development in economic, environmental and social terms as well as well-functioning, legitimate and effective markets"—at only moderate risk ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). The strategy rests on a long investment horizon, wide diversification, strict risk-taking guidelines and a limit of 10% ownership in any single company. The asset mix evolved from fixed income into equities: by the early 2010s the strategic allocation was 60% equities and 40% fixed income, less real estate capped at 5%; by end-2020 the strategic benchmark was **70% equities and 30% fixed income**, with the equity share rebalanced back to 70% by the end of the second quarter of 2020 ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf); [Norges Bank Annual Report 2020](https://www.norges-bank.no/contentassets/4f5b42f6218843968c078c34dfd5d3e6/annual-report-2020.pdf?v=24022021181045&ft=.pdf)). Unlisted real estate was added in the early 2010s, and unlisted renewable-energy infrastructure followed in the late 2010s: in Recommendation 344 S (2018–2019) the Storting agreed to permit such investments and raised that limit from NOK 60 billion to NOK 120 billion ([NBIM submission to the Ministry of Finance, September 2019](https://www.nbim.no/en/news-and-insights/submissions-to-ministry/2019/mandate-for-the-government-pension-fund-global--investments-in-renewable-energy-infrastructure/)).

Because all petroleum revenues are transferred to the fund and invested abroad, the fund acts as a **sterilization mechanism**: foreign currency from oil and gas exports is converted and invested offshore, so the associated demand pressure does not inflate the domestic non-tradable sector—a direct counter to Dutch disease. Fund assets **may not be invested in Norway** ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). Spending is capped by the fiscal rule, so only the real return, not the capital, is phased into the budget ([NBIM, "About the fund"](https://www.nbim.no/en/about-us/about-the-fund/)).

**Ethical guidelines and the Council on Ethics.** The GPFG has been subject to guidelines for observation and exclusion since 2004 ([regjeringen.no, "Ethical guidelines"](https://www.regjeringen.no/en/topics/the-economy/the-government-pension-fund/responsible-management/ethical-guidelines/id447009/)). The framework rests on two obligations: management should secure lasting value creation for current and future generations, and the fund should avoid investment in companies that cause or contribute to serious violations of ethical norms. The criteria are both **product-based** (tobacco, cannabis, certain weapons, coal) and **conduct-based** (human-rights violations, sale of weapons used to violate international humanitarian law, corruption and other financial crime, environmental damage, and greenhouse-gas emissions). The independent Council on Ethics assesses companies and submits recommendations; **decision-making authority rests with the Executive Board of Norges Bank**, which may also use observation and active ownership as alternatives to exclusion. Both Norges Bank's exclusion list and the Council's recommendations are published ([regjeringen.no](https://www.regjeringen.no/en/topics/the-economy/the-government-pension-fund/responsible-management/ethical-guidelines/id447009/); [Council on Ethics](https://etikkradet.no/en/)).

**Transparency and accountability.** The fund meets 16 of 16 good-governance standards assessed by NRGI (clear deposit, withdrawal and investment rules; transparency; publicly available audits; effective oversight), scores 100/100 on the NRGI Resource Governance Index natural-resource-fund module, and 98/100 on the Truman Sovereign Wealth Fund Scoreboard ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). Norway was a member of the International Working Group of Sovereign Wealth Funds that drafted the Santiago Principles (2008), and the fund's structure is consistent with them ([IWG, "Santiago Principles," 2008](https://www.ifswf.org/sites/default/files/santiagoprinciples_0_0.pdf)). Accountability runs through annual reports, published holdings, audited financial statements, the Auditor General's audit, and annual Ministry reports to the Storting on the ethics work ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf); [regjeringen.no](https://www.regjeringen.no/en/topics/the-economy/the-government-pension-fund/responsible-management/ethical-guidelines/id447009/)).

**Funding sources: petroleum taxes, royalties, SDFI and the national oil company.** Deposits consist of gross petroleum revenues—taxes, royalties, operating income from the State's Direct Financial Interest (SDFI), dividends, and transfers from the Petroleum Insurance Fund—minus government expenses, plus net revenues from the government's sale of shares in Statoil and other sector equity, plus the return on the fund's own investments ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). The SDFI is the system under which the state directly owns holdings in oil and gas fields, pipelines and onshore facilities; it was established on 1 January 1985, before which the state held licence interests only through Statoil, and when Statoil was listed in 2001 the SDFI portfolio was transferred to the state-owned management company Petoro ([Norwegian Petroleum, "The government's revenues"](https://www.norskpetroleum.no/en/economy/governments-revenues/)). The boundary between the fund and the commercial oil sector is clear: the fund holds no domestic assets and does not hold Equinor (formerly Statoil) shares; the state's commercial equity stake and its dividends are held and received outside the fund, while dividends and petroleum tax receipts are transferred into the fund as government revenue ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf); [Norwegian Petroleum](https://www.norskpetroleum.no/en/economy/governments-revenues/)). **Evidence note:** the exact realized end-2020 asset-allocation split in percentage terms was not verified from a primary source; the table reports the strategic benchmark and the documented rebalancing, plus the earlier 60/40 allocation.

### 4.3 Outcomes, lessons and transferability

Norway's outcomes support the framework's core claims. On **Dutch disease avoidance**, all petroleum revenues are transferred to the fund, fund assets may not be invested in Norway, and only the expected real return is phased into the budget ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)); the ECB's review of oil-exporting countries identifies Norway (with Botswana) as one of two examples of "successful natural resource management" ([Sturm, Gurtner & Gonzalez Alegre 2009, ECB Occasional Paper 104](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpops/ecbocp104.pdf)). On **fiscal discipline and stability**, the rule delinked spending from contemporaneous revenue; the fund is fully integrated into the annual budget and parliament must approve all withdrawals, and the rule allows temporary deviations under specified circumstances—an escape clause that preserves flexibility ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). On **transparency and institutions**, the fund scored 16/16 on NRGI's good governance standards, 100/100 on the RGI natural resource fund score and 98/100 on the Truman scoreboard ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). On **intergenerational savings**, the fund's market value reached NOK 10,914 billion at end-2020 and it held on average 1.5% of all listed global companies ([Norges Bank Annual Report 2020](https://www.norges-bank.no/contentassets/4f5b42f6218843968c078c34dfd5d3e6/annual-report-2020.pdf?v=24022021181045&ft=.pdf); [NBIM, "About the fund"](https://www.nbim.no/en/about-us/about-the-fund/)).

Six elements are most often cited as transferable: (1) clear, stable fiscal rules—the 4% benchmark has been in place since 2001 ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)); (2) investing revenues abroad—fund assets may not be invested in Norway; (3) transparency and independent central-bank management—the Ministry of Finance owns the fund, NBIM operates it, and the Supervisory Council and Auditor General oversee it; (4) political consensus—Norway's major political parties have agreed on the rule; (5) fiscal discipline—the rule delinks spending from resource revenues; and (6) separating spending decisions from resource revenues—all petroleum revenues go to the fund and only the expected return is budgeted ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)).

**Preconditions and transferability.** The decisive reference is van der Ploeg and Venables (2011): "What is appropriate for a high income and capital abundant country, such as Norway or Kuwait, is unlikely to be appropriate for a country with low income, widespread poverty and scarcity of capital, such as Ghana or Uganda" ([van der Ploeg & Venables 2011](https://tonyvenables.weebly.com/uploads/1/1/5/4/115449811/harnessing2.pdf)). For capital-scarce developing economies, the PIH prescription underlying Norway's rule is not optimal: incremental consumption should be skewed toward present generations and savings directed to domestic private and public capital rather than foreign assets. The transferability boundary is therefore sharp: the institutional and governance elements are portable, but the macroeconomic core—save abroad, spend only the real return—is optimal for capital-abundant Norway but welfare-reducing for capital-scarce producers.

| Norway's preconditions | Typical low-capacity producer |
|---|---|
| High income, capital abundant ([van der Ploeg & Venables 2011](https://tonyvenables.weebly.com/uploads/1/1/5/4/115449811/harnessing2.pdf)) | Low income, capital scarce, high domestic returns to capital |
| Strong institutions and governance ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)) | Weak institutions, rent-seeking |
| Political consensus on the fiscal rule ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)) | Political instability, patronage |
| Fund established 1990, after decades of production ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)) | Earlier discovery, limited time to build institutions |

*Transferable:* transparency, independent central-bank management, clear and stable rules, political consensus, and separation of spending from resource revenues. *Not transferable:* the macroeconomic core of saving abroad and spending only the real return, which is optimal for capital-abundant Norway but welfare-reducing for capital-scarce producers ([van der Ploeg & Venables 2011](https://tonyvenables.weebly.com/uploads/1/1/5/4/115449811/harnessing2.pdf)). Small population, high human capital and late discovery are commonly cited as additional preconditions but could not be verified from primary sources here.

**Critiques.** (1) *Over-saving:* van der Ploeg and Venables argue the PIH/bird-in-hand rule is not optimal for capital-scarce economies and that consumption should be skewed toward present generations; by implication, Norway's model is excessively conservative if emulated by developing countries ([van der Ploeg & Venables 2011](https://tonyvenables.weebly.com/uploads/1/1/5/4/115449811/harnessing2.pdf)). (2) *Ethical-investment debates:* the ethical guidelines, the Council on Ethics, negative screening of weapons, and exclusion of investments risking human-rights violations, severe environmental damage and gross corruption are themselves contested ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)). (3) *Sheer fund scale:* the GPFG's size and its ~1.5% average share of listed global companies raise questions about market impact and the political economy of managing such a large asset ([NBIM, "About the fund"](https://www.nbim.no/en/about-us/about-the-fund/)). (4) *Political economy of spending pressure:* the ECB documents competing pressures on oil-exporter fiscal policy—distribution-related demands, development spending needs and international considerations ([Sturm et al. 2009](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpops/ecbocp104.pdf)). (5) Currency appreciation, climate concerns and "fund euphoria" are widely discussed in the literature, but specific supporting sources could not be verified here; the NRGI profile's environmental-damage exclusion criterion is the closest verified element relevant to climate concerns ([NRGI 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Norway_July2013.pdf)).

**Comparison with other cases.** Chile operates a structural balance rule with dedicated stabilization and pension reserve funds; like Norway, it combines a fiscal anchor with funds, but its rule is price-based rather than wealth-based ([NRGI](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/documents/structural-balance-policy-in-chile.pdf); [Engel et al.](https://www.eduardoengel.com/papers/66%20Engel_Neilson_Valdes_Vol17_393_425.pdf)). Botswana is identified by the ECB alongside Norway as a successful case, reflecting its Pula Fund and governance-based escape from the curse ([Sturm et al. 2009](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpops/ecbocp104.pdf); [NRGI, *Botswana* country profile, 2013](https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/NRF_Botswana_July2013.pdf)). Kuwait is grouped with Norway by van der Ploeg and Venables as a high-income, capital-abundant economy for which a Norway-like approach is appropriate ([van der Ploeg & Venables 2011](https://tonyvenables.weebly.com/uploads/1/1/5/4/115449811/harnessing2.pdf)). Alaska and Timor-Leste are related applications (the Alaska Permanent Fund dividend and the Timor-Leste Petroleum Fund) analyzed in Sections 2.1 and 2.4. Comparable 2020 figures for Nigeria and Venezuela could not be verified from primary sources here, so no quantitative comparison table is offered for those cases; populating it would require triangulation against IMF Article IV consultations, World Bank development indicators and NRGI country profiles.
