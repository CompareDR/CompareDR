## Executive Summary

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)—the acquisition of genetic material from a non-parental organism—has long been central to microbial evolution, but plant research now shows that it also contributes to plant genome evolution, host–parasite interactions, and plant-associated microbial adaptation. In the plant world, the strongest and most mechanistically resolved examples are **Agrobacterium-mediated T-DNA transfer** and **parasitic plant–host RNA and gene exchange**. Plant–fungus transfers are usually rarer, older, and harder to prove, but phylogenomic studies support **bidirectional exchange**. Virus–plant HGT is best documented through **endogenous viral elements**: viral DNA fragments integrated into plant genomes, some of which remain transcriptionally active.

My concrete assessment is that HGT in plants should not be treated as a marginal curiosity. It is **mechanistically common at biological interfaces where cells or vasculature are physically connected**—especially haustoria, wounds, root infections, symbioses, and viral infection sites. However, the evidentiary strength differs sharply by system: **Agrobacterium T-DNA integration is the clearest case of stable DNA-level HGT**, parasitic plant systems show the clearest **ongoing macromolecule exchange**, and plant–fungus and virus-mediated plant-to-plant transfers remain more difficult to demonstrate as stable, inherited nuclear-gene transfers.

# 1. Between Parasitic Plants and Host Plants

## 1.1. Why Parasitic Plants Are Favorable Systems for HGT

Parasitic plants are unusually favorable environments for plant-to-plant HGT because they form **haustoria**, specialized invasive organs that physically connect parasite tissues with host tissues. Haustoria function as physiological bridges, allowing movement of water, nutrients, metabolites, proteins, RNAs, and potentially DNA between species. This physical continuity is especially important because plant cell walls normally limit direct cell-to-cell exchange across species boundaries ([Penn State ETDA, n.d.](https://etda.libraries.psu.edu/catalog/19772)).

Major agriculturally important parasitic plant groups include **Cuscuta** spp. dodders, **Striga** spp. witchweeds, and **Orobanche/Phelipanche** broomrapes. Their haustoria act as vascular interfaces, often linking to host xylem and/or phloem and enabling cross-species movement of biomolecules ([Frontiers in Plant Science, 2025a](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2025.1716304/full)).

### Key structure: Haustorium

| Feature | Role in exchange |
|---|---|
| **Haustorium** | Invasive parasitic organ that penetrates host tissue |
| **Xylem/phloem connection** | Enables nutrient and macromolecule transport |
| **Parenchyma bridge** | Provides a route for long-distance RNA movement |
| **Host–parasite junction** | Site where cross-species biomolecule trafficking occurs |

mRNA movement can occur from the haustorium through parenchyma cells and phloem across the **Cuscuta–host junction**, supporting the idea that haustorial connections are not merely nutrient taps but also molecular communication interfaces ([Frontiers in Plant Science, 2025a](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2025.1716304/full)).

---

## 1.2. Genetic Material Transferred in Parasitic Plant Systems

Parasitic plant interactions involve multiple classes of transferred genetic or gene-expression material:

- **mRNAs**
- **small RNAs, including microRNAs**
- **long noncoding RNAs**
- **possibly DNA or DNA-derived sequences over evolutionary time**
- **secondary siRNAs triggered in host tissue**

The best-studied system is **Cuscuta**, especially *Cuscuta campestris*, *Cuscuta pentagona*, and *Cuscuta australis*.

---

## 1.3. Cuscuta–Host mRNA Exchange

A landmark pre-2021 research direction showed that **Cuscuta haustoria enable bidirectional mRNA movement** between parasite and host. In *Cuscuta pentagona* attached to host plants such as *Arabidopsis thaliana* and tomato, thousands of host transcripts were detected inside parasite tissue, while many parasite transcripts were detected in host tissue. One study reported that thousands of *Arabidopsis* transcripts—representing nearly half of the expressed *Arabidopsis* transcriptome—were detectable in attached *Cuscuta*, demonstrating massive bidirectional RNA movement ([Kim, 2014](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/items/48030aa9-2e5f-47f5-836f-3080ad6e9eaf)).

However, the biological significance of transferred mRNAs remains incompletely resolved. Before 2021, researchers could show movement convincingly, but whether most transferred host mRNAs are translated or functionally useful to the parasite remained uncertain ([Kim, 2014](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstream/handle/10919/50509/Kim_G_D_2014.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)).

### Example: *Cuscuta* and flowering signals

Later reviews report that some host mRNAs can be translated in dodder and that tobacco **FT mRNA** transferred to *Cuscuta australis* can induce flowering in the parasite. This is important because it suggests that at least some transferred RNAs are not passive molecular leakage but can have developmental function ([Frontiers in Plant Science, 2025b](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2025.1712714/pdf)).

---

## 1.4. Cuscuta microRNAs as Virulence Factors

A particularly important discovery is that *Cuscuta campestris* produces many **haustorium-enriched, novel 22-nucleotide microRNAs** during parasitism. These parasite-derived miRNAs move into the host and target host mRNAs. In *Arabidopsis*, they cause host mRNA cleavage, trigger secondary siRNA production, and reduce target transcript accumulation. This indicates that parasite miRNAs function as **virulence factors**, actively weakening host defense or hormone pathways ([Shahid et al., 2018](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/micrornas-from-the-parasitic-plant-cuscuta-campestris-target-host/)).

Known *Cuscuta* miRNA targets include host genes involved in immunity and hormone signaling:

| Parasite | Host | Host target genes | Functional relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Cuscuta campestris* | *Arabidopsis thaliana* | **BIK1**, **TIR1**, **AFB3**, **ABF2**, **HSFB4/SCZ**, **SERK2**, **SNAK2** | Suppression of immune and hormone-signaling pathways |
| *Cuscuta australis* | Tobacco | **FT mRNA** transferred from host | Can induce flowering in parasite |
| *Cuscuta* spp. | Multiple hosts | Host mRNAs and parasite mRNAs | Bidirectional transcript movement |

These examples show that parasitic plant HGT-like exchange is not only about DNA integration. In living parasitic interactions, **RNA trafficking is a major functional currency**.

---

## 1.5. Striga and Other Root Parasites

*Striga* and *Orobanche/Phelipanche* are root parasitic plants with haustoria that connect to host roots. Although the provided research set contains fewer specific gene-level examples for *Striga* than for *Cuscuta*, the same biological principle applies: haustorial vascular interfaces create opportunities for molecular exchange. A broader comparative point is that HGT appears more frequent in plants with increased heterotrophy, including parasitic lineages, supporting the idea that parasitism increases exposure to foreign genetic material ([Quispe-Huamanquispe et al., 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.02015/full)).

**Assessment:** In parasitic plants, the best direct evidence before 2021 concerns **RNA movement**, especially in *Cuscuta*. Stable DNA-level HGT between parasitic plants and hosts is plausible and supported by broader phylogenomic work, but the clearest experimentally tractable mechanism is haustorium-mediated RNA exchange.

# 2. Between Fungi and Plants

## 2.1. Directionality: Bidirectional but Unevenly Documented

Plant–fungus HGT is **bidirectional**, but it is generally rarer and older than bacterial HGT. Most reported cases are inferred from phylogenetic incongruence, unusual gene distribution, and similarity searches rather than direct observation. Plant–fungus interactions create opportunities for HGT because fungi can live as pathogens, endophytes, mycorrhizal symbionts, or decomposers in intimate contact with plant tissues ([Plant–Fungus Horizontal Gene Transfer, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)).

A 2018 study of the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus *Rhizophagus irregularis* emphasized that the physical association among fungi, plants, and bacteria provides opportunities for genetic exchange. It identified putative horizontally derived genes in *R. irregularis*, including genes acquired from bacteria and plants, with more than half of foreign genes expressed in transcriptomic experiments, suggesting possible functionality ([Li et al., 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)).

---

## 2.2. Fungi-to-Plant Transfers

### Example 1: Fungal genes in early land plants

Phylogenetic analyses support transfer of several fungal genes into plant lineages. In one study, seven fungal genes were inferred to have moved into plants. These included genes encoding:

- **Flotillin-like protein**
- **E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase**
- **Centromere protein B**
- **Ubiquitin-conjugating protein**
- **Macro domain-containing protein**
- **Translation factor**
- **FAD-binding domain-containing protein**

The flotillin-like protein case is notable because land plant sequences were embedded within a fungal sequence group, consistent with acquisition from fungi by an ancestor of land plants. This fits the broader view that early land plants had close symbioses with fungal lineages such as Glomeromycota and Mucoromycotina ([Li et al., 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)).

### Example 2: Moss and lycophyte genes of fungal origin

Specific plant examples include:

| Recipient plant | Donor inferred | Gene or protein | Possible relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Physcomitrella patens* moss | Fungi | **FAD-binding domain-containing protein** | Metabolic or redox function |
| *Physcomitrella patens* | Fungi | **Macro domain-containing protein** | Regulatory or stress-related function |
| *Selaginella moellendorffii* | Fungi | **Ubiquitin-conjugating protein** | Protein regulation |
| *Monoraphidium neglectum* | Fungi | **E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase** | Protein turnover and regulation |

These examples are especially relevant because some horizontally acquired genes in early plants have been associated with biological processes such as plant defense, hormone biosynthesis, and terrestrial adaptation ([Li et al., 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)).

### Example 3: Fungal intron transfer into plant mitochondria

A classic case involves a group I intron in the mitochondrial **cox1** gene of *Peperomia polybotrya*. Phylogenetic analysis grouped the intron with fungal mitochondrial introns, while the surrounding plant exon had a different evolutionary history. This led to the hypothesis that *Peperomia* acquired the intron from a fungal donor ([Rosewich & Kistler, 2000](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)).

---

## 2.3. Plant-to-Fungus Transfers

Plant-to-fungus HGT is less frequently documented than fungi-to-plant transfer but is supported by several reported cases.

### Example 1: Plant-derived genes in *Rhizophagus irregularis*

The arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus *Rhizophagus irregularis* was reported to contain genes acquired from bacteria and plants. Some foreign genes were expressed, indicating they may be functional in the fungus. The transferred genes were linked to processes such as gene-expression regulation, mitosis, and signal transduction—functions potentially relevant to symbiotic adaptation ([Li et al., 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)).

### Example 2: Plant expansin genes in fungi

Plant expansins are proteins associated with loosening plant cell walls. Reports of plant expansin genes transferred to fungi are significant because such genes could help fungi interact with or colonize plant tissues. This is a plausible adaptive transfer: a fungus acquiring plant-like cell-wall-modifying capacity could gain an advantage during infection or symbiosis ([Plant–Fungus Horizontal Gene Transfer, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)).

### Example 3: Plant-derived genes in *Colletotrichum*

Evidence of gene transfer from plants has been documented in the fungal genus *Colletotrichum*, a group containing many plant pathogens. Although the exact gene-level details vary by study, the broad significance is that plant pathogens may acquire plant genes that improve host colonization or metabolic compatibility ([Plant–Fungus Horizontal Gene Transfer, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)).

---

## 2.4. Interpretation

Plant–fungus HGT is best understood as **bidirectional but asymmetrical in evidentiary clarity**:

- Fungi-to-plant transfers are supported by several phylogenomic examples in early land plants.
- Plant-to-fungus transfers are plausible and reported, especially in symbiotic or pathogenic fungi, but many cases require careful phylogenetic validation.
- Close associations—mycorrhizae, endophytes, pathogenic infections, and decaying plant biomass—likely increase opportunities for transfer.

**Assessment:** Plant–fungus HGT is real, but compared with Agrobacterium and Cuscuta systems, it is usually less direct, more ancient, and more dependent on phylogenomic inference.

# 3. Between Bacteria and Plants: Agrobacterium T-DNA Transfer

## 3.1. Agrobacterium as a Natural Genetic Engineer

The best-understood bacteria-to-plant HGT system is **Agrobacterium-mediated transformation**. *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* and *Rhizobium/Agrobacterium rhizogenes* transfer a segment of plasmid DNA called **T-DNA** into plant cells. This transferred DNA can integrate into the plant nuclear genome and alter plant physiology ([Quispe-Huamanquispe et al., 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.02015/full)).

### Mechanism

1. Plant wounds release phenolic compounds.
2. Phenolics activate bacterial **virulence genes**.
3. Vir proteins process the Ti or Ri plasmid T-DNA.
4. T-DNA is delivered into the plant cell.
5. T-DNA is imported into the nucleus.
6. T-DNA integrates into the plant genome.
7. T-DNA genes are expressed in plant cells.

Important T-DNA genes include:

| Gene | Function |
|---|---|
| **iaaM** | Auxin biosynthesis |
| **iaaH** | Auxin biosynthesis |
| **rolA**, **rolB**, **rolC** | Root formation and developmental alteration |
| **opine synthase genes** | Production of opines used by bacteria |
| **mis** | Mikimopine synthesis |
| **cus** | Cucumopine synthase, found in mini T-DNAs |

Expression of T-DNA genes can cause crown gall tumors or hairy roots, but natural plant transformants often do **not** show classic disease phenotypes ([Matveeva & Lutova, 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).

---

## 3.2. Natural Agrobacterium Transformants

Natural Agrobacterium-derived T-DNA sequences, called **cellular T-DNAs** or **cT-DNAs**, have been found in several plant lineages. Early examples include **Nicotiana** and **Linaria**. In *Nicotiana glauca*, cT-DNA contains homologs of T-DNA genes such as **NgrolB**, **NgrolC**, **NgORF13**, **NgORF14**, and **Ngmis**. Some of these homologs are expressed, suggesting they are not merely degraded pseudogenes ([Matveeva & Lutova, 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).

A 2019 study expanded this pattern, reporting cT-DNAs in numerous dicot species and suggesting that natural Agrobacterium transformation may be more widespread than previously recognized ([Matveeva et al., 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11103-019-00913-y)).

---

## 3.3. Sweet Potato as a Naturally Transgenic Food Crop

The most famous crop example is cultivated sweet potato, *Ipomoea batatas*. In 2015, Kyndt and colleagues published a PNAS study showing that the cultivated sweet potato genome contains **Agrobacterium-derived T-DNAs with expressed genes**. This made sweet potato a documented example of a **naturally transgenic food crop** ([Kyndt et al., 2015](https://ilcym.cipotato.org/publications/the-genome-of-cultivated-sweet-potato-contains-agrobacterium-t-dnas-with-expressed-genes-an-example-of-a-naturally-transgenic-food-crop/)).

The Agrobacterium-like sequences were not dismissed as contamination. Instead, researchers used multiple methods to show that they are genuinely part of the sweet potato genome. One T-DNA was found in all 291 tested sweet potato cultivars and in some wild relatives, suggesting that the integration occurred in an ancestor before domestication ([Ghent University, 2015](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150421084204.htm)).

### Sweet potato T-DNA facts

| Feature | Finding |
|---|---|
| Species | *Ipomoea batatas* |
| Donor | Agrobacterium-like bacterium |
| Integrated sequences | **IbT-DNA1**, **IbT-DNA2** |
| Expression | T-DNA genes expressed in sweet potato tissues |
| Distribution | One T-DNA found in all 291 tested cultivars |
| Interpretation | Ancient, stable, inherited HGT event |

A later study found that **IbT-DNA1** and **IbT-DNA2** are not restricted to cultivated hexaploid sweet potato. They also occur in tetraploid *I. batatas* and related species in *Ipomoea* series **Batatas**, including *I. cordatotriloba*, *I. tenuissima*, and *I. trifida* ([Quispe-Huamanquispe et al., 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)).

---

## 3.4. Wider Embryophyte Evidence

More recent genome-scale surveys report that natural Agrobacterium-mediated HGT is much more widespread across land plants than once thought. A survey of more than 14,000 embryophytes identified 2,614 naturally transformed species containing integrated T-DNA sequences. T-DNA-like sequences were detected not only in flowering plants but also in mosses and ferns. Reduced **mini T-DNAs**, sometimes containing only one gene such as **cucumopine synthase**, appear common in mosses and ferns and may represent ancient forms of T-DNA not associated with classic tumor or hairy-root disease ([IBMP/CNRS, 2026](https://www.ibmp.cnrs.fr/des-transferts-horizontaux-de-genes-medies-par-agrobacterium-detectes-chez-les-embryophytes-revelent-un-role-ancien-des-mini-t-adn/?lang=en)).

**Assessment:** Agrobacterium provides the strongest plant-world example of stable DNA-level HGT because the mechanism is known, the transferred DNA is identifiable, and natural inherited examples exist in major plant lineages and crops.

# 4. Between Viruses and Plants

## 4.1. Do Viruses Leave Genes Inside Plants?

Yes. Viruses can leave genetic material inside plant genomes as **endogenous viral elements** or **EVEs**. EVEs are viral sequences integrated into host genomes. In plants, many characterized EVEs originate from DNA viruses, especially **Caulimoviridae** and **Geminiviridae** ([ANR EVENTS Project, 2017](https://anr.fr/Project-ANR-17-CE20-0001)).

EVEs are important because they show that virus-to-plant HGT can persist over evolutionary time. Some EVEs are degraded fossils, but others may remain transcriptionally active or even influence host defense.

### Examples

| Viral group | Plant examples | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Caulimoviridae** | Rice, sorghum, citrus, grape, apple, pear, strawberry, eucalyptus, poplar, tomato, potato, cucumber, cotton | Endogenous florendovirus-like elements integrated in angiosperm genomes |
| **Geminiviridae** | Yams | Endogenous geminiviral elements, some transcriptionally active |
| Endogenous viral elements generally | Many angiosperms | Can inform virus evolution and possible host functions |

The EVENTS project summary notes that ancestral caulimovirid DNA was captured in the genomes of many economically important crops and that endogenous geminivirus-like elements in yams are transcriptionally active ([ANR EVENTS Project, 2017](https://anr.fr/Project-ANR-17-CE20-0001)).

---

## 4.2. Can Viral Genes Become Functional in Plants?

Some EVEs may affect plant biology in several possible ways:

- Acting as natural viral transgenes
- Producing viral-derived RNAs or proteins
- Contributing to antiviral resistance
- Modifying gene expression through promoters, splice sites, or fused open reading frames
- Serving as records of ancient viral infections

The strongest pre-2021 conclusion is that viral integrations are real and widespread. The more cautious conclusion is that functionality varies case by case and requires experimental validation.

---

## 4.3. Can Viruses Act as Carriers or Vectors Between Plants?

Viruses can act as **vectors of genetic material**, but the strength of evidence depends on what kind of transfer is being claimed.

### Strongly supported

- Viruses transfer their **own genomes** between plant cells and between plants during infection.
- Viral DNA can integrate into plant genomes as EVEs.
- Plant viruses are widely exploited in biotechnology as vectors for gene expression or gene silencing.

### Plausible but less strongly established for natural inherited HGT

- Viruses could theoretically carry host-derived sequences and move them between plants.
- Stable plant-to-plant nuclear HGT mediated by viruses would require:
  1. Capture of plant DNA or RNA,
  2. Movement to another plant,
  3. Conversion to DNA if needed,
  4. Nuclear integration,
  5. Germline inheritance.

General HGT literature recognizes viruses as potential carriers of genetic material across species, but in plants, the best-documented natural record is still **virus-to-plant integration**, not routine virus-mediated plant-to-plant gene transfer ([Horizontal Gene Transfer, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer)).

**Assessment:** Viruses clearly contribute genetic material to plant genomes through EVEs. Their role as natural plant-to-plant HGT vectors is plausible and biologically reasonable, but less firmly demonstrated than Agrobacterium T-DNA transfer or Cuscuta-mediated RNA exchange.

# Comparative Synthesis

| Interface | Main structure or mechanism | Main material transferred | Strength of evidence | Representative examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parasitic plant–host | Haustorium, vascular bridge | mRNA, miRNA, lncRNA, possibly DNA over evolution | Strong for RNA movement; moderate for stable DNA HGT | *Cuscuta campestris* miRNAs targeting **BIK1**, **TIR1**, **AFB3** |
| Fungus–plant | Symbiosis, infection, mycorrhizae | Nuclear genes, organellar introns, functional proteins | Moderate; often phylogenomic | Fungal genes in *Physcomitrella*, *Selaginella*; plant genes in *Rhizophagus* |

| Virus–plant | Infection and genome integration | Viral DNA/RNA-derived sequences | Strong for EVEs; weaker for plant-to-plant HGT | Florendovirus-like EVEs; yam endogenous geminiviral elements |

# Overall Conclusion

HGT in the plant world is best understood as a spectrum of processes rather than a single phenomenon. At one end are **active, ongoing molecular exchanges**, such as *Cuscuta* miRNAs and mRNAs moving across haustoria. At the other end are **ancient, inherited genome integrations**, such as Agrobacterium T-DNAs in *Ipomoea batatas* and endogenous viral elements in crop genomes.

The clearest hierarchy of evidence is:

1. **Agrobacterium-to-plant T-DNA transfer**: strongest mechanistic and genomic evidence.
2. **Parasitic plant–host RNA exchange**: strongest living-interface evidence, especially in *Cuscuta*.
3. **Plant–fungus HGT**: real and bidirectional, but often ancient and inferred.
4. **Virus–plant HGT**: strong for viral sequence integration, less conclusive for virus-mediated stable plant-to-plant gene transfer.

My evidence-based opinion is that future HGT research in plants should prioritize **interfaces where physical biological continuity exists**: haustoria, mycorrhizae, endophyte infection zones, wounded tissues, and viral replication sites. These are the places where the barriers between species are lowest and where HGT is most likely to become evolutionarily meaningful.
