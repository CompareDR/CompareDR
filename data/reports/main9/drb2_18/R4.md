# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants: Parasitic Relationships and Other Inter-Kingdom Routes

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) is the movement of genetic material between unrelated organisms without reproduction. In plants, intimate biological contacts — parasitism, symbiosis, pathogenesis, and viral infection — create the physical interfaces that make such transfers possible. This report consolidates findings published mainly before 2021 into four arenas: parasitic plant–host exchange, fungus–plant exchange, *Agrobacterium*–plant exchange, and virus–plant exchange.

## 1. Gene Exchange Between Parasitic Plants and Their Hosts

Parasitic plants exchange genes with their hosts through a specialized infection organ called the **haustorium**, and the exchange is large-scale, haustorium-biased, and strongly asymmetric in favor of host-to-parasite transfer at the DNA level. RNA, by contrast, moves in both directions.

### The haustorium: anatomy of the exchange interface

- The **haustorium** is the defining infection organ of parasitic plants; it mediates attachment, penetration of host tissues, and establishment of vascular continuity that enables exchange of water, nutrients, and genetic information ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/)).
- **All haustoria contain a xylem–xylem connection**, giving the parasite access to host water and dissolved solutes; this is the one vascular connection common to every parasitic lineage ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/)).
- In the stem parasite *Cuscuta* (dodder), haustorial cells grow invasively between and through host cells toward the vascular tissue. Cells contacting host xylem differentiate into xylem strands; cells contacting host phloem differentiate into phloem/transfer cells. Parasite cells may additionally form **shared plasmodesmata** with host cells, extending the association to symplastic continuity ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/)).
- Phloem connections are functionally important in *Cuscuta*, although no anatomic evidence of open sieve-pore continuity has been documented; exchange is inferred to occur across the apoplastic/symplastic boundary ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/)).
- Because xylem-only interfaces favor small solutes, while phloem/transfer-cell interfaces permit bulkier macromolecular cargo, the type of connection constrains what can move: xylem-feeding hemiparasites exchange mainly water and solutes, whereas phloem-connected parasites such as *Cuscuta* can exchange mRNAs, small RNAs, and proteins ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/)).
- Parasitism has evolved independently at least 12 times and includes more than 4,000 parasitic species, so haustoria are structurally diverse, but a shared "bauplan" (an attachment region plus parasitic tissue inside the host) is recognized across lineages ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/)).

### What moves across the interface

| Interface structure | Material class | Direction | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Xylem–xylem bridge (universal) | Water, dissolved nutrients; DNA fragments inferred | Host → parasite | Anatomy strong; DNA transfer moderate ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/)) |
| Phloem/transfer cells + shared plasmodesmata (*Cuscuta*) | mRNA, proteins, small RNAs | Bidirectional | Strong ([Kim et al. 2014](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/genomic-scale-exchange-of-mrna-between-a-parasitic-plant-and-its-/); [Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/)) |
| Invasive intracellular penetration | Nuclear DNA fragments/genes | Host → parasite | Confirmed phylogenomically ([Yang et al. 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113); [Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)) |
| Plasmodesmata (symplastic continuity) | siRNA/miRNA; mRNA | Host → parasite (siRNA uptake); bidirectional (mRNA) | Moderate–strong ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/); [Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)) |
| Haustorial interface (genome-wide) | Retrotransposons, pseudogenes, non-coding sequences | Host → parasite | Moderate ([Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)) |

- **Nuclear DNA fragments/genes** are the best-documented class. Genome-scale surveys detected 52 high-confidence host→parasite events in three Orobanchaceae parasites and 108 transcribed, probably functional events in *Cuscuta campestris*, plus 42 additional regions carrying host-derived transposon, pseudogene, and non-coding sequences ([Yang et al. 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113); [Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)).
- **mRNA transcripts move bidirectionally.** Transcriptome sequencing of *Cuscuta pentagona* and its host showed that mRNAs move "in high numbers and in a bidirectional manner," representing thousands of different genes, with nearly half the expressed *Arabidopsis* transcriptome detected in the parasite ([Kim et al. 2014](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/genomic-scale-exchange-of-mrna-between-a-parasitic-plant-and-its-/)).
- **Small RNAs also move.** *Cuscuta* takes siRNAs from its hosts, and horizontally acquired *Cuscuta* genes are frequent sources of 24-nucleotide siRNAs associated with RNA-directed DNA methylation; one HGT encoding a leucine-rich repeat protein kinase overlaps a microRNA implicated in regulating host gene expression ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/); [Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)).
- **Proteins and retrotransposons** also cross the interface: *Cuscuta* takes up host macromolecules including proteins, and host-derived transposon sequences have been identified among acquired *Cuscuta* regions ([Westwood et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411121/); [Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)).
- Despite massive RNA movement, genomic evidence indicates that stable integration proceeds mainly through **genomic DNA fragments rather than retro-processed RNA**. Intron-sequence comparisons show genomic-fragment integration far more often than retro-RNA sequences in Orobanchaceae, and *Cuscuta* genome sequencing supports genomic DNA rather than retroprocessed RNA as the main route ([Yang et al. 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113); [Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)). In other words, mRNA traffic is enormous and functionally relevant at the RNA level, but heritable genomic integration appears to be driven mainly by DNA-level transfer.

### Genome-scale host-to-parasite transfers: *Striga* and *Cuscuta*

- **The landmark single-gene case is *Striga hermonthica*.** Yoshida et al. (2010) reported that this eudicot root parasite of African cereals contains a nuclear gene widely conserved among grasses but absent from other eudicots. Phylogenetically the gene clusters with sorghum genes, its monocot host, indicating capture from *Sorghum bicolor* or a related grass. The transferred gene, designated ***ShContig9483***, encodes a 448-amino-acid protein of unknown function ([Yoshida et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20508124/)). A degenerated poly(A) sequence within it has been interpreted as supporting an mRNA-related origin for this particular transfer ([Spallek et al. 2013](https://bsppjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/mpp.12058)).
- **The genome-scale censuses converted anecdotes into quantitative patterns.** Yang et al. (2016) sequenced transcriptomes of three parasitic Orobanchaceae — the hemiparasite *Triphysaria versicolor* and the root parasites *Phelipanche aegyptiaca* and *Striga hermonthica* — plus the free-living outgroup *Lindenbergia*. They detected 52 high-confidence HGT events, often from lineages corresponding to known hosts, and showed that HGT frequency rises with increasing heterotrophy. Purifying selection predominated, with a small fraction of adaptively evolving sites ([Yang et al. 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).
- ***Cuscuta campestris* revealed genome-scale acquisition.** The *C. campestris* genome sequence identified 64 high-confidence horizontal gene transfer candidates derived from at least 32 different donor sequences, many transcribed and upregulated at the host–parasite interface; the same genome showed losses of photosynthesis and soil-nutrient uptake genes plus a recent whole-genome duplication ([Vogel et al. 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-04344-z)). Yang et al. (2019) extended this to 108 transcribed and probably functional HGT events in *Cuscuta campestris* and relatives, plus 42 additional host-derived regions, again supporting genomic-DNA transfer over retroprocessed RNA ([Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)).
- **Acquired genes are preferentially expressed in the haustorium** in both Orobanchaceae and *Cuscuta*, and many are upregulated at the infection interface — consistent with an adaptive role in parasitism rather than genomic noise ([Yang et al. 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113); [Vogel et al. 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-04344-z); [Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)).
- **Convergent capture strengthens the adaptive interpretation:** 18 functional *Cuscuta* HGTs were acquired from the same gene families by independent HGT events in Orobanchaceae parasites, and most are highly expressed in haustoria in both lineages. Between 16 and 20 transcribed *Cuscuta* HGT events are inferred as ancestral to the genus, implicating HGT in the radiation of *Cuscuta* ([Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)). This pattern underlies the interpretive "stolen genes as weapons" framing — parasites co-opting host genes to exploit hosts more effectively — though the popular framing is stronger than the demonstrated functional proof for most individual genes.

### Reverse transfer: parasite-to-host gene flow

- **Host→parasite transfer dominates the nuclear record, but parasite→host transfer is real.** The best-supported reverse case is mitochondrial: Mower et al. (2010) showed by PCR survey that at least three mitochondrial genes — ***atp1***, ***atp6***, and ***matR*** — were recently transferred from *Cuscuta* (the *C. campestris* group) into a *Plantago* clade. qPCR confirmed mitochondrial location, and the identical phylogenetic distribution of the three foreign genes plus retention of cytidines at ancestral RNA-editing positions indicate a single DNA-mediated transfer event. Three gene-conversion events between native and foreign *atp1* copies were detected. Notably, the foreign genes degraded into pseudogenes and were not detectably transcribed, so the transfer is real but likely non-functional in the recipient ([Mower et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1741-7007-8-150)).
- **Repeated mitochondrial *atp1* acquisition** by parasitic flowering plants occurred in four extreme parasite lineages where the parasite lives inside the host for much of its lifespan; the phenomenon was first documented broadly by Bergthorsson et al. (2003), who reported widespread mitochondrial gene transfer in flowering plants with outcomes including duplication, recapture of nucleus-lost genes, and chimaeric genes ([Bergthorsson et al. 2003](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/1f92b790-b50d-4b8f-b4a8-b1ce8927a566); [Yang et al. 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).
- **The *cox1* group I intron** is one of the most frequently detected HGT events in angiosperm mitochondria and is highly overrepresented in parasitic plants examined to date, consistent with parasitic contact facilitating its spread ([Cho & Palmer 1998](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.95.24.14244); [Sanchez-Puerta et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2727383/); [Fan et al. 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep36285)).
- **Rafflesiaceae show genome-scale mitochondrial HGT.** Xi et al. (2013) sequenced 38 protein-coding and rRNA mitochondrial genes from *Rafflesia cantleyi* and five related species, including two *Tetrastigma* hosts. Depending on species, **24%–41% of these gene sequences show HGT evidence**. Most transgenes have intact reading frames and are actively transcribed, and some maintain synteny with donor and recipient lineages, implying displacement of native genes by homologous recombination. An earlier phylogenomic study found about 2.1% of *R. cantleyi* nuclear gene transcripts were host-derived. The parasite–host divergence is about 115 million years, making non-native origin unambiguous ([Xi et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371/journal.pgen.1003265)).
- **Orobanchaceae also show mitochondrial host→parasite transfer.** Kwolek et al. (2017) reported the first host-to-parasite mitochondrial gene transfer in Orobanchaceae, extending the phenomenon beyond nuclear genes ([Kwolek et al. 2017](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/0db2/aad9c3faa54b4274f09f02de95a5f0e8e41e.pdf)); Kado et al. (2018) found HGT genes with introns retained in almost all cases, consistent with DNA-level rather than retro-RNA integration ([Kado et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6294234/)).

### The bridge hypothesis: can a generalist stem parasite connect unrelated hosts?

- *Cuscuta* is a generalist stem parasite with a broad host range and can simultaneously attack multiple, unrelated host species; Yang et al. (2016) accordingly describe parasitic plants as "nexus points" for HGT, with HGT rising with heterotrophy ([Yang et al. 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).
- The parasite→host route is demonstrated: *Cuscuta* mitochondrial DNA entered *Plantago*, proving that a generalist parasite can donate DNA to a host ([Mower et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1741-7007-8-150)).
- **However, no pre-2021 study directly demonstrates gene flow between two unrelated host species through a shared *Cuscuta* individual.** The bridge hypothesis is therefore best labeled a plausible, unproven hypothesis, not an established mechanism. The exchange is also asymmetric: host→parasite transfer is more frequent and better documented, whereas parasite→host transfer is rarer and, where documented, largely mitochondrial and often non-functional ([Mower et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1741-7007-8-150)).

### Uncertainty in parasitic-plant HGT

- **DNA-level versus RNA-mediated integration remains unresolved.** mRNA movement is demonstrably massive, yet genomic analyses favor genomic-fragment integration; the two routes may operate in parallel for different genes ([Yang et al. 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113); [Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)).
- **Plastid DNA movement across haustoria is poorly documented** compared with mitochondrial transfer and should not be assumed.
- **False-positive risk persists.** BLAST-only screens are prone to contamination and incomplete-database artifacts; phylogenomic pipelines reduce but do not eliminate this risk, and some early or single-study cases remain to be independently replicated ([Yang et al. 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).
- **Ancient versus recent transfer dating is difficult** and inferred from transcriptome sampling across the genus rather than molecular-clock evidence ([Yang et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)).
- **The Yoshida et al. (2019) *Striga asiatica* genome paper was not directly retrievable for this report**; its gene count and host-gene/retrotransposon acquisition statement rest on its indexed abstract, so specific transferred-gene identities and exact HGT counts are not enumerated here ([Yoshida et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31522940/)).

## 2. Gene Exchange Between Fungi and Plants

Plant–fungus HGT is genuinely bidirectional, but the two directions are not equally well documented. Fungus→plant transfer has more functionally validated cases, while plant→fungus transfer rests largely on one well-sampled arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus genome. The asymmetry may partly reflect sampling bias: plant genomes are larger and better assembled, making a foreign fungal gene easier to detect than a foreign plant gene in a compact fungal genome.

### Fungus-to-plant transfers

- **The best-supported case is *Fhb7*, a disease-resistance gene.** *Thinopyrum elongatum* (tall wheatgrass, a wild wheat relative) carries ***Fhb7***, which encodes a **glutathione S-transferase (GST)** that detoxifies trichothecenes — the mycotoxins that make Fusarium head blight so damaging — by de-epoxidation. *Fhb7* GST homologs are absent from other plants but show high homology to fungal genes; Wang et al. concluded that *Th. elongatum* gained *Fhb7* by HGT from an **endophytic *Epichloë* species**, a fungus that lives inside grasses and can bioprotect against pathogenic fungi ([Wang et al. 2020](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/usdaarsfacpub/2340/)).
- ***Fhb7* introgressions into cultivated wheat (*Triticum aestivum*) confer resistance to both Fusarium head blight and crown rot in diverse wheat backgrounds without yield penalty**, making the fungal-derived gene a practical resistance resource ([Wang et al. 2020](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/usdaarsfacpub/2340/)).
- **The mitochondrial *cox1* group I intron is a repeated fungal-to-plant transfer.** This is the only group I intron found in angiosperm mitochondrial genomes and sits in ***cox1*** (cytochrome oxidase subunit 1). It encodes a homing endonuclease that cleaves intronless alleles and inserts the intron via double-strand-break repair, making it a selfish, invasive element. Sanchez-Puerta et al. surveyed 640 angiosperms (212 families) and inferred roughly **70 separate HGT events** for this intron, proposing that it was originally seeded into angiosperms by a single transfer from fungi, with all later transfers occurring plant-to-plant ([Sanchez-Puerta et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2727383/); original report [Cho et al. 1998](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC24358/)).
- **The moss *Physcomitrella patens* genome contains 57 families of nuclear genes acquired from prokaryotes, fungi, or viruses**, widely cited as evidence that HGT contributed to land colonization ([Yue et al. 2012](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms2148), as reported in [Li et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)).
- **Surveys also report DNA movement into algae and bryophytes from fungi, bacteria, and viruses**, but specific gene names, donor identities, and functional evidence could not be verified from primary sources here; these should be treated as suspected context rather than confirmed protein-coding fungal-to-plant HGT ([Hibdige et al. 2020 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2020/10/13/2020.02.17.952150.full.pdf)).

### Plant-to-fungus transfers

- **The benchmark case is the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus (AMF) *Rhizophagus irregularis*.** Li et al. (2018) screened its genome with the Blast2hgt pipeline, which computes two BLAST-based surrogate scores (the HGT index *h* and alien index AI). They identified **19 fungal genes transferred between fungi and bacteria/plants**, of which **seven were obtained from bacteria**; a further **18 genes** were recently acquired from either plants or bacteria, and gene duplication expanded three foreign genes. More than half of the foreign genes were expressed across 41 transcriptomic datasets, and functional annotation implicated regulation of gene expression, mitosis, and signal transduction. The authors conclude that horizontal gene influx through endosymbiosis is a source of new functions for *R. irregularis* and may have contributed to symbiotic adaptation ([Li et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)).
- **Important qualification:** the 19-gene set is not exclusively plant-derived — bacterial donors account for 7, and the remaining 12 are attributed to fungi and/or plants without individual resolution in the published abstract ([Li et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)). The strictly plant-derived subset is therefore not resolved.
- **Phylogenomic context supports rarity.** Richards et al. (2009) compared six plant genomes with 159 other genomes, including 46 fungal species and five green algae, and demonstrated a pattern of rare and ancient HGT between plants and fungi; the detailed per-species event list could not be verified from the retrieved primary text ([Richards et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/)).
- **Plant pathogens show HGT, but the donor is fungal.** For *Zymoseptoria tritici*, *Fusarium oxysporum*, *Magnaporthe oryzae*, and *Colletotrichum* spp., extensive HGT has been documented between fungi or involves accessory chromosomes conferring pathogenicity, not plant-to-fungus transfer; no confirmed plant-derived gene with expression and functional evidence was located for these species ([Qiu et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4876562/); [Fitzpatrick 2011](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/3037/1/DF_Horizontal.pdf)).
- **Cautionary case: *Trichoderma*.** A study of nine *Trichoderma* genomes reported massive lateral transfer of genes encoding plant cell-wall-degrading enzymes, but the donors are its "plant-associated hosts," i.e., other fungi it parasitizes, not plants — a reminder that donor identity in plant-associated systems is easily misassigned ([Druzhinina et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371/journal.pgen.1007322)).
- **Negative result: *Laccaria bicolor*.** The ectomycorrhizal genome analysis reported a lack of carbohydrate-active enzymes for plant cell-wall degradation and no confirmed plant-derived genes — the opposite of what extensive plant-to-fungus acquisition would predict for a root symbiont ([Martin et al. 2008](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1165379)).
- **Caution: grass-to-grass lateral transfer is not fungal-to-plant HGT.** *Alloteropsis semialata* (a grass) was reported to carry 59 laterally acquired protein-coding genes from at least nine different donors as part of 23 large foreign DNA fragments (up to 170 kb), with functions associated with photosynthesis, disease resistance, and abiotic stress tolerance. The donors are grasses, not fungi, and the case should not be counted as plant–fungus HGT even though it is often cited alongside such cases; the 59-gene count comes from a preprint and should be checked against the peer-reviewed Dunning et al. (2019) paper ([Hibdige et al. 2020 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2020/10/13/2020.02.17.952150.full.pdf)).

### Mechanisms and evidence standards

- **Endophytic intracellular contact** is the most plausible route for *Fhb7*: the donor was an endophytic *Epichloë* species living inside grass tissues, showing that intracellular plant–fungal contact can accompany gene transfer, though the physical transfer mechanism remains unknown ([Wang et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32273397/)).
- **Mycorrhizal symbiosis interfaces** are plausible, with genomic support: AMF form arbuscules inside plant root cells and associate with bacteria in a tripartite system, and Li et al. state that the intimate association provides opportunities for genetic material exchange and that horizontal gene influx through endosymbiosis is a source of new functions ([Li et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)).
- **Group I intron homing** is a demonstrated molecular mechanism for the *cox1* intron's mobility ([Sanchez-Puerta et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2727383/)).
- **Illegitimate pollination and shared vectors** have been proposed for plant-to-plant spread of the *cox1* intron, but no fungal-to-plant DNA-transfer mechanism has been directly demonstrated ([Sanchez-Puerta et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2727383/)).
- **Phylogenetic inference is the gold standard for detecting HGT.** Fitzpatrick states that the most convincing method uses phylogenetic inference, because strongly supported topological disagreement between a gene tree and the species phylogeny can be parsimoniously explained only by HGT. Surrogate methods — patchy phyletic distribution, shared synteny, codon-usage bias, GC-content anomalies — are faster but less reliable and produced high-profile false positives, such as the retracted claim of hundreds of bacterial genes in the human genome. Contamination, misassembly, and lineage sorting must be excluded before an event is called confirmed ([Fitzpatrick 2011](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/3037/1/DF_Horizontal.pdf)).
- **Endosymbiotic gene transfer (EGT) must be distinguished from HGT.** EGT is the concerted movement of organelle genes into the host nucleus and is thought possible only in endosymbiosis; it should not be conflated with lateral transfer of individual genes ([Qiu et al. 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12915-016-0264-3)).
- **The extent of eukaryotic HGT remains contested**, and counts depend heavily on pipeline stringency ([Qiu et al. 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12915-016-0264-3)).

### Uncertainty in plant–fungus HGT

- The physical mechanism that moved *Fhb7* from *Epichloë* into *Th. elongatum* is unknown; the endophytic lifestyle makes close contact plausible but does not demonstrate the route ([Wang et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32273397/)).
- The original fungal donor of the *cox1* intron is inferred, not identified to species, and the number of independent fungal-to-plant seed transfers is debated ([Sanchez-Puerta et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2727383/)).
- No confirmed fungal-to-plant NBS-LRR or PR protein gene transfer was identified; the best fungal-to-plant disease-resistance case, *Fhb7*, encodes a GST, not an NBS-LRR or PR protein.
- Is plant→fungus HGT genuinely rarer than fungus→plant transfer, or systematically underdetected? The asymmetry may reflect sequencing bias rather than biology ([Li et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)).
- Are the *R. irregularis* foreign genes functional for symbiosis or transient passengers? Expression evidence exists, but functional proof is lacking for most candidates ([Li et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)).

## 3. *Agrobacterium* T-DNA Transfer and Natural Plant Transformants

*Agrobacterium* is the best-characterized natural bacterial-to-plant gene transfer system known. Unlike the parasitic-plant and fungal cases inferred from genome comparisons, this pathway has been dissected step by step at the molecular level for decades. It is a genuine inter-kingdom DNA transfer mechanism — not a laboratory invention — and it underlies most plant biotechnology. Several wild plant species carry integrated *Agrobacterium* T-DNA, demonstrating that this mechanism operates in nature.

### The molecular transfer mechanism

- ***Agrobacterium tumefaciens*** causes **crown gall**, a tumor-like growth usually at the root–shoot junction of a wide range of dicotyledonous plants and some gymnosperms. ***Agrobacterium rhizogenes*** causes **hairy root disease**. ***Agrobacterium vitis*** causes cane gall on grapevines, and ***A. rubi*** causes cane gall disease; ***A. radiobacter*** is a related, generally non-pathogenic species ([Subramoni et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00322/full); [Gelvin 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2012.00052/full); [Chen et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/full)).
- The transferred DNA (**T-DNA**) resides on a large **Ti (tumor-inducing) plasmid** in *A. tumefaciens* or **Ri (root-inducing) plasmid** in *A. rhizogenes*; the Ti plasmid exceeds 200 kb. Strains can carry one, two, or three T-DNA regions ([Subramoni et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00322/full); [Chen et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/full)).
- **Step 1 — signal perception in the rhizosphere.** Free-living *Agrobacterium* keeps *vir* genes essentially silent. When plant wounds release phenolic compounds (prototypically **acetosyringone**), certain sugars, and acidic conditions, the **VirA/VirG two-component system** transduces the phenolic signal into transcriptional activation of the *vir* regulon ([Subramoni et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00322/full)).
- **Step 2 — T-strand excision at the borders.** T-DNA is delimited by left and right border repeats of ~25 bp. Upon *vir* induction, **VirD2**, a site- and strand-specific endonuclease/relaxase, nicks the T-DNA at the border repeats and covalently attaches to the 5′ end of the released single strand (the T-strand). **VirD1** assists VirD2 in border cleavage, particularly at the left border. Transfer initiates at the right border and proceeds toward the left border, so integrated T-DNAs are often truncated at the left end. **VirC1** binds the overdrive sequence near the right border and stimulates T-DNA processing ([Gelvin 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2012.00052/full); [Chen et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/full)).
- **Step 3 — coating and protection of the T-strand.** The single-stranded T-strand is coated by **VirE2**, a single-stranded DNA-binding protein that forms a compact helical structure around the DNA, protecting it from nucleases and mediating nuclear import. The resulting **T-complex** comprises the T-strand plus associated VirD2 and VirE2 proteins ([Gelvin 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2012.00052/full)).
- **Step 4 — type IV secretion and the T-pilus.** The T-complex is exported across the bacterial cell envelope by the **VirB/VirD4 type IV secretion system**, composed of 11 VirB proteins and the VirD4 coupling protein. The VirB machinery assembles a **T-pilus** extending from the bacterium to the host cell surface; VirD4 recognizes the T-strand–VirD2 complex and delivers it to the secretion channel ([Gelvin 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2012.00052/full)).
- **Step 5 — nuclear import in the plant cell.** Once inside the plant cytoplasm, the T-complex reaches the nucleus because **VirD2 carries a C-terminal bipartite nuclear localization signal (NLS)** recognized by plant importin α proteins (especially IMPa-4 in *Arabidopsis*), which shuttle the complex through nuclear pores. **VirF**, another delivered effector, facilitates nuclear import and integration by targeting the plant protein VIP1 for degradation ([Gelvin 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2012.00052/full)).
- **Step 6 — integration into the plant genome.** The T-strand integrates into host chromatin by **non-homologous/illegitimate recombination**, often using microhomology between the T-DNA ends and the host genome. Integrated T-DNAs can occur as single copies or tandem/inverted repeats and are frequently incomplete at the left border. Once integrated, the T-DNA is stably inherited through mitosis and meiosis and its genes are expressed in the plant cell ([Chen et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full); [Subramoni et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00322/full)).

### Oncogenes and opines carried on the T-DNA

- **Oncogenes** redirect plant hormone balance to drive cell proliferation. On *A. tumefaciens* Ti plasmids, ***iaaM*** (tryptophan monooxygenase) and ***iaaH*** (indoleacetamide hydrolase) drive auxin biosynthesis, while ***ipt*** (isopentenyl transferase) synthesizes cytokinin. On *A. rhizogenes* Ri plasmids, the root oncogenes ***rolA***, ***rolB***, ***rolC***, and ***rolD*** modulate auxin/cytokinin sensitivity and produce the hairy-root phenotype; ***aux1***/***aux2*** and ***cyt*** homologs also contribute to hormone overproduction. Collectively, transformed cells overproduce auxin and cytokinin, causing the uncontrolled division characteristic of crown gall and hairy root tissues ([Subramoni et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00322/full); [Chen et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/full)).
- **Opine synthase genes** produce **opines** — unusual amino acid–sugar condensates that the plant cannot metabolize and that accumulate to high concentrations in transformed tissues. **nopaline** is synthesized by nopaline synthase (***nos***), **octopine** by octopine synthase (***ocs***), **mannopine** by mannopine synthase (***mas***), and **mikimopine** by mikimopine synthase (***mis***); **agrocinopine** synthesis is encoded outside the T-DNA. The opine type depends on the *Agrobacterium* strain ([Subramoni et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00322/full); [Chen et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/full)).
- Opines serve as a **private nutrient source** for the inciting bacterium: the bacterium encodes opine catabolism genes on the Ti/Ri plasmid outside the T-DNA and is attracted to opine sources by chemotaxis. Opines also activate quorum sensing and can induce conjugative transfer of Ti/Ri plasmids between strains ([Chen et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/full); [Subramoni et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00322/full)).
- **No T-DNA gene or sequence is required for transfer itself** — any DNA placed between the T-DNA borders can be transferred, integrated, and expressed. Because the T-DNA is located distantly from the *vir* genes, the two can be separated onto different plasmids without impairing transfer. This feature allowed construction of **binary vectors** — a small manipulable plasmid carrying the gene of interest between T-DNA borders plus a helper plasmid supplying *vir* genes in *trans* — and thus made the natural mechanism the foundation of most plant genetic engineering ([Gelvin 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2012.00052/full); [Subramoni et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00322/full)).

### Natural transformants: sweet potato, *Nicotiana*, and *Linaria*

Several plant lineages carry and express *Agrobacterium* T-DNA in their genomes without laboratory engineering — they are **natural transformants**. In all well-documented cases the donor is *A. rhizogenes* (Ri plasmid), not *A. tumefaciens* (Ti plasmid), so the integrated sequences are called **cellular T-DNA (cT-DNA)**. The direction is bacterium → plant; there is no evidence that these cT-DNA sequences move back into *Agrobacterium* in nature ([Chen & Otten 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/pdf); [Matveeva & Lutova 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).

- **Sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*).** Two distinct T-DNA regions, **IbT-DNA1** and **IbT-DNA2**, were unambiguously demonstrated by PCR, quantitative PCR, Southern blotting, genome walking, and BAC library screening and sequencing ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)).
  - **IbT-DNA1** contains four ORFs homologous to *Agrobacterium* ***iaaM***, ***iaaH***, ***C-prot*** (C-protein), and ***Acs*** (agrocinopine synthase) — TR-DNA-type genes combining auxin biosynthesis and opine synthesis. IbT-DNA1 sits in an intron of a plant gene strongly homologous to plant *F-box* genes ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)).
  - **IbT-DNA2** contains at least five ORFs homologous to ***ORF14***, ***ORF17n***, ***RolB/RolC***, ***ORF13***, and ***ORF18/ORF17n*** — TL-DNA-type rooting-locus genes. The two regions thus correspond to the two physically separate T-DNAs (TL-DNA and TR-DNA) that agropine-type Ri plasmids carry ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)).
  - **Expression:** qRT-PCR detected transcripts of IbT-DNA1 and IbT-DNA2 ORFs in different sweet potato tissues ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)).
  - **Distribution:** IbT-DNA1 was detected in all 291 cultigens examined but not in close wild relatives; IbT-DNA2 was detected in 45 of 217 genotypes, including cultivated and wild species. The authors interpreted fixation of IbT-DNA1 in the domesticated gene pool as evidence that it provided traits selected during domestication — a plausible inference from presence/absence patterns, not a demonstrated phenotype or selection coefficient ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)).
  - **Wild relatives:** Quispe-Huamanquispe et al. (2019) found both IbT-DNA1 and IbT-DNA2 in tetraploid *I. batatas* with highly similar sequences at the same locus as in cultivated sweet potato; IbT-DNA1 also occurred in *I. cordatotriloba* and *I. tenuissima*, and IbT-DNA2 in *I. trifida*, showing that integrated IbT-DNAs are not confined to the crop ([Quispe-Huamanquispe et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)).
- ***Nicotiana* (tobacco and relatives).** White et al. (1983) used Southern blotting to detect *A. rhizogenes*-like T-DNA sequences in the nuclear genome of uninfected *Nicotiana glauca* (tree tobacco), the first demonstration of cT-DNA. Furner et al. (1986) extended the finding to *N. tabacum*, *N. tomentosiformis*, *N. tomentosa*, and *N. otophora*, and showed that the *N. glauca* cT-DNA is an imperfect inverted repeat whose left arm carries *rolB* and *rolC* homologs and whose right arm carries only a *rolC* homolog with premature stop codons ([Chen & Otten 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/pdf)).
  - Deep sequencing of the ancestral tobacco *N. tomentosiformis* revealed four distinct cT-DNAs (TA, TB, TC, TD), representing four independent transformation events; an additional type, TE, was found in *N. otophora*. Across the genus, seven different T-DNAs from different *Agrobacterium* strains have been recognized, with up to four successive insertion events in section Tomentosae ([Chen & Otten 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/pdf)).
  - Whole-genome sequencing of *N. glauca* confirmed a single gT insertion (a partial, imperfect inverted repeat) with homologs of ***orf13a***, ***orf13***, ***orf14***, ***rolC***, ***rolB***, and ***mis***; no evidence for multiple T-DNA insertions was found ([Khafizova et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5766994/)).
  - cT-DNA genes are expressed at low levels in *Nicotiana* and are not associated with hairy-root or tumor symptoms. In some *N. tabacum* cultivars the opine-synthesis gene ***TB-mas2'*** is expressed in roots and opines are produced. The *rol* genes of *N. glauca* have been proposed to influence developmental and morphological traits that contributed to the evolution of the genus ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/); [Chen & Otten 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/pdf)).
- ***Linaria* (toadflax).** Matveeva et al. (2012) discovered cT-DNA sequences in *Linaria vulgaris*, extending natural transformation beyond Solanaceae. The *L. vulgaris* cT-DNA is present in two copies organized as a tandem imperfect direct repeat and contains ***LvORF2***, ***LvORF3***, ***LvORF8***, ***LvrolA***, ***LvrolB***, ***LvrolC***, ***LvORF13***, ***LvORF14***, and the opine-synthesis gene ***Lvmis***. All screened *L. vulgaris* and *L. dalmatica* plants carried the same oncogenes and *mis* gene. *rolC* homologs of *L. vulgaris* and *L. creticola* are expressed in vitro and can encode full-length peptides; expression in planta remains to be demonstrated ([Matveeva & Lutova 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full); [Matveeva et al. 2018](https://vavilov.elpub.ru/jour/article/view/1454?locale=en_US)).

### Uncertainty in *Agrobacterium*–plant transfer

- **Integration mechanism detail is incompletely resolved.** T-DNA integration is described as non-homologous/illegitimate recombination, but the precise molecular pathway and host repair proteins remain unresolved; recent work indicates T-DNA can integrate even when multiple DNA repair pathways are disrupted, suggesting redundancy ([Chen et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).
- **Function and selection claims are inferential.** For sweet potato, the domestication-selection claim rests on presence/absence patterns, not a demonstrated phenotype or selection coefficient; no dN/dS or population-genetic selection test was found in the accessed primary text ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)). For *Linaria*, expression is documented only in vitro, not in planta ([Matveeva et al. 2018](https://vavilov.elpub.ru/jour/article/view/1454?locale=en_US)).
- **Candidate-gene verification.** ***ORF26*** and ***cellR*** mentioned in some secondary contexts were not confirmed in the primary sweet potato description and should not be cited as established IbT-DNA genes ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)).
- **Other reported transformants**, such as *N. benavidesii* and other species, remain unconfirmed ([Chen & Otten 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01600/pdf)).
- **Frequency in nature is still being assessed.** The *Nicotiana*, *Linaria*, and *Ipomoea* cases confirm that natural T-DNA integration has occurred and been fixed, but their overall contribution to plant genome evolution remains an open question ([Chen et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).

## 4. Viral Genes Left in Plant Genomes and Viruses as Vectors

Plant genomes harbor viral DNA sequences integrated into their nuclear chromosomes — collectively termed **endogenous viral elements (EVEs)** — and a subset known as **endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs)** derives from the double-stranded DNA virus family *Caulimoviridae*. Most EVEs are molecular fossils, but a minority remain transcriptionally active and can, in exceptional cases, reactivate to produce infectious virus. Viruses can also act as carriers or vectors of host genetic material, though the only experimentally documented natural case stops short of full gene transfer.

### Endogenous viral elements and endogenous pararetroviruses

- **EVEs are viral sequences integrated into nuclear chromosomes**, enabling vertical transmission and potential fixation in host populations. Integration into the host genome is not part of the normal life cycle of plant viruses; it is an accidental, rare event ([Staginnus & Richert-Pöggeler 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16949329/)).
- **EPRVs are a class of EVEs derived from *Caulimoviridae***, which replicate via a reverse transcription step but do not require genomic integration for their life cycle. EPRVs have been identified as integrated counterparts of most members of the family ([Staginnus & Richert-Pöggeler 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16949329/)). Endogenous florendoviruses are major components of plant genomes ([Geering et al. 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms6269)).
- The most accepted integration mechanism is **non-homologous recombination** between viral and host genomes, usually in the context of double-stranded DNA break repair or a transposon-mediated process. Once integrated, EVEs are transmitted vertically through the germ line and can become fixed by drift or selection.
- **Virus families involved.** *Caulimoviridae* is the predominant source of plant EVEs; endogenous geminiviruses have been characterized in yam (*Dioscorea*) genomes ([Filloux et al. 2015](https://academic.oup.com/ve/article/1/1/vev002/2568675)); and EVEs derived from non-retroviral dsRNA, ssRNA, and ssDNA viruses have also been described in plants.
- **Most EPRVs are transcriptionally or translationally inactive** because they are partial, rearranged, or contain inactivating mutations — a genomic "fossil record" of past infections. Some EPRVs form clusters from simultaneous integration of several complete or partial copies in tandem or nested arrangements.
- **Some EPRVs are transcriptionally active** and can produce RNAs serving as precursors of extrachromosomal viral DNA, leading to systemic and vertically transmitted infections. Their RNAs can also induce RNA interference (RNAi) and gene silencing through small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), potentially conferring virus-derived resistance. If inserted into or near host genes, EPRVs can modify transcription or mRNA processing; several EPRVs are inserted in introns of *Vitis vinifera* genes, while most described EPRVs sit in intergenic regions with no apparent deleterious effect.

### Reactivation: EPRVs as reservoirs of infection

- **Banana streak virus (BSV) is the best-documented reactivation case.** *Musa balbisiana* carries integrated copies of BSV that are infectious by releasing virions in interspecific hybrids. A single integration event was identified as the origin of an infectious endogenous pararetrovirus, and the endogenous sequences show evolution consistent with banana domestication. Reactivation is triggered by hybridization stress in interspecific banana hybrids ([Gayral et al. 2008](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC2447048/); [Gayral et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2898222/)).
- **PVCV in petunia** can be induced from an endogenous provirus by activation of a single 13.5 kb sequence, producing infectious virus via both episomal and chromosomal forms ([Richert-Pöggeler et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC212712/)).
- **TVCV in *Nicotiana edwardsonii*** probably gives rise to episomal, infectious particles, but so far only vertical (not horizontal) transmission has been documented ([Lockhart et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10811941/)).
- **SCBV appears not to be integrated** into the sugarcane genome based on Southern analysis, contrasting with other badnaviruses such as BSV; this discrepancy may reflect methodological sensitivity, genuine biological absence, or strain-specific differences ([Geijskes et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15045565/)).

### Viruses as carriers of host genes: the BCTIV case

- **The only real-time, experimentally documented natural case of a plant virus capturing host chromosomal DNA and moving it toward other plant species is the *Beet curly top Iran virus* (BCTIV)–sugar beet system.** In field-grown sugar beet (*Beta vulgaris*) infected with BCTIV — a circular single-stranded DNA geminivirus with a 2845-nt genome — Catoni et al. (2018) detected small circular DNA molecules of 1294–1503 nt that are hybrids of viral and host DNA. The viral portion spans the intergenic region, including the conserved stem-loop and origin of rolling-circle replication; the remaining AT-rich host-derived portion lacks open reading frames and was shown by PCR to come from healthy *B. vulgaris* chromosomal DNA. The same molecules appeared within 4 weeks of experimental agroinfection and replicated, became encapsidated into viral particles, and spread systemically with the virus ([Catoni et al. 2018](https://d-nb.info/1176341154/34)).
- **Mechanism and species specificity.** Because the viral portion supplies the *cis*-acting replication origin and the BCTIV Rep protein acts in trans, the minicircles are replicated and packaged by the helper virus. When captured *B. vulgaris* DNA was introduced into other BCTIV-sensitive hosts co-infected with BCTIV, it replicated and was transcribed. However, formation was species-specific: in experimentally infected *Nicotiana benthamiana* and *Arabidopsis thaliana*, all small circles were defective viral molecules containing only BCTIV sequences, with no host DNA. The authors conclude that efficient minicircle formation "seems to be species specific, determining the direction of information flow between virus hosts" ([Catoni et al. 2018](https://d-nb.info/1176341154/34)).
- **Assessment: a special case, not a general phenomenon.** Catoni et al. describe the work as having likely documented in real time the initial steps of a possible path of virus-mediated horizontal transfer of chromosomal DNA between plant species, and note that virus-mediated acquisition of host genetic material appears to be rare. Three caveats matter: no integration of the captured DNA into the recipient genome was shown; no inheritance or function was shown; and the captured host fragments lacked ORFs, so no functional gene was transferred. The process should therefore be treated as a plausible, experimentally observed initial step, not as confirmed natural HGT between plant species ([Catoni et al. 2018](https://d-nb.info/1176341154/34)).

### Engineered viral vectors for gene delivery between plants

Natural virus-mediated gene movement is rare and limited; engineered viral vectors are routine but constrained by cargo size, host range, and the inability of RNA viruses to integrate.

- **The first published plant-virus vector** was a gene-replacement vector based on brome mosaic virus (BMV) (French et al. 1986), but it lacked the coat protein and could not spread beyond inoculated cells. TMV-based vectors followed (Takamatsu et al. 1987); the gene of interest was inserted upstream of the coat-protein gene under a duplicated subgenomic promoter, but the design was unstable and homologous recombination caused insert loss. CaMV vectors were hampered by narrow host range and practical limits on inserted DNA ([Ibrahim et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2019.00803/full)).
- **The landmark DNA-virus vector** was Hayes et al. (1988), who used a tomato golden mosaic virus (TGMV) vector to introduce the **neo** gene (neomycin phosphotransferase) into plants. They highlighted two advantages: systemic infection of whole plants without regeneration from single cells, and autonomous replication giving high-copy amplification; they also proposed that stable master copies of the vector could integrate into chromosomal DNA, allowing heritable gene amplification ([Hayes et al. 1988](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/23483/)).
- **Modern DNA and RNA virus vectors deliver genome-engineering reagents.** Geminiviruses are especially attractive because they have small genomes (~2.8 kb; 4–6 ORFs), a wide host range, and a single Rep protein initiating replication via rolling-circle and homologous-recombination mechanisms. Vectors based on Bean yellow dwarf virus (BeYDV), Cabbage leaf curl virus (CaLCuV), Wheat dwarf virus (WDV), and Tobacco rattle virus (TRV) have delivered CRISPR, TALEN, and ZFN reagents in *Nicotiana benthamiana*, tomato, potato, rice, wheat, tobacco, and petunia ([Zaidi et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.00539/full)).
- **Virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS)** is a widely used reverse-genetics application; PVX-based vectors were among the early systems for expression and VIGS, and TRV is a standard VIGS vector ([Chapman et al. 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1344890/); [Lacomme et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18770535/)).

### Limitations and uncertainty in virus–plant gene transfer

- **How far is natural virus-mediated HGT demonstrated?** Only the initial steps — capture, replication, and transcription of host DNA in a co-infected host — are documented, and only for one virus–host system; integration, inheritance, and function remain unshown ([Catoni et al. 2018](https://d-nb.info/1176341154/34)).
- **Is the phenomenon general?** Species-specific minicircle formation and the absence of host DNA in *N. benthamiana* and *A. thaliana* suggest a special case rather than a general route, and the captured *B. vulgaris* fragments lacked ORFs, so whether intact functional plant genes can be naturally captured and expressed is unknown ([Catoni et al. 2018](https://d-nb.info/1176341154/34)).
- **Engineered vector limits.** Cargo size is constrained in CaMV, geminiviruses, and RNA viruses; host ranges differ; DNA viruses can integrate master copies while RNA viruses generally give only transient expression; and regulatory treatment of viral-vector-delivered editing is still evolving ([Hayes et al. 1988](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/23483/); [Zaidi et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.00539/full)).
- **Fossil versus functional EVEs remains unresolved for most sequences.** The majority are likely molecular fossils, but the fraction retaining transcriptional activity or functional potential, and the conditions that switch them on, is poorly quantified ([Staginnus & Richert-Pöggeler 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16949329/); [Geering et al. 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms6269)).
- **Reactivation triggers** beyond the BSV and PVCV cases are poorly understood, and whether EVEs confer heritable virus resistance at the population level remains largely speculative ([Gayral et al. 2008](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC2447048/); [Richert-Pöggeler et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC212712/)).
