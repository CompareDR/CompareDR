# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants Through Parasitic and Infectious Relationships

## Scope, terminology, and source policy

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) in plants spans several biologically different phenomena that should not be conflated. At one end is **direct cross-species trafficking**—for example, an mRNA or small RNA moving through a parasitic haustorium. At the other is **evolutionary HGT**, in which foreign DNA becomes incorporated into a recipient genome and is transmitted through subsequent generations. The latter is the stronger sense of “HGT” used in evolutionary genomics. Between these extremes are cases such as viral DNA persisting in chromosomes or a virus transporting host DNA into another plant without demonstrated germline integration. The distinction is especially important in parasitic plants, where molecular exchange can be abundant even though only a tiny fraction of exchanged material becomes heritable. [[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1803716/)

This report emphasizes studies published **before 2021**, prioritizing primary experimental and genomic papers. A small amount of post-2020 literature is mentioned only where it materially changes how a pre-2021 claim should be interpreted. In accordance with the requested exclusion, the 2021 article *Horizontal Gene Transfers in Plants* by Emilie Aubin, Moaine El Baidouri, and Olivier Panaud, together with all four specified URLs and derivative material from them, was **not used, cited, quoted, paraphrased, or relied upon** in preparing this report.

A useful evidence hierarchy for the discussion below is: **directly observed trafficking** of a defined molecule; **genomic integration demonstrated by sequence/context and inheritance**; **phylogenetically inferred ancient HGT**; and, weakest of all, **a proposed HGT based mainly on unusual similarity or patchy taxonomic distribution**. Plant–parasite RNA exchange sits strongly in the first category; *Agrobacterium* natural transgenesis and several endogenous viral elements reach the second; many plant–fungus transfers necessarily rest on phylogenomic inference. [[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19584142/)

## Between parasitic plants and their hosts

- **The haustorium is the principal physical interface, but RNA movement and heritable DNA transfer need to be separated.** In stem-parasitic *Cuscuta* (dodder), haustorial tissue penetrates the host stem and establishes vascular continuity with host xylem and phloem. Anatomical work also found plasmodesmatal connections between parasite searching hyphae and host parenchyma, providing a symplastic route through which macromolecules can move. These connections make the haustorium an unusually intimate interspecific junction. They explain observed RNA and virus movement very well; however, the fact that ancient HGT genes are found in *Cuscuta* genomes does **not** by itself demonstrate that intact DNA was directly seen crossing a haustorium. The route for the historical DNA transfers remains inferred from the biology of the association. [[3]](https://doi.org/10.1093/jxb/erj076)

- **Host mRNAs can move into *Cuscuta*.** Roney and colleagues (2007) studied *Cuscuta pentagona* parasitizing tomato, *Solanum lycopersicum*. They detected the tomato transcript **LeGAI** in the parasite and subsequently found several additional host-derived transcripts. With pumpkin hosts, transcripts including **CmNACP, CmSUTP1,** and **CmWRKYP** were detected in attached dodder. A related study found tomato transcripts for the α- and β-subunits of pyrophosphate-dependent phosphofructokinase (**LePFP**), the small subunit of Rubisco, and **LeGAI** inside *Cuscuta*, with some host RNA detectable well away from the attachment point. These experiments establish interspecific **RNA trafficking**, not integration of those RNAs into the dodder genome. [[4]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1803716/)

- **The exchange is massively bidirectional at the transcript level.** Kim and colleagues (2014) performed transcriptome-scale experiments with *Cuscuta pentagona* attached to *Arabidopsis thaliana* and tomato. Thousands of mRNAs moved across the host–parasite interface in **both directions**; in the *Arabidopsis* interaction, transcripts representing a striking fraction of the expressed host transcriptome were detectable in parasite/interface samples. The important conceptual point is that parasitism creates a channel for extensive interspecific information flow, but the mobile transcripts are transient molecules rather than automatically heritable HGT events. [[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25124438/)

- **Small regulatory RNAs also cross the interface and can manipulate the other species.** Shahid and colleagues (2018) found that *Cuscuta campestris* produces haustorium-enriched microRNAs during parasitism of *Arabidopsis thaliana*. Several 22-nt parasite miRNAs enter host tissue, cleave host mRNAs, and trigger production of secondary siRNAs. Experimentally supported host targets included defense/development-associated genes such as **AFB2, AFB3, BIK1, TIR1,** and **SEOR1**. This is a particularly clear example of functional cross-species RNA transfer: the transferred genetic information regulates host genes, but the parasite miRNA sequence does not thereby become a stable part of the host chromosome. [[6]](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature25027)

- **Stable host-to-parasite nuclear HGT is nevertheless well established in both *Striga* and *Cuscuta*.** Yoshida and colleagues (2010) identified **ShContig9483** in the root parasite *Striga hermonthica*. Its sequence clustered unexpectedly with homologs from the host lineage represented by *Sorghum bicolor*, rather than with normal eudicot relatives of *Striga*. A run of adenines near the transferred sequence was interpreted as compatible with an mRNA/retroprocessing intermediate, making this an unusual candidate for RNA-mediated heritable HGT. It is fundamentally different from merely finding sorghum RNA inside a feeding parasite because the sequence resides in the *Striga* nuclear genome. [[7]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20508124/)

- **Many other parasitic-plant HGTs look more like DNA transfer than reverse-transcribed mRNA.** A broad Orobanchaceae analysis published in 2016 recovered 52 high-confidence transfers, and intron retention in many transferred genes favored movement of genomic DNA rather than processed RNA. Genome studies of *Orobanche minor* and *Aeginetia indica* likewise found numerous foreign genes, often with introns preserved in homologous positions; some transferred segments may have been much larger than a single coding sequence. Thus, the *Striga* retroprocessed-looking case should not be treated as the universal mechanism. [[8]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27791104/)

- ***Cuscuta* genomes contain numerous likely host-derived genes.** Particularly informative pre-2021 examples include an **albumin 1/KNOTTIN-like gene** inferred to have come from legumes and a **strictosidine synthase-like (SSL)** gene independently acquired from Brassicaceae-related donors. The *SSL* transfer was detected in *Cuscuta australis* and also in the parasitic *Orobanche aegyptiaca*; intron preservation supported a genomic-DNA route. An anthocyanin acyltransferase-like BAHD gene, often called **CaATL**, in *C. australis* was likewise inferred to have a Fabaceae origin. Later whole-genome studies of *C. campestris* expanded the inventory to dozens of high-confidence HGT candidates and showed that many are expressed, including genes enriched at haustoria. [[9]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24411025/)

- **The evolutionary direction is not absolutely one-way.** Host-to-parasite transfer is especially conspicuous in nuclear genomes because parasites repeatedly contact many host lineages, but Mower and colleagues (2004) reported the reverse pattern in mitochondria: defective **atp1** sequences in *Plantago* species were inferred to have been acquired from parasitic Orobanchaceae and Convolvulaceae/*Cuscuta*-related donors. This is evidence that intimate parasitic contact can, over evolutionary time, support gene movement in either direction. [[10]](https://www.nature.com/articles/432165b)

**Bottom line for parasitic plants:** the haustorium creates real vascular and symplastic continuity that demonstrably carries mRNAs, small RNAs, proteins, and even viruses. Heritable HGT is a rarer, separate outcome. In *Striga*, at least one famous nuclear transfer has features consistent with an RNA/cDNA intermediate; in *Cuscuta* and Orobanchaceae more broadly, intron-containing foreign genes strongly indicate that **genomic DNA transfer has also occurred repeatedly**. [[11]](https://doi.org/10.1093/jxb/erj076)

## Between fungi and plants

- **Plant–fungus evolutionary HGT is bidirectional, but apparently rare.** Richards and colleagues (2009) compared plant genomes with a broad set of fungal and other genomes and subjected candidates to phylogenetic tests. Of nine supported plant–fungus events, **five were inferred fungi→plant and four plant→fungus**. Significantly, none of the five fungal-to-plant recipients in that particular dataset was an angiosperm; the authors characterized plant–fungus HGT as infrequent. Their candidates included fungal-derived plant genes annotated as an **L-fucose permease**, a **major facilitator superfamily transporter**, a **phospholipase/carboxylesterase**, and an **iucA/iucC siderophore-biosynthesis protein**, principally in *Physcomitrella patens* and *Selaginella moellendorffii*. [[12]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19584142/)

- **An older and relatively compelling fungi→plant example involves a mitochondrial intron rather than an ordinary nuclear protein gene.** Vaughn and colleagues (1995) reported that the mitochondrial **cox1** gene of *Peperomia polybotrya* contains a group-I intron closely related to fungal introns. The approximately 1-kb element also carries an open reading frame compatible with a homing endonuclease, fitting the known mobility biology of group-I introns. Phylogenetic evidence favored acquisition from a fungal lineage. This case is useful because mobile introns have an intrinsic mechanism that makes horizontal movement mechanistically credible. [[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7490770/)

- **The most famous pre-2021 “fungal disease-resistance gene acquired by a plant” is *Fhb7*, but its HGT interpretation should be presented as a strong 2020 claim rather than an uncontested fact.** Wang and colleagues (2020) cloned **Fhb7** from *Thinopyrum elongatum*. The gene encodes a glutathione S-transferase (GST) that detoxifies Fusarium trichothecene toxins by de-epoxidation; when introgressed into wheat, the locus conferred resistance to Fusarium head blight and crown rot. The authors found close fungal homologs and proposed that *Fhb7* had entered *Thinopyrum* from an endophytic **Epichloë** fungus. [[14]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32273397/)

- **That particular evolutionary reconstruction was subsequently challenged.** Post-2020 comparative work identified *Fhb7*-related sequences more widely among Triticeae grasses than expected under the original, narrowly localized transfer scenario, creating debate about when and where the gene originated and whether all homologs have the same disease-resistance phenotype. Thus the biochemical role established for the 2020 *Fhb7* locus and the claim that its ancestry reflects a specific *Epichloë*→*Thinopyrum* HGT should be kept conceptually separate. The latter remains an active evolutionary interpretation rather than a universally settled case. [[15]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9416630/)

- **Other fungal-origin sequences in grasses strengthen the general plausibility of fungus→plant HGT without resolving *Fhb7*.** Shinozuka and colleagues (2020) identified two genes in cool-season grasses with very high similarity to sequences from **Epichloë** endophytes and inferred independent ancient transfers into grass lineages. Expression evidence indicated that at least some of these acquired genes are transcriptionally active. Such cases are phylogenomic reconstructions; no one observed a fungal gene physically enter the grass germ line. [[16]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33199756/)

- **Plant→fungus HGT has its own named candidates.** Armijos Jaramillo and colleagues (2013) inferred that an S8A subtilisin gene, designated **CPLS** (“Colletotrichum plant-like subtilisin”), passed from a plant lineage into an ancestor of *Colletotrichum*. The gene is expressed in *Colletotrichum graminicola* during maize infection, suggesting—but not proving—that the acquired plant-like protease contributes to host interaction or pathogenicity. The evidence for direction comes from phylogenetic placement and distribution rather than a directly observed transfer event. [[17]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0059078)

- **A second plant→fungus candidate is a leucine-rich-repeat gene in cereal pathogens.** A 2013 analysis of *Pyrenophora teres* and *P. tritici-repentis* identified a **leucine-rich repeat (LRR) protein gene** most closely related to homologs from barley, *Hordeum vulgare*, and interpreted it as host→fungus HGT. Because functional and expression data were limited, this should be described as a credible phylogenomic candidate, not as experimentally demonstrated movement during infection. [[18]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3612039/)

- **The 2009 bidirectionality study also supplied plant→fungus candidates**, including a **zinc-binding alcohol dehydrogenase**, a **phosphate-responsive 1-family protein**, and other plant-like sequences in fungi including *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*, *Botrytis cinerea*, and *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*. As with most ancient interkingdom cases, phylogenetic reconstruction must exclude alternatives such as differential gene loss, contamination, hidden paralogy, and poor taxon sampling. The paper itself therefore treated HGT as rare rather than assuming every plant-like fungal gene represented transfer. [[19]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19584142/)

**Bottom line for fungi and plants:** evolutionary transfers appear **bidirectional**, but there is much less direct mechanistic evidence than in *Agrobacterium* or *Cuscuta* RNA trafficking. The strongest cases combine robust phylogenetics with genomic context and expression; claims based mainly on surprising sequence similarity warrant more caution. *Fhb7* is biologically important and experimentally validated as a resistance locus, but its exact HGT history is more debated than the headline “fungus gave wheat a resistance gene” suggests. [[20]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19584142/)

## Between bacteria and plants

- ***Agrobacterium* is the clearest mechanistically understood natural DNA-delivery system in plants.** Pathogenic strains carry a tumor-inducing (Ti) or root-inducing (Ri) plasmid. The DNA actually transferred is the **T-DNA**, defined by border repeats; bacterial **vir** genes generally remain in the bacterium and encode the machinery that processes and exports the T-DNA. Natural T-DNAs typically carry genes that alter plant hormone physiology and/or direct production of opines, helping create the gall or hairy-root niche exploited by the bacterium. [[21]](https://www.nature.com/articles/301348a0?error=cookies_not_supported&code=ae3ea9d6-df39-4c44-8b83-577250376b72)

- **Signal perception and T-DNA processing begin at a wounded plant site.** Wounded plant cells release phenolics, sugars, and acidic signals; the phenolic **acetosyringone** is a canonical inducer. The membrane sensor kinase **VirA** activates the response regulator **VirG**, which turns on other virulence operons. **VirD1/VirD2** then act at the T-DNA border repeats: VirD2 nicks the borders and remains covalently attached to the 5′ end of the resulting single-stranded **T-strand**. [[22]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6717549/)

- **Export uses a bacterial type-IV secretion system.** Proteins **VirB1–VirB11** together with the coupling protein **VirD4** assemble the type-IV secretion apparatus that transports the T-strand/VirD2 complex and virulence effector proteins into the plant cell. **VirE2**, a single-stranded-DNA-binding effector, coats or associates with the transferred DNA and participates in protection and intracellular/nuclear trafficking. The process therefore resembles a dedicated molecular injection system rather than passive uptake of naked bacterial DNA. [[23]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6717549/)

- **Chromosomal integration is largely completed by plant DNA-repair machinery.** T-DNA does not encode its own canonical chromosomal integrase. Instead, transferred DNA becomes captured at plant DNA breaks through host repair processes. In *Arabidopsis thaliana*, van Kregten and colleagues (2016) found that DNA polymerase theta (**Pol θ**, encoded by **TEBICHI/POLQ**) was critical for efficient random T-DNA integration in their system, and junction structures were consistent with polymerase-theta-mediated end joining. The broader lesson is that *Agrobacterium* supplies the DNA and transfer machinery, while the recipient plant contributes much of the final integration chemistry. [[24]](https://www.nature.com/articles/nplants2016164)

- **Natural “transgenic plants” were known decades before the sweet-potato discovery.** White and colleagues (1983) found *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* T-DNA-homologous sequences in apparently uninfected **Nicotiana glauca**. Subsequent work showed that the chromosomal T-DNA (**cT-DNA**) is an inherited genomic element rather than an active bacterial infection. Genes reported from *N. glauca* cT-DNA include **rolB, rolC, ORF13,** and **ORF14**; some are transcribed in genetic tumors of *N. glauca × N. langsdorffii* hybrids. [[25]](https://www.nature.com/articles/301348a0?error=cookies_not_supported&code=ae3ea9d6-df39-4c44-8b83-577250376b72)

- **Another natural cT-DNA case occurs in *Linaria vulgaris*.** Work published in 2012 identified homologs of **rolB, rolC, ORF13, ORF14,** and **mis** arranged as ancient integrated T-DNA. Their persistence demonstrates past bacterial→plant HGT even though expression was not detected under the tested conditions. [[26]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23134518/)

- **Sweet potato is the landmark crop example.** Kyndt and colleagues (2015) reported two *Agrobacterium*-derived regions, **IbT-DNA1** and **IbT-DNA2**, in the genome of cultivated sweet potato, *Ipomoea batatas*. IbT-DNA1 contains homologs of **iaaM** (tryptophan-2-monooxygenase), **iaaH** (indole-3-acetamide hydrolase), **C-prot**, and **Acs** (agrocinopine synthase). These are not laboratory transformation constructs: the sequences occur as part of the plant genome and some T-DNA genes are transcriptionally expressed. [[27]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25902487/)

- **Population distribution makes the sweet-potato result especially striking.** In the 2015 survey, **IbT-DNA1 was detected in all 291 cultivated accessions tested** but not in the sampled close wild relatives, a pattern consistent with insertion before or during the lineage leading to domesticated sweet potato followed by retention during cultivation. **IbT-DNA2**, by contrast, was present in a subset of cultivated and wild *Ipomoea* accessions. Its ORFs include homologs related to **ORF14, ORF17n, RolB/RolC, ORF13,** and **ORF18/ORF17n**. PCR, genomic sequencing, Southern-type analyses, and genomic-context evidence together supported genuine plant-chromosome integration rather than bacterial contamination. [[28]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25902487/)

- **What the sweet-potato case does and does not prove.** It proves that natural *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation can enter the hereditary genome of a plant lineage and persist in an important food crop. It does **not** establish that the bacterial insert caused sweet-potato domestication; the ubiquity of IbT-DNA1 in cultivated material makes such an evolutionary role intriguing, but causation requires functional evidence beyond association. [[28]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25902487/)

**Bottom line for bacteria and plants:** unlike most ancient plant–fungus HGT, *Agrobacterium* provides a near-complete mechanistic chain from donor DNA processing to cell-to-cell delivery. Natural cT-DNAs in *Nicotiana*, *Linaria*, and especially *Ipomoea batatas* show that the same basic process exploited in biotechnology has repeatedly happened without human intervention. [[29]](https://www.nature.com/articles/301348a0?error=cookies_not_supported&code=ae3ea9d6-df39-4c44-8b83-577250376b72)

## Between viruses and plants

- **Yes—viral genes can remain in plant genomes after infection, but this is not the normal outcome of every infection.** A viral sequence becomes an **endogenous viral element (EVE)** only if viral DNA, or a DNA copy of viral RNA, is incorporated into a plant chromosome and survives in a cell lineage that contributes to reproduction. Many infections stay entirely episomal and leave no heritable viral sequence. Thus “virus infected the plant” and “virus became part of the plant genome” are distinct statements. [[30]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8570630/)

- **DNA viruses provide some of the earliest examples.** Bejarano and colleagues (1996) described multiple repeats of **geminivirus-related DNA** in the nuclear genome of *Nicotiana tabacum*. The integrated material included sequences related to the viral replication region, including an **AL1/Rep-like gene** and origin-associated DNA. Related endogenous elements in *Nicotiana* relatives supported an ancient germline integration event rather than contamination by an ongoing infection. [[31]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8570630/)

- **Pararetroviruses are particularly important because they make DNA during replication even though integration is not obligatory.** Banana streak viruses (BSVs; family Caulimoviridae, genus *Badnavirus*) occur as integrated sequences in **Musa** genomes. Ndowora and colleagues (1999) showed that endogenous badnavirus sequences in *Musa* could give rise to active infection, establishing that a chromosomal viral relic can sometimes regenerate episomal virus. Later work on *Musa balbisiana* distinguished different endogenous BSV alleles and showed that only some integrated configurations are infectious. [[32]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10069946/)

- **The DNA-versus-RNA-virus distinction is mechanistically important.** Geminiviruses already replicate through DNA, and plant pararetroviruses generate DNA by reverse transcription, so their sequences can in principle be captured by chromosomal DNA-repair pathways. Ordinary positive-sense RNA viruses such as potyviruses have no obligatory DNA stage or viral integrase. For them, stable endogenization requires an external source of reverse-transcriptase activity, plausibly supplied by plant retrotransposons or related retroelements. [[33]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15680426/)

- **A striking RNA-virus example is Potato virus Y-related DNA in grapevine.** Tanne and Sela (2005) reported a chromosomal sequence related to a **Potato virus Y (PVY) coat-protein cistron** in grapevine (*Vitis*), and found that the integrated sequence was expressed. They proposed recombination between viral and host/retroelement RNA followed by reverse transcription and integration. Thus even a non-retro RNA virus can leave a genomic “fossil,” but it needs host-associated reverse-transcription machinery because its normal life cycle does not make DNA. [[33]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15680426/)

- **Viruses can also carry pieces of host DNA, providing a plausible route toward plant-to-plant HGT.** A particularly informative 2018 study of **Beet curly top Iran virus (BCTIV)** in *Beta vulgaris* found that infection could generate hybrid circular molecules containing virus plus captured plant chromosomal DNA. These minicircles replicated and could be encapsidated; in coinfection experiments, captured *B. vulgaris* sequences could reach other BCTIV-susceptible plants and be transcribed there. This directly demonstrates several necessary steps for a viral “shuttle” mechanism—host-DNA capture, packaging/movement, entry into another plant, and expression. [[34]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-07775-w)

- **However, that 2018 result stopped short of demonstrating complete natural HGT.** The study did **not** establish stable incorporation of the transported beet DNA into a recipient species’ chromosomes followed by germline transmission. It therefore provides a mechanistic bridge toward virus-mediated plant-to-plant HGT, not proof that a host gene was naturally transferred and evolutionarily fixed in another plant species by that route. This evidentiary distinction is crucial. [[35]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-07775-w)

- **Experimental viral vectors show what viruses can do, but they should not be cited as natural HGT without additional evidence.** Cauliflower mosaic virus (**CaMV**) can recombine with homologous viral sequences engineered into plant chromosomes; experiments in transgenic *Brassica* and *Nicotiana* generated viable recombinant viruses, in some cases with altered biological properties. Such experiments demonstrate powerful recombination and gene-delivery capacities, but because the donor sequence was deliberately introduced, they are evidence for **experimental gene transfer**, not for an independently documented natural plant-to-plant HGT event. [[36]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1546451/)

- **Parasitic plants can even provide a physical bridge for virus transmission between otherwise separate plants.** Experiments with *Cuscuta* showed that Potato virus Y could pass from one host through the parasite to another host because the dodder establishes functional phloem connections. This is a vivid example of a parasite facilitating viral movement between plants, but it is still **virus transmission**, not horizontal transfer of a host chromosomal gene unless the virus additionally captures host DNA and that DNA becomes heritably integrated in the recipient. [[37]](https://doi.org/10.1093/jxb/erj076)

**Bottom line for viruses and plants:** plant genomes contain genuine inherited remnants of DNA viruses, pararetroviruses, and even some non-retro RNA viruses. Viruses can also capture and transport host DNA, so they are mechanistically credible HGT vectors. By 2020, however, the evidence was much stronger for **viral endogenization** and **experimental or intermediate-stage DNA shuttling** than for a fully demonstrated natural chain in which a virus took a functional nuclear gene from plant A, delivered it to plant B, integrated it into B's germ line, and established it evolutionarily. [[38]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8570630/)

## Conclusions

Across the four systems, the strongest pre-2021 evidence supports a spectrum rather than one universal HGT mechanism. **Parasitic plants** provide the clearest case of sustained interspecific macromolecule trafficking: haustorial vascular and plasmodesmatal connections permit extensive mRNA and small-RNA movement, while genome sequences show that a much smaller subset of foreign DNA—or occasionally apparently retroprocessed RNA—has become permanently inherited. *Cuscuta*, *Striga*, *Orobanche*, and *Aeginetia* collectively show that parasitism can create repeated opportunities for such transfer. [[39]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25124438/)

**Plant–fungus HGT is bidirectional but comparatively rare and often reconstructed retrospectively.** The 2009 phylogenomic analysis provided evidence for both fungi→plant and plant→fungus transfers; particular cases such as the *Peperomia* mitochondrial **cox1** intron are mechanistically plausible, while the celebrated 2020 **Fhb7** resistance-gene story should be reported with the qualification that its precise evolutionary origin has subsequently been questioned. [[40]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19584142/)

**Agrobacterium represents the most mechanistically resolved bacterial route.** VirA/VirG signaling, VirD-mediated T-strand generation, VirB/VirD4 type-IV secretion, and host-assisted chromosomal integration explain how bacterial T-DNA can become plant DNA. Natural cT-DNAs in *Nicotiana glauca*, *Linaria vulgaris*, and *Ipomoea batatas* show that natural transgenesis is an evolutionary reality, not merely a laboratory technology. The sweet-potato **IbT-DNA1** genes **iaaM, iaaH, C-prot,** and **Acs** are among the clearest crop examples. [[41]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6717549/)

Finally, **viruses leave evolutionary fossils and can act as genetic shuttles**, but the evidentiary threshold matters. Endogenous geminivirus DNA in *Nicotiana*, infectious endogenous badnaviral sequences in *Musa*, and PVY-related DNA in grapevine establish genuine virus→plant genomic incorporation. BCTIV experiments show that a plant virus can capture, package, move, and express host chromosomal DNA in another plant, yet stable germline integration in the recipient was not demonstrated. Consequently, as of the pre-2021 literature, viruses were strongly supported as potential HGT vehicles and experimentally effective vectors, but natural virus-mediated **heritable plant-to-plant gene transfer** remained less conclusively demonstrated than *Agrobacterium* transgenesis or parasitic-plant genomic HGT. [[38]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8570630/)
