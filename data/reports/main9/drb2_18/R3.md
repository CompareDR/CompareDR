# Horizontal Gene Transfer in the Plant World: Transfers via Parasitism, Symbiosis, and Pathogenesis (Pre-2021 Literature)

## TL;DR

- **HGT is real and widespread in plants**, and the most dramatic, best-documented cases arise through **intimate physical contact** — parasitic haustoria (dodder, witchweed, Rafflesia, Balanophoraceae), *Agrobacterium* T-DNA transfer (the sweet potato is a natural GMO), viral endogenization (banana streak virus in the banana B genome), and mitochondrial capture (the *Amborella* mitogenome is ~86% foreign). Plants tolerate HGT partly because they **lack a sequestered germline (no Weismann barrier)**, so genes entering somatic cells can reach the next generation.
- **Direction depends on the partnership**: parasite↔host exchange is bidirectional (mRNA, small RNA, DNA, whole mitochondrial genomes); fungus↔plant transfer is bidirectional (fungal β-1,6-glucanase and *Fhb7* GST into grasses; plant subtilisin into *Colletotrichum*); bacteria→plant is dominated by *Agrobacterium* T-DNA; virus→plant leaves integrated viral elements, but the idea of viruses shuttling *host* genes between plant species remains largely hypothetical.
- **Evidence rests on phylogenetic incongruence, high sequence identity between distant taxa, intron presence/absence, and shared insertion sites** — but the field is dogged by **contamination artifacts and weak short-sequence phylogenies**, so rigorous cases demand multiple independent lines of proof (e.g., foreign and native genes on the same contig, plus expression).

## Key Findings

- **Parasitic plants are the richest source of plant HGT.** *Cuscuta* exchanges genomic-scale mRNA with hosts (nearly half the *Arabidopsis* expressed transcriptome — about 9,518 genes — was detected in the parasite) and carries 108 probably functional horizontally acquired genes; *Striga* captured a nuclear gene from sorghum; Rafflesiaceae and the holoparasite *Lophophytum mirabile* have had their mitochondrial gene space largely taken over by host DNA.
- **The sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) is a naturally transgenic food crop**, carrying expressed *Agrobacterium* T-DNA (with *iaaM*, *iaaH*, *C-prot*, *Acs* and *rol*-family genes) in every one of 291 cultivars tested — a landmark for the GMO debate.
- **Fungus↔plant HGT is genuinely bidirectional and adaptively important**, exemplified by the fungal-derived Fusarium-resistance gene *Fhb7* now bred into wheat.
- **Viruses routinely integrate into plant genomes** (endogenous pararetroviruses, non-retroviral RNA-virus fragments, geminivirus-related DNA), and some integrants (banana, petunia, tobacco) can reactivate into infectious virus — but virus-mediated *interspecies* transfer of host genes is not well established.

## Details

### Introduction: What HGT Is and Why It Was Thought Rare in Plants

- **Definition.** Horizontal (lateral) gene transfer is the movement and genomic integration of genetic material between species by means other than normal sexual reproduction. It is distinct from vertical inheritance and from intracellular gene transfer between a cell's own compartments (e.g., plastid-to-nucleus).
- **Historically thought rare in eukaryotes.** HGT is pervasive in prokaryotes but was long considered negligible in complex multicellular eukaryotes. The key barrier cited is the **Weismann barrier**: in most animals the germline is sequestered early, so a gene acquired by a somatic cell cannot be transmitted. **Plants have no sequestered germline** — gametes arise late from meristematic tissue — so a foreign gene integrated into a somatic cell that later gives rise to reproductive tissue can be inherited. This makes plant HGT mechanistically more plausible than animal HGT.
- **Additional plant-specific facilitators.** Direct tissue contact through parasitic haustoria, natural grafting, epiphytic/wound contact, illegitimate pollination, and vectors such as bacteria, fungi, and viruses. Plant mitochondria are especially receptive because they fuse/divide and take up DNA, which is why the earliest documented plant HGT cases were mitochondrial.
- **Detection methods** (detailed in the Conclusion): phylogenetic incongruence, anomalous nucleotide composition/codon usage, patchy taxonomic distribution, and shared insertion sites.

### 1. HGT Between Parasitic Plants and Their Hosts

**Anatomical route.** Parasitic plants attach via the **haustorium**, which penetrates host tissue and forms a continuum with host **xylem and phloem**, enabling symplastic (plasmodesmata-mediated) and apoplastic exchange of water, nutrients, proteins, mRNA, small RNAs, and DNA.

**Cuscuta (dodder) — stem holoparasites**

- *Cuscuta* spp. (*Cuscuta pentagona*, *C. campestris*, *C. australis*) coil around host stems and form haustoria connecting to host xylem and phloem.
- **Genomic-scale mRNA exchange.** Kim, LeBlanc, Wafula, dePamphilis & Westwood (*Science*, 2014) showed bidirectional mRNA movement between *C. pentagona* and hosts *Arabidopsis thaliana* and tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*). Mobile transcripts numbered in the thousands; specifically, **9,518 *Arabidopsis* genes (~45% of its expressed transcriptome) and 347 tomato genes (1.6%) were detected in *Cuscuta***, with *Arabidopsis* reads averaging ~1.1% of mapped reads in the parasite. Mobile host mRNAs can be translated to protein in *C. campestris*, with tRNA-like structures enhancing mobility.
- **The SHOOT MERISTEMLESS (STM) case.** Alakonya et al. (*Plant Cell*, 2012) found that haustorium formation in *C. pentagona* on tobacco (*Nicotiana tabacum*) is accompanied by upregulation of dodder KNOX transcription factors, including a **SHOOT MERISTEMLESS-like (STM)** gene. Host plants engineered to express an RNAi construct against *Cuscuta STM* transmitted small RNAs into the parasite, silencing *STM* and disrupting haustorial development — a demonstration of trans-specific small-RNA movement across the haustorial junction.
- **Functional HGT genes in Cuscuta.** Yang, Wafula, dePamphilis et al. (*Nature Plants*, 2019) identified **108 transcribed, probably functional HGT events** in *C. campestris* and relatives, plus 42 additional host-derived transposon/pseudogene/non-coding regions; genome sequencing supported transfer of genomic DNA (not retroprocessed RNA) as the mechanism. Strikingly, **18 *Cuscuta* functional HGTs came from the same gene families as independent HGTs in Orobanchaceae** (convergent HGT). One case involved a horizontally transferred **leucine-rich repeat protein kinase** overlapping a microRNA that regulates host genes (Shahid et al., *Nature*, 2018, showed *C. campestris* miRNAs target host mRNAs).
- **Strictosidine synthase-like (SSL).** Zhang et al. (*BMC Plant Biology*, 2014) showed *C. australis* (and the root parasite *Orobanche aegyptiaca*) independently acquired **Brassicaceae-specific strictosidine synthase-like (SSL)** genes by HGT.
- **Mitochondrial caveat.** Assembly of the *C. australis* and *C. campestris* mitogenomes found little to no clear evidence of HGT *into* the *Cuscuta* mitogenome — a reminder that HGT is not uniform across genomic compartments even in parasites; historically *Cuscuta* was known to donate a mitochondrial gene to hosts and to acquire nuclear genes.

**Striga (witchweed) and Orobanchaceae — root parasites**

- **Striga hermonthica / Sorghum.** Yoshida, Maruyama, Nozaki & Shirasu (*Science*, 2010) reported the first clear nuclear HGT into a parasitic plant: the gene **ShContig9483**, present in the eudicot parasite *S. hermonthica*, is a nuclear monocot gene most similar to a *Sorghum bicolor* gene (with lesser similarity to *Oryza sativa*) and absent from other eudicots — captured from its grass host. The gene is intronless in the parasite, consistent with an mRNA-derived transfer.
- **Striga gesnerioides SHR4z.** Su, Wafula, Honaas, dePamphilis, Timko et al. (*New Phytologist*, 2020) described **SHR4z (Suppressor of Host Resistance 4z)**, a secreted leucine-rich-repeat "decoy" effector from the haustorium of *S. gesnerioides* race SG4z that is delivered into cowpea (*Vigna unguiculata*) cells, [PubMed][ref-01] where it triggers turnover of the host E3 ubiquitin ligase **VuPOB1** to suppress immunity. (This is effector-protein delivery/virulence rather than genomic HGT, but it illustrates haustorial macromolecular trafficking, the same conduit HGT exploits.)
- **Striga asiatica genome.** Yoshida et al. (*Current Biology*, 2019) sequenced *S. asiatica* and found HGT of host genes and **retrotransposons**: ~29 *S. asiatica* reverse-transcriptase sequences cluster with 48 *Sorghum bicolor* rt sequences, indicating direct transfer from the host followed by amplification in the parasite genome; additional trees implicate *Oryza* and *Zea mays*.
- **Orobanchaceae functional HGT survey.** Yang, Zhang, Wafula, dePamphilis et al. (*PNAS*, 2016) analyzed *S. hermonthica*, *Phelipanche aegyptiaca* (*=Orobanche aegyptiaca*), *Triphysaria versicolor*, and the free-living control *Lindenbergia*. They detected **52 high-confidence HGT events**, increasing with degree of parasitic dependence; most were DNA-mediated genomic transfers, and the Brassicaceae SSL genes retained introns in both donor and parasite (confirming direct genomic transfer). Evolution of the horizontally acquired legume **albumin 1 (KNOTTIN-like)** gene in *Phelipanche aegyptiaca* and relatives was documented by Zhang et al. (*BMC Evolutionary Biology*, 2013). A Penn State dissertation reported two adjacent high-confidence HGT transgenes in *S. hermonthica* — the first genomic integration longer than one gene identified in a parasitic plant.

**Rafflesiaceae (Rafflesia, Sapria) and Tetrastigma hosts — endoparasites**

- Rafflesiaceae are endophytic holoparasites of *Tetrastigma* (Vitaceae) vines; parasite and host are separated by ~115 million years of evolution.
- **Nuclear HGT.** Xi et al. (*BMC Genomics*, 2012) found that in *Rafflesia cantleyi*, **49 transcripts (2.1% of observed transcripts)** were likely host-derived — versus only 13 (0.8%) in the host *Tetrastigma rafflesiae* — and **31 of 47 putative HGTs were confirmed in genomic DNA**; many vertically inherited genes even converged on host-like codon usage.
- **Massive mitochondrial HGT.** Xi et al. (*PLOS Genetics*, 2013) analyzed 38 mitochondrial genes across *Rafflesia cantleyi*, *R. tuan-mudae*, and *Sapria himalayana* and concluded that **24%–41% of these gene sequences show evidence of HGT** (depending on species); assembled contigs contained both transgenes and native genes, proving genomic integration. Barkman et al. (*PNAS*, 2004) and Davis & Wurdack (2004) earlier showed mitochondrial loci (e.g., *nad1B-C*) group Rafflesiaceae with *Tetrastigma* rather than with Malpighiales relatives — the classic phylogenetic-incongruence signal.
- **Plastid genome loss.** Rafflesiaceae have undergone complete plastid genome elimination (Molina et al., 2014), and nuclear plastid-targeted DNA-repair/replication genes are extensively lost.
- **Sapria himalayana genome.** Cai et al. (*Current Biology*, 2020) documented deeply altered genome architecture with abundant host-derived HGT in both genic and intergenic regions.

**Mitochondrial gene/genome transfer in non-parasites and other parasites**

- **Amborella trichopoda.** Rice, Palmer et al. (*Science*, 2013) found the 3.9-Mb *Amborella* mitogenome contains **six genome-equivalents of foreign mitochondrial DNA** — as Palmer summarized, "the native genes are outnumbered by the foreign genes six to one" — including essentially **entire mitochondrial genomes from three green algae and one moss donor** (later shown to be a chimera of two moss whole-genome transfers), plus genes from other angiosperms. Their fusion-compatibility model posits whole-mitochondria capture (aided by *Amborella*'s epiphyte load and wound-suckering), then mitochondrial fusion and recombination. Bergthorsson, Adams & Palmer (*PNAS*, 2004) earlier documented multiple mitochondrial gene acquisitions from mosses and angiosperms.
- **Lophophytum mirabile (Balanophoraceae).** Sanchez-Puerta et al. (*New Phytologist*, 2017; *Mol. Phylogenet. Evol.*, 2019) reported the most extreme eudicot case: **34 of 43 mitochondrial protein genes** were acquired from mimosoid legume hosts (Fabaceae), with replacement of up to 26 native homologs; ~60% of the mtDNA has Fabaceae affinity (~96% sequence identity). Related transfers occur in *Ombrophytum subterraneum*, including from Asteraceae, and these foreign genes dominate the functional mitochondrial gene space (2020).
- **Plantago and Bartsia.** *Plantago* mitogenomes show elevated substitution rates and host-related HGT, and these genera featured in the broader Mower/Palmer surveys documenting frequent, phylogenetically local mitochondrial HGT (including Mower et al., *BMC Biology*, 2010, on multiple mitochondrial genes acquired by a host from a parasitic plant, followed by gene conversion).

**Transposable element transfer between parasites and hosts**

- Beyond the *Striga asiatica*–*Sorghum* retrotransposon transfer above, plant-to-plant TE transfer is documented by Diao, Freeling & Lisch (*PLoS Biology*, 2006): a **MULE (Mutator-like element)** shared between *Setaria* and rice (*Oryza sativa*) that is too similar to be vertically inherited given divergence time — described as the first well-documented horizontal transfer of a nuclear-encoded transposon between higher plants.

**Evidence types used across these cases:** phylogenetic incongruence (host-nested gene trees), high sequence identity between distantly related taxa, intron presence/absence (DNA vs. RNA-mediated transfer), and shared insertion sites/contigs containing both foreign and native genes.

### 2. HGT Between Fungi and Plants

**Directionality: bidirectional.** HGT occurs from fungi into plants, from plants into fungi, and extensively fungus-to-fungus.

**Fungus-to-plant**

- **Moss Physcomitrella/Physcomitrium patens.** Yue et al. (*Nature Communications*, 2012) identified **57 families of nuclear genes** in the *P. patens* genome acquired from prokaryotes, fungi, or viruses; many were transferred to ancestors of green/land plants [Nature][ref-02] and relate to development and stress. Two large genomic regions of mostly fungi-specific genes were later found in *P. patens*, suggesting HGT of large fragments possibly contributing to defense against microbial pathogens.
- **Fungal-derived genes in grasses.** Shinozuka et al. (*Scientific Reports*, 2017) reported horizontal transfer of a fungal **β-1,6-glucanase** gene (plant copy **LpBGNL**) from an ancestral *Epichloë* endophyte (related to *Epichloë festucae* var. *lolii*) into the cool-season grass perennial ryegrass (*Lolium perenne*) — described as the first evidence of a nuclear gene of fungal origin entering an angiosperm lineage. Shinozuka et al. (*Scientific Reports*, 2020) then found **two *Epichloë*-originated genes** in cereal and pasture grass genomes (Poeae and Triticeae tribes, including wheat *Triticum aestivum* and barley *Hordeum vulgare*), with **96% and 85% DNA identity** to the *Epichloë* genes, transferred independently over the last ~39 million years.
- **Fhb7 in wheatgrass.** Wang et al. (*Science*, 2020) cloned the Fusarium head blight resistance gene **Fhb7** from *Thinopyrum elongatum* (mapped to a 245-kb region); it encodes a glutathione S-transferase (GST) that detoxifies trichothecenes by de-epoxidation. Fhb7 GST homologs are "absent in the plant kingdom," while a sequence "~97% identity with Fhb7 was found in endophytic fungi of an *Epichloë* species" — evidence it was acquired by HGT from an endophytic *Epichloë* fungus, then introgressed into cultivated wheat by conventional breeding.
- **Neochrome (plant-to-plant, noted for context).** Li et al. (2014) showed ferns acquired the chimeric red/blue photoreceptor **neochrome** from hornworts (a bryophyte lineage) via HGT ~179 Mya — a plant-to-plant transfer included by contrast with the fungus-to-plant cases.

**Plant-to-fungus**

- **Colletotrichum subtilisin.** Armijos Jaramillo, Vargas, Sukno & Thon (*PLoS ONE*, 2013) showed the plant-pathogenic fungal genus *Colletotrichum* acquired a plant-like **subtilisin** (serine protease) gene from a plant ancestor ~150–155 Mya, potentially enabling molecular mimicry during infection.
- **Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus Rhizophagus irregularis.** Li et al. (*Frontiers in Plant Science*, 2018) found *R. irregularis* acquired genes from plants and bacteria (in gene regulation, mitosis, and signal transduction) contributing to its symbiotic lifestyle.
- **Ectomycorrhizal Amanita.** ACC deaminase genes transferred to *Amanita* ECM fungi are upregulated in symbiosis and may modulate host immunity (Wang et al., 2020).

**Fungus-to-fungus (the ToxA case, included as requested for context)**

- **ToxA.** The wheat virulence effector gene **ToxA** was horizontally transferred among three fungal wheat pathogens — *Parastagonospora* (*Stagonospora*) *nodorum*, *Pyrenophora tritici-repentis*, and *Bipolaris sorokiniana* — as part of a conserved ~14-kb element [ScienceDirect][ref-03] (named **ToxhAT**, a Type II DNA transposon), with >92% nucleotide identity indicating a recent transfer; [ScienceDirect][ref-03] *P. nodorum* is the likely ultimate source [bioRxiv][ref-04] (Friesen et al., 2006; McDonald et al., 2018; the *mBio* 2019 ToxhAT study).
- **Colletotrichum–Magnaporthales.** Qiu et al. (*BMC Biology*, 2016) found >90 genes (33 high-confidence) transferred between *Magnaporthales* (including rice blast *Pyricularia/Magnaporthe oryzae*) and *Colletotrichum*, enriched in plant-cell-wall-degrading carbohydrate-active enzymes.

### 3. HGT Between Bacteria and Plants

**Agrobacterium T-DNA transfer mechanism (the archetype of interkingdom DNA transfer)**

- *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* (crown gall) and *A. rhizogenes* (hairy root) transfer a segment of DNA (**T-DNA**) from their **Ti/Ri plasmid** into the plant nuclear genome.
- **Mechanism:** Plant wound signals induce **vir (virulence) genes**. The **VirD1–VirD2** endonuclease complex nicks the **T-DNA border repeats** (25-bp imperfect direct repeats); **VirD2** remains covalently attached to the 5′ end of the released single-stranded T-strand. The VirD2–T-strand complex is exported through a **VirB/VirD4 type IV secretion system** (T4SS; 11 VirB proteins + VirD4) alongside effector proteins (**VirE2, VirE3, VirD5, VirF**). In the plant, **VirE2** (a single-stranded-DNA-binding protein) coats and protects the T-strand; VirD2 and VirE2 nuclear-localization signals pilot it into the nucleus, where it integrates (largely via non-homologous end joining). T-DNA carries genes for auxin/cytokinin biosynthesis (tumor/root formation) and **opine synthesis**, which the bacterium consumes.

**Naturally transgenic plants — Sweet potato (the flagship case)**

- **Kyndt et al. (*PNAS*, 2015).**
  
   The cultivated hexaploid sweet potato 
  
  *Ipomoea batatas*
  
   genome contains two
  
  *Agrobacterium*
  
   T-DNA regions:
  
  - **IbT-DNA1:** four intact ORFs homologous to **tryptophan-2-monooxygenase (iaaM)**, **indole-3-acetamide hydrolase (iaaH)**, **C-protein (C-prot)**, and **agrocinopine synthase (Acs)**, plus a truncated inverted *iaaM*. It is inserted into an intron of an F-box gene in a transcriptionally active region, and was detected in **all 291** cultivated hexaploid accessions tested but absent from close wild relatives — consistent with *A. rhizogenes*-type origin.
  - **IbT-DNA2:** at least five ORFs homologous to **ORF14, ORF17n, RolB/RolC family, ORF13, and ORF18/ORF17n**. Detected in 42 of the cultigens.
  - Both loci are **expressed** in sweet potato tissues. The authors frame this as an ancient natural transformation event and emphasize its significance for the GMO debate — a widely and traditionally consumed staple food crop that is "naturally transgenic."
- **Follow-up (Scientific Reports, 2019):** IbT-DNA1 and IbT-DNA2 also occur in tetraploid *I. batatas* and related species (*I. cordatotriloba*, *I. tenuissima*, *I. trifida*), showing the transfer predates hexaploid domestication.

**Other naturally transgenic plants**

- **Nicotiana (tobacco).** The first naturally transgenic plants were found in *Nicotiana* (White et al., *Nature*, 1982/1983). *N. glauca* carries a cellular T-DNA (**cT-DNA**) organized as an imperfect inverted repeat containing homologs **NgrolB (orf11), NgrolC (orf12), Ngorf13, Ngorf14, NgrolD (orf15)**, and a **mikimopine synthase (mis)** gene similar to *A. rhizogenes* pRi1724. *N. tomentosiformis* (a tobacco progenitor) carries multiple T-DNA inserts (Chen, Otten et al., *Plant J.*, 2014).
- **Linaria vulgaris.** Matveeva et al. (*MPMI*, 2012) documented natural *Agrobacterium*-to-*Linaria* HGT (including *rolC* homologs).
- **Widespread survey.** Matveeva & Otten (*Plant Molecular Biology*, 2019) reported cT-DNA in **23 of 275 dicot species examined**, within the genera ***Eutrema, Arachis, Nissolia, Quillaja, Euphorbia, Parasponia, Trema, Humulus, Psidium, Eugenia, Juglans, Azadirachta, Silene, Dianthus, Vaccinium, Camellia, and Cuscuta***, concluding that "**at least 7% of the dicotyledonous species are naturally transformed plants.**" A 2020 update added further genera (*Aeschynomene, Eperua, Eucalyptus, Boswellia, Nyssa, Populus*, etc.).

**Non-Agrobacterium bacteria-to-plant HGT**

- The *Physcomitrella patens* survey (Yue et al., 2012) attributed many of the 57 acquired gene families to prokaryotic donors, indicating ancient bacteria-to-plant transfers beyond *Agrobacterium*. An insect-resistance gene in the fern *Azolla* was likely acquired from bacteria.

### 4. HGT Between Viruses and Plants

**Do viruses leave genes in plant genomes?** Yes — chiefly via endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs), plus integrated fragments of non-retroviral RNA viruses and geminivirus-related DNA.

**Endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs; family Caulimoviridae)**

- **Endogenous banana streak virus (eBSV) in Musa balbisiana.** BSV (a badnavirus) sequences are integrated mainly in the **B genome** derived from *M. balbisiana*. Gayral et al. (*Journal of Virology*, 2008) showed the wild diploid cv. Pisang Klutuk Wulung (PKW) contains eBSV of BSV Goldfinger (BSGfV) as rearranged tandem repeats that nonetheless include a **full-length viral genome**; under stress (tissue culture, interspecific crossing) the integrant recombines to release **infectious virions**. Banana is one of only three plant species known to carry infectious EPRVs, with integrants for at least three BSV species (BSGFV, BSIMV/Imove, BSOLV/Obino l'Ewai).
- **Endogenous tobacco vein clearing virus (eTVCV) in Nicotiana.** Lockhart et al. (2000) characterized TVCV in the hybrid *Nicotiana edwardsonii*; infection arises from activation of replication-competent endogenous TVCV sequences, and the virus is seed-transmitted to 100% of progeny but is not mechanically or graft-transmissible. Related solanaceous EPRVs (NsEPRV in *N. sylvestris*, first found by Jakowitsch et al., 1999; NtoEPRV) form a TVCV-like group; most NsEPRVs are mutated, non-infectious, and may confer resistance via methylation-based silencing.
- **Endogenous petunia vein clearing virus (ePVCV) in Petunia hybrida.** Richert-Pöggeler et al. (*EMBO J.*, 2003) showed integrated PVCV in *Petunia hybrida* (and wild *P. axillaris, P. integrifolia*) is normally silenced by DNA methylation in pericentromeric heterochromatin, but stress/aging can induce infectious virus; absent in *P. parodii*.
- **Endogenous rice tungro bacilliform virus-like (eRTBV) in Oryza**, plus Caulimoviridae-derived integrants across many plant genomes (bitter orange *Poncirus trifoliata*, potato *Solanum tuberosum*, tomato, tobacco, banana). Tobacco EPRVs can reach ~1,000 copies.

**Non-retroviral integrated RNA virus sequences (NIRVS/NRVSs)**

- Chiba et al. (*PLoS Pathogens*, 2011) reported the first widespread endogenization of non-retroviral RNA virus sequences in plant genomes: sequences from **Partitiviridae** (coat-protein genes — the most widespread), **cytorhabdoviruses/varicosaviruses** (nucleocapsid genes, across >9 plant families including Brassicaceae and Solanaceae), and a **betaflexivirus** replicase (in cucumber). A partitivirus CP-like sequence closely matched *Arabidopsis thaliana* **ILR2** (an auxin/IAA regulator). Integration patterns (present in some species/genera but not others) make these useful phylogenetic markers and indicate multiple independent integration events.

**Geminivirus-related DNA (GRD) in Nicotiana**

- **Geminivirus-related DNA (GRD)** is integrated in *Nicotiana* (Murad et al., *Heredity*, 2004; Ashby et al., 1997). The *N. tabacum* nuclear genome carries ~25 tandem direct repeats of GRD containing sequences similar to the geminiviral **AL1/Rep (replication-associated)** gene and the *cis*-essential replication origin. Two families (GRD3, GRD5) derive from begomoviruses; GRD5 integrated into the common ancestor of *N. kawakamii, N. tomentosa, N. tomentosiformis*, and GRD3 more recently in *N. tomentosiformis*. Lefeuvre et al. (*PLoS ONE*, 2011) used GRD sequences in *N. tabacum* (14), *N. tomentosiformis* (5), *N. kawakamii* (3), and *N. tomentosa* (1) to date begomovirus origins to >10 Mya. [PLOS][ref-05]

**Can viruses act as vectors carrying genes between plants?**

- **Virus capture of host genes:** Some plant viruses acquire host-derived sequences, and geminiviruses have been discussed as capturing host DNA — evidence that genetic material can pass from plant to virus.
- **Plausibility of virus-mediated plant-to-plant HGT:** Viruses are frequently invoked as *potential* vectors (via grafting, seed/pollen transmission of EPRVs, or capture-and-redeposit), but direct, well-documented cases of a virus transducing a nuclear host gene from one plant species into another remain scarce and largely hypothetical in pre-2021 literature. EPRV and NIRVS data prove viral genomes integrate into plant genomes; they do not by themselves prove viruses routinely shuttle *host* genes between plant species.
- **Caveat/debate:** The "virus-as-vector for interspecies plant HGT" hypothesis is **not well established**. Most confirmed plant HGT proceeds through direct tissue contact (parasitism, grafting) or *Agrobacterium*, not viral transduction.

## Recommendations

For a researcher or writer building on this synthesis:

- **Anchor each of the four themes on its strongest, best-replicated case, then treat weaker claims as provisional.** Use *Cuscuta* mRNA exchange (Kim et al. 2014) and the Orobanchaceae/*Cuscuta* functional-HGT surveys (Yang et al. 2016, 2019) as the backbone for parasites; *Fhb7* and the *Colletotrichum* subtilisin for bidirectional fungus↔plant HGT; sweet potato (Kyndt et al. 2015) and the Matveeva & Otten (2019) survey for bacteria→plant; and eBSV in banana for virus→plant. These are the claims least likely to be overturned.
- **Prioritize primary sources over reviews and news items.** Several strong secondary summaries appeared in search (EurekAlert, RNA-Seq Blog, Plantae); trace every number back to the primary journal article before publishing. Where a figure came from a preprint (e.g., the Rafflesiaceae mitogenome bioRxiv), flag it as not-yet-peer-reviewed.
- **Explicitly separate "macromolecule trafficking" from "genomic HGT."** The *Striga gesnerioides* SHR4z effector and *Cuscuta* mobile mRNAs/small RNAs demonstrate the *conduit* for HGT but are not themselves heritable genomic integrations. Keep this distinction sharp to avoid overstating HGT.
- **Treat the virus-as-vector hypothesis as open.** State clearly that integration of viral genomes into plant genomes is proven, but virus-mediated interspecies transfer of *host* genes is not.
- **Benchmarks that would change these conclusions:** (1) a peer-reviewed, contamination-controlled genome showing a virus-transduced nuclear host gene moving between two plant species would upgrade the virus-vector hypothesis from "hypothetical" to "documented"; (2) failure to replicate a claimed HGT in an independent, cleanly assembled genome (as nearly happened with *Cuscuta* mitochondrial HGT) should downgrade it; (3) demonstration of foreign+native genes on a single long-read contig plus expression data is the threshold for treating any new case as high-confidence.

## Caveats

- **Contamination and artifacts are the central threat to HGT claims.** Host DNA contaminating parasite samples, endophytic/microbial DNA in plant assemblies, chimeric assemblies, incomplete taxon sampling, and long-branch attraction all generate false positives — especially with short organellar sequences that yield poorly resolved trees. Some transfers initially reported into *Cuscuta* mitogenomes were not supported on careful re-analysis.
- **Directionality and dating are often uncertain.** For *Fhb7*, later work found homologs across multiple Triticeae genera, suggesting the transfer may predate the *Thinopyrum* lineage; for GRD and EPRVs, integration dates rest on molecular-clock assumptions with wide confidence intervals.
- **Compartment matters.** Plant mitochondria are HGT hotspots (fusion, DNA uptake, recombination) while plastids are highly resistant; nuclear HGT in angiosperms, though real, is far rarer than mitochondrial HGT and each case warrants individual scrutiny.
- **Some items in this report are effector/RNA-trafficking phenomena, not heritable genomic HGT** (e.g., *Cuscuta* STM RNAi, SHR4z), and are included to explain the physical routes HGT uses; they are labeled as such.
- **A small number of clarifying post-2020 references are cited only to contextualize earlier findings** (e.g., the *Sapria* 2020 genome, Shinozuka 2020, Su et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2020), consistent with the pre-2021 priority; the core landmark discoveries are all pre-2021.
- **Where evidence is contested it is flagged in-text** (Cuscuta mitogenome HGT; the virus-vector hypothesis; Fhb7 timing). Numerical values quoted (e.g., 108 vs. 52 HGT events, 24–41% of Rafflesiaceae mitochondrial genes, ~7% of dicots with cT-DNA) are method- and sampling-dependent and should be read as best current estimates, not fixed constants.
