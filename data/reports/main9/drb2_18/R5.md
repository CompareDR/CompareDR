# Horizontal Gene Transfer in the Plant World, with Emphasis on Parasitic Relationships

**A systematic compilation of discoveries made primarily before 2021**

---

## Scope and Method

This report surveys documented cases of **horizontal gene transfer (HGT)** — the movement and genomic integration of genetic material between non-mating species — involving plants. It is organized into the four requested partner systems: (1) parasitic plants and their hosts, (2) fungi and plants, (3) bacteria and plants, and (4) viruses and plants. The emphasis is on primary discoveries published **before 2021**; a small number of later syntheses are cited only where they help interpret pre-2021 findings. Every case below is supported by specific **species names and gene names**, and each section discusses the physical structures, the type of genetic material moved, and the direction of transfer.

---

## Executive Summary

- **Parasitic plants are the most prolific HGT hotspots known among complex eukaryotes.** The feeding organ — the **haustorium** — creates symplastic cell-to-cell continuity with the host, allowing passage of DNA, mRNA, small RNAs, and proteins. Documented transfers range from single genes (*Striga hermonthica* acquiring *ShContig9483* from sorghum) to genome-scale acquisition (*Cuscuta campestris* with **108 functional HGT events**; *Rafflesia cantleyi* with dozens of transcribed host genes; *Sapria himalayana* with ≥1.2% of its genome of host origin) ([PubMed–Yoshida 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20508124/), [XTBG–Yang 2019](https://ir.xtbg.ac.cn/bitstream/353005/11497/1/Convergent%20horizontal%20gene%20transfer%20and%20cross-talk%20of%20mobile%20nucleic%20acids%20in%20parasitic%20plants.pdf), [Harvard DASH–Xi 2012](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/3f7841ca-2fba-479a-be3a-cf148716a39b/content)).
- **Fungus–plant transfer is bidirectional.** Plants received antifungal-relevant genes such as the Fusarium head blight resistance gene ***Fhb7*** (an *Epichloë*-derived glutathione S-transferase now in wheat relatives) and a β-1,6-glucanase (*LpBGNL* in perennial ryegrass); fungi received plant genes such as a **subtilisin** (*Colletotrichum*) and a **leucine-rich repeat (LRR) protein** from barley (*Pyrenophora*) ([PMC–Shinozuka 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5567365/), [PLoS ONE–Sun 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0060029)).
- ***Agrobacterium* is nature's genetic engineer**, transferring T-DNA via a type-IV secretion system. Plants that are **naturally transgenic** include *Nicotiana*, *Linaria*, and — most famously — **sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*)**, which carries two expressed bacterial T-DNAs (*IbT-DNA1*, *IbT-DNA2*) in all cultivated varieties tested ([PNAS–Kyndt 2015, cited in Nat. Plants](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-017-0002-z), [Wiley PBI–Gheysen lab](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/pbi.13986)).
- **Viruses leave durable genomic "fossils" in plants** (endogenous pararetroviruses in banana, petunia, tobacco; geminivirus-related DNA in *Nicotiana* and yams) and can act as **vectors**: the geminivirus Beet curly top Iran virus packages host chromosomal DNA into transmissible hybrid "minicircles," an experimentally demonstrated route for virus-mediated HGT between plants ([Nat. Commun.–Catoni 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-07775-w), [PNAS–Bejarano 1996](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC40128/)).

## 1. HGT Between Parasitic Plants and Their Hosts

Parasitic plants represent the single richest source of verified plant–plant HGT cases. The reason is physical intimacy: parasites breach host tissues and fuse their physiology to the host's vascular system, creating a conduit through which macromolecules — including nucleic acids — routinely flow.

### 1.1 Through what structures? The haustorium as the transfer bridge

- **The haustorium is the key organ.** All parasitic plants form a haustorium (from Latin *haurire*, "to drink"), a specialized invasive organ that penetrates host roots or stems and establishes continuity with host xylem and phloem; it is the interface where water, nutrients, solutes — and macromolecules — are exchanged ([PMC–Kaiser et al. 2015 review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4316696/)).
- **Symplastic, cell-to-cell connections.** *Cuscuta* (dodder) haustoria form **interspecific plasmodesmata** and sieve-pore connections with the host. The functional proof came from phloem-mobile green fluorescent protein (GFP, 25 kDa), which moves from transgenic hosts into *Cuscuta* — unequivocal evidence of open symplastic continuity between two different species ([PMC–interspecific plasmodesmata review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8049502/)).
- **A massive bidirectional molecular flux.** Beyond nutrients, the interface carries:
  - **mRNAs by the thousands, in both directions.** In *Cuscuta pentagona* growing on *Arabidopsis thaliana*, **45% (9,518) of the expressed host genes** were detected as transcripts in the parasite, while **24% (5,973) of parasite genes** appeared in host tissue; on tomato the exchange was smaller but still bidirectional (1.6% / 0.8%) (Figure 1B) ([PubMed–Kim 2014, Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25124438/), [Front. Plant Sci. 2025 synthesis](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2025.1716304/full)).
  - **Small regulatory RNAs as weapons.** *Cuscuta* microRNAs are exported into hosts, where they target and down-regulate host defense and signaling genes — cross-species RNA interference documented in 2018 ([PMC–interspecific plasmodesmata review, citing Shahid 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8049502/)).
  - **Proteins and even whole viruses.** Several hundred host proteins were found in *Cuscuta australis* (and vice versa), and plant viruses have been known since 1944 to pass through the dodder bridge between plants ([PMC–interspecific plasmodesmata review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8049502/)).

### 1.2 What genetic material is transferred?

- **Genomic DNA.** Genome sequencing of *Cuscuta campestris* showed that its 108 horizontally acquired genes carry **introns and flanking non-coding host sequences**, indicating transfer of genomic DNA rather than retroprocessed RNA; single transfer blocks exceeding **100 kb** have been measured in Orobanchaceae parasites ([XTBG–Yang 2019, Nat. Plants](https://ir.xtbg.ac.cn/bitstream/353005/11497/1/Convergent%20horizontal%20gene%20transfer%20and%20cross-talk%20of%20mobile%20nucleic%20acids%20in%20parasitic%20plants.pdf), [HAL review, citing Kado & Innan 2018](https://hal.science/hal-03443232v1/document)).
- **mRNA/cDNA intermediates.** In other cases the signature points to an RNA intermediate: the sorghum-derived gene in *Striga hermonthica* ends in a **poly-A-like tail** and lacks introns — hallmarks of reverse-transcribed mRNA ([Wiley PEI review, describing Yoshida 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/pei3.70087)).
- **Mitochondrial DNA is transferred most readily.** Across parasitic lineages, mitochondrial genes dominate the HGT record: *Rafflesia cantleyi*, *R. tuan-mudae*, and *Sapria himalayana* acquired an estimated **24–41% of their mitochondrial gene sequences** from their *Tetrastigma* hosts ([PLoS Genet.–Xi 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article/info%3Adoi%2F10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1003265)).
- **Mobile elements.** Transposons also cross the boundary — two **hAT transposon genes** moved from Brassicaceae hosts into broomrapes (*Orobanche*/*Phelipanche*) and remain actively expressed ([Sci. Rep.–Zhang 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep30192.pdf)).

### 1.3 Landmark case studies (host → parasite)

- ***Striga hermonthica* (witchweed) — the first proven nuclear HGT in a parasitic plant.** Yoshida and colleagues (*Science*, 2010) showed that this African root hemiparasite of cereals carries a **nuclear gene, *ShContig9483*** (encoding a 448-aa protein of unknown function), that is conserved across grasses (Poaceae) but absent from all other eudicots; phylogenetically it clusters with its host **sorghum (*Sorghum bicolor*)**, demonstrating capture of a host nuclear gene in nature, soon after *Striga* speciation ([PubMed–Yoshida 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20508124/), [Wikipedia–Striga hermonthica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Striga_hermonthica)).
- ***Cuscuta* (dodder) — the most dramatic functional HGT known in complex organisms.** Analyses of *C. campestris* and relatives identified **108 transcribed, probably functional HGT events** (plus 42 regions of host-derived transposons, pseudogenes, and non-coding DNA), building on an earlier estimate of 64 high-confidence candidates (Vogel et al., 2018). Remarkably, **18 of these genes belong to the same gene families independently acquired by Orobanchaceae root parasites**, and most are highly expressed in haustoria in both lineages — evidence of convergent, adaptive gene theft for the parasitic lifestyle ([XTBG–Yang 2019](https://ir.xtbg.ac.cn/bitstream/353005/11497/1/Convergent%20horizontal%20gene%20transfer%20and%20cross-talk%20of%20mobile%20nucleic%20acids%20in%20parasitic%20plants.pdf), [Acta Biol. Crac. review](https://abcbot.pl/pdf/63_2/2021-02-abcbot-02.pdf)).
- **Convergent acquisition across parasite families.** A 2016 comparative study of *Striga*, *Orobanche*, and *Phelipanche* found that HGT frequency rises with the degree of heterotrophy, with dozens of orthogroups traceable to host donors — grasses for *Striga*, mainly Rosaceae and Fabaceae for *Phelipanche* ([VT thesis repository–Yang 2016, Sci. Adv.](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstreams/2ab6cca1-30b0-4668-9356-db2bd78d7a11/download)).
- ***Rafflesia* and *Sapria* (Rafflesiaceae) — genome-scale theft from grapevine-family hosts.** Transcriptome phylogenomics showed *Rafflesia cantleyi* carries **49 actively transcribed genes (2.1% of analyzed transcripts)** acquired from its obligate host ***Tetrastigma rafflesiae***; most are nuclear, many retain introns (implicating DNA transfer), and expression levels match native genes — prompting the "genomic deception" hypothesis that host-like genes help the parasite evade host immunity ([Harvard DASH–Xi 2012](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/3f7841ca-2fba-479a-be3a-cf148716a39b/content)). The trend continues at genome scale: at least **1.2% of the *Sapria himalayana* genome** is host-derived, including transposons and genes potentially adaptive for parasitism, some revealing *extinct* host associations like "DNA fossils" ([Harvard Davis Lab PDF–Thorogood 2021, citing Cai 2021](https://davislab.oeb.harvard.edu/files/davislab/files/new_phytologist_-_2021_-_thorogood_-_endoparasitic_plants_and_fungi_show_evolutionary_convergence_across_phylogenetic.pdf?m=1660058181)).
- **Specific stolen genes with known functions.**
  - **Strictosidine synthase-like (*SSL*) genes** from Brassicaceae hosts were found in both the root parasite ***Phelipanche aegyptiaca*** and the shoot parasite ***Cuscuta australis*** ([Sci. Rep.–Zhang 2016, citing Zhang 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep30192.pdf)).
  - **Albumin genes** from legume (Fabaceae) hosts were acquired by ***Orobanche* spp., *Phelipanche* spp., and *Cuscuta pentagona*** ([Sci. Rep.–Zhang 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep30192.pdf)).
  - A **defense-related gene with 87% identity to *Sorghum bicolor*** trafficked into *Striga hermonthica*, and is highly expressed in haustoria — implicating HGT in parasite–host molecular crosstalk ([JKUAT thesis, citing Yang 2016](http://ir.jkuat.ac.ke/bitstream/handle/123456789/4790/SYLVIA_THESIS_final copy.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)).

### 1.4 The reverse direction (parasite → host)

Transfer is **not one-way**: hosts can also capture parasite genes.

- Mower and colleagues (*Nature*, 2004) reported the first cases of gene flow **from parasitic to host plants**: the mitochondrial ***atp1*** gene moved from the parasites ***Cuscuta*** and ***Bartsia*** (Orobanchaceae) into the host genus ***Plantago*** ([Nature–Mower 2004](https://www.nature.com/articles/432165b)).
- Follow-up work showed the event was large: ***atp1*, *atp6*, and *matR*** were all transferred from *Cuscuta* into the common ancestor of *Plantago coronopus*, *P. macrorhiza*, and *P. subspathulata* — probably a single DNA-mediated event (retained RNA-editing-site cytidines rule out an RNA intermediate). The foreign copies later degraded into untranscribed pseudogenes, but underwent **gene conversion** with native copies, showing how foreign DNA can be edited into recipient genomes ([PMC–Mower 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/)).
- Parasite-derived mitochondrial genes also reached ***Geranium*** (*cox2*, *rpl5*, *rps4* from *Cuscuta*), confirming that parasite-to-host mitochondrial transfer recurs across lineages ([PMC–Lin 2022, Cuscuta mitogenome study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9234195/)).

## 2. HGT Between Fungi and Plants

**Answer in brief: the transfer is bidirectional.** Genome-scale surveys and targeted studies document ancient fungus→plant transfers, recent fungus→plant transfers with clear adaptive value (including a genuine disease-resistance gene), and rare but well-supported plant→fungus transfers that appear to enhance fungal pathogenicity or symbiosis.

### 2.1 From fungi to plants

- **The earliest recognized case — a fungal intron invading plant mitochondria.** The group-I intron of the mitochondrial ***cox1*** gene is of **fungal origin**; it first appeared in angiosperms (e.g., in *Peperomia*) and then spread explosively — jumping between plant lineages more than a thousand times, one of the most mobile elements known in plant genomes (Vaughn et al. 1995; Cho et al. 1998; Sanchez-Puerta et al. 2008, reviewed in [Acta Biol. Crac. 2021](https://abcbot.pl/pdf/63_2/2021-02-abcbot-02.pdf)).
- **Systematic evidence of rare, ancient transfers.** A phylogenomic comparison of six plant proteomes against 153 other genomes identified **nine plant–fungus gene transfers**, mostly ancient and fungus→plant; one striking case is a **bifunctional siderophore biosynthesis gene (*iucA*/*iucC* domains)** that moved serially from bacteria → fungi → plants, presumably aiding iron acquisition in soil environments ([Plant Cell–Richards 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/)).
- ***LpBGNL*: an anti-fungal enzyme gene from a symbiotic fungus to a grass.** Perennial ryegrass (*Lolium perenne*) carries a **β-1,6-glucanase gene (*LpBGNL*)** acquired from its endophytic symbiont ***Epichloë festucae***; conservation of the intron position indicates transfer of genomic DNA, likely after endophyte cell death liberated DNA near plant cells. Because β-1,6-glucanase degrades fungal cell walls, the acquired gene plausibly helps the plant resist pathogenic fungi ([Sci. Rep.–Shinozuka 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5567365/)).
- ***Fhb7*: a fungal-origin disease-resistance gene in wheat's gene pool — the flagship example.** The major **Fusarium head blight (FHB) resistance locus *Fhb7*** in the wheat relative ***Thinopyrum elongatum*** encodes a **glutathione S-transferase (GST)** that detoxifies the trichothecene mycotoxins produced by *Fusarium*; phylogenetic evidence shows the gene originated by HGT from an ***Epichloë*** endophyte. Introgressed into bread wheat, it confers FHB resistance without yield penalty — the clearest demonstration that fungus→plant HGT can create a disease-resistance gene later exploited in breeding (Wang et al., *Science* 2020, 368:eaba5435, described in [PMC–GST analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10214746/) and [Wiley PEI review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/pei3.70087)).

### 2.2 From plants to fungi

- **A plant subtilisin gene in a pathogen's toolbox.** The plant-pathogenic genus ***Colletotrichum*** acquired **plant-like subtilisin genes (CPLSs)** from plants roughly **150–155 million years ago**; the genes are upregulated during maize infection and may act via molecular mimicry to aid plant pathogenesis ([Armijos Jaramillo 2013, PLoS ONE 8:e59078, as developed in PMC–CPLS study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3917961/) and [Springer review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11274-026-05042-5)).
- **A host defense gene captured by a cereal pathogen.** In the necrotrophs ***Pyrenophora teres*** and ***P. tritici-repentis*** (net blotch of barley; tan spot of wheat), 14 HGT genes of bacterial or plant origin were identified; notably, a **leucine-rich repeat (LRR) protein gene** was acquired from the **host plant (barley, *Hordeum vulgare*)**. Because NBS-LRR-type proteins mediate plant disease resistance (e.g., the wheat *Tsn1* sensitivity protein), the stolen LRR — predicted to be secreted — may **subvert host resistance** and interfere with *Tsn1*–ToxA interactions ([PLoS ONE–Sun 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0060029)).
- **A mutualist stocking up on host genes.** The arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus ***Rhizophagus irregularis*** carries **plant-derived genes** — including a **transposase (MULE) family, a pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) protein, a HAUS augmin-like protein, a retrotransposon protein, a calcium-binding protein, and a protein kinase** — plus bacterial genes; more than half are expressed, suggesting functional roles in the oldest plant symbiosis on Earth ([Front. Plant Sci.–Li 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5982333/)).

## 3. HGT Between Bacteria and Plants: *Agrobacterium* and Its T-DNA

The bacterium–plant route is unique in this report because the transfer machinery is **deliberate and genetically encoded by the donor**: *Agrobacterium* is a natural genetic engineer, and its transformation of plant cells was the first known case of active DNA transfer from prokaryotes to eukaryotes ([PMC–Lacroix & Citovsky review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6717549/)).

### 3.1 How *Agrobacterium* transfers T-DNA to plants — the mechanism

- **The vehicle is the Ti (tumor-inducing) plasmid.** *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* carries a large Ti plasmid bearing (i) the **T-DNA** region that is actually transferred and (ii) the ***vir* (virulence) region** encoding the transfer machinery; the related *A. rhizogenes* carries the root-inducing (Ri) plasmid that causes hairy-root disease ([Wiley MMI review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/mmi.12423)).
- **Step-by-step transfer** ([New Phytol.–Pitzschke review](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.17184), [PMC–Lacroix & Citovsky](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6717549/)):
  1. **Wound signals activate the bacteria.** Phenolics (e.g., acetosyringone) and sugars released by wounded plant cells are sensed by the **VirA/VirG** two-component system, which switches on the *vir* genes.
  2. **T-strand generation.** **VirD1/VirD2** nick the 25-bp T-DNA border repeats, releasing a single-stranded T-DNA (T-strand) with VirD2 covalently attached to its 5′ end.
  3. **Export through a type-IV secretion system (T4SS).** The **VirB/VirD4** channel pumps the T-strand–VirD2 complex, along with effector proteins (VirE2, VirE3, VirD5, VirF), into the plant cell.
  4. **Nuclear import.** In the cytoplasm, **VirE2** coats the T-strand (the "T-complex"), and host factors (e.g., VIP1) guide it through nuclear pores.
  5. **Integration.** T-DNA integrates into plant chromosomes by **illegitimate (non-homologous) recombination**, is expressed from eukaryotic-style promoters, and is inherited by daughter cells.
- **What the T-DNA encodes.** Natural T-DNAs carry **oncogenes** — ***iaaM*** (tryptophan-2-monooxygenase) and ***iaaH*** (indoleacetamide hydrolase) for auxin synthesis, ***ipt*** (isopentenyl transferase) for cytokinin synthesis, and the *rol* gene set (*rolA/rolB/rolC/rolD*) in *A. rhizogenes* — which drive uncontrolled proliferation (crown galls or hairy roots), plus **opine-synthesis genes** (e.g., ***nos*** for nopaline, ***acs*** for agrocinopines) whose products feed the colonizing bacteria ([PMC–Lacroix & Citovsky](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6717549/), [Wiley MMI review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/mmi.12423)).

### 3.2 Naturally transgenic plants: bacteria have already written genes into plant genomes

Long before human genetic engineering, *Agrobacterium* T-DNAs became **genomically fixed and sexually transmitted** in several plant lineages — so-called **natural transformants** carrying "cellular T-DNA" (cT-DNA) ([Matveeva & Otten review, Front. Plant Sci. 2014, archived](https://www.academia.edu/download/82286387/bf0033158620220317-13582-1ytr52m.pdf)):

- ***Nicotiana* (tobacco genus).** In 1983, sequences homologous to *A. rhizogenes* T-DNA were found in uninfected ***Nicotiana glauca*** — the first cT-DNA ever described. It forms an imperfect inverted repeat containing homologs of the oncogenes ***NgrolB*, *NgrolC*, *NgORF13*, *NgORF14*** and the mikimopine-synthase gene ***Ngmis***, and these ancient genes **are still expressed**, suggesting an evolutionary role. Related cT-DNAs occur in *N. tabacum*, *N. tomentosiformis*, *N. tomentosa*, and *N. otophora* (White et al., 1983; Furner et al., 1986) ([Academia.edu archive–Matveeva & Lutova 2014](https://www.academia.edu/download/82286387/bf0033158620220317-13582-1ytr52m.pdf), [ResearchGate–rol genes review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232793172_Sequences_homologous_to_Agrobacterium_rhizogenes_T-DNA_in_the_genomes_of_uninfected_plants)).
- ***Linaria* (toadflax).** *Linaria vulgaris* carries two tandem copies of a cT-DNA containing ***rolA*, *rolB*, *rolC*, *ORF2*, *ORF3*, *ORF8*, *ORF13*, *ORF14*,** and ***mis***; comparative data date the transformation event(s) to roughly **1.5–2 million years ago** and support a monophyletic origin within the genus (Matveeva et al., 2012; Matveeva & Lutova, 2014) ([ResearchGate–rol genes review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377973399_rol_Genes_of_Agrobacteria_Possible_Biological_Functions)).

### 3.3 The sweet potato case: a naturally transgenic food crop

The most celebrated example of natural *Agrobacterium*-mediated HGT is one of humanity's staple crops.

- **The discovery.** In 2015, Kyndt and colleagues (*PNAS* 112:5844–5849) demonstrated that cultivated sweet potato (***Ipomoea batatas***) is a **naturally transgenic plant**: its genome contains two *Agrobacterium* T-DNAs, ***IbT-DNA1*** and ***IbT-DNA2***, inserted by an ancestor of *A. rhizogenes* ([Nat. Plants reference record](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-017-0002-z), [Wiley PBI–fast-track review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/pbi.13986)).
- **The two inserts and their genes** ([ResearchGate–rol genes review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377973399_rol_Genes_of_Agrobacteria_Possible_Biological_Functions)):
  - ***IbT-DNA1*** (~22 kb, corresponding to *A. rhizogenes* T~R~-DNA) contains intact ***iaaM*, *iaaH*, *acs* (agrocinopine synthase)**, and **C-protein** genes, plus a defective inverted *iaaM* copy. It was found in **all 291 cultivated sweet potato varieties examined — but not in closely related wild species** — suggesting it was selected during domestication.
  - ***IbT-DNA2*** (corresponding to T~L~-DNA) contains intact ORFs homologous to ***ORF14*, *ORF17n*, *rolB*, *rolC*, *ORF13*,* and *ORF18***; it occurs in about **45 of 217 genotypes**, in both cultivated and wild accessions.
- **The genes are alive, not fossils.** At least four *IbT-DNA1* genes and two *IbT-DNA2* genes **are expressed** in every tissue type tested (leaf, stem, roots, storage roots), and the inserts show signs of having contributed to sweet potato evolution — leading the authors to argue the relationship should be seen not as parasitism but as a kind of **symbiosis** ([Wiley PBI–fast-track review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/pbi.13986)).
- **Why it matters.** Every cultivated sweet potato on Earth is, by molecular definition, a transgenic organism created by nature — a fact widely invoked to recalibrate public and regulatory debates about GMOs, and a direct proof that *Agrobacterium* HGT can produce agronomically successful phenotypes ([Wiley PBI–fast-track review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/pbi.13986)).

## 4. HGT Between Viruses and Plants

Viruses interact with plant genomes in two distinct ways relevant here: (i) viral sequences **become permanently integrated** into plant chromosomes ("endogenous viral elements"), and (ii) viruses can **act as carriers** that pick up host DNA and move it into other plants.

### 4.1 Do viruses leave their genes inside plants after infection? Yes — as endogenous viral elements

- **Endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs) are widespread.** DNA from Caulimoviridae (dsDNA reverse-transcribing plant viruses) has integrated into many plant genomes by accidental illegitimate recombination during double-strand-break repair; these inserts are vertically inherited "viral fossils" ([PMC–D’Hont 2008, J. Virol.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2447048/)).
- **Banana: integrated viruses that are still infectious.** The genome of ***Musa balbisiana*** harbors integrated copies of at least four **Banana streak virus (BSV)** species — *BSV Obino l'Ewai (BSOlV)*, *BSV Imové (BSImV)*, *BSV Mysore*, and *BSV Goldfinger (BSGfV)*. The BSGfV insert exists as two alleles (7 and 9) from a **single integration event dated to ~0.63 million years ago**; allele 7 remains **infectious** and releases virus particles in interspecific (A×B) hybrids, triggered by hybridization, in-vitro culture, and stress — a serious problem for banana breeding ([PMC–D’Hont 2008](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2447048/), [PMC–Gayral 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2898222/)).
- **Petunia and tobacco: silenced integrated viruses.** **Petunia vein clearing virus (PVCV)** in *Petunia hybrida* and **tobacco vein clearing virus (TVCV)** in *Nicotiana tabacum* are the other classic infectious EPRVs; unlike banana, their expression is kept in check by **DNA methylation** in petunia and tomato relatives ([PMC–D’Hont 2008](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2447048/)).
- **Geminivirus DNA fossilized in tobacco.** In 1996, **geminivirus-related DNA (GRD)** was discovered as multiple direct repeats at a unique locus in the ***Nicotiana tabacum*** nuclear genome (classes GRD5, GRD53, GRD3; also GRD2 in *N. tomentosa*). Each repeat carries a degenerate viral ***rep* (replication protein, AL1) gene** plus a replication-origin-like sequence — the first proven integration of a DNA virus into a plant genome (Bejarano et al., PNAS 1996; Kenton et al., 1995) ([PMC–Bejarano 1996](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC40128/), [PMC–Filloux 2015 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5014472/)).
- **Yams: endogenous geminivirus genes that are switched on — possibly as natural antiviral resistance.** Many *Dioscorea* (yam) species contain **transcriptionally active endogenous geminiviral sequences**, including expressed *rep*-like genes. Because transgenic expression of *rep* fragments is known to confer geminivirus resistance, endogenized viral genes may function as a **natural form of engineered virus immunity**; related sequences were also detected in lettuce, apple, coffee, and poplar genomes ([PMC–Filloux 2015, Virus Evol.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5014472/)).
- **Even giant-virus footprints exist.** Plant genomes (e.g., of *Physcomitrella* and other lineages) enclose sequences derived from **giant virus relatives** (Maumus et al., 2014, cited in [PMC–Li 2018 reference list](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5982333/)).

### 4.2 Can viruses act as carriers/vectors for gene transfer between plants? Yes — experimentally demonstrated

- **Hybrid virus–host "minicircles" in sugar beet.** In a landmark real-time demonstration, Catoni and colleagues (*Nature Communications*, 2018) found that sugar beet (*Beta vulgaris*) infected with the geminivirus **Beet curly top Iran virus (BCTIV)** spontaneously generates circular **hybrid DNA minicircles (~1,300–1,600 nt)** composed of a viral intergenic segment (carrying the stem-loop origin of rolling-circle replication) fused to **chromosomal DNA fragments of the host plant** ([Nat. Commun.–Catoni 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-07775-w)).
- **These captured host DNAs are fully mobile cargo.** The minicircles:
  - **replicate** to high copy number using the viral machinery (one reached copy numbers comparable to the virus itself);
  - **spread systemically** within the plant;
  - **are transcribed**, producing novel transcripts of the captured host DNA;
  - **are encapsidated into BCTIV virions** — meaning they can be carried by the virus's leafhopper vector (*Circulifer haematoceps*) to other plants; and
  - when co-inoculated with BCTIV, replicate and are transcribed in **other host species (*Nicotiana benthamiana*, *Arabidopsis thaliana*)** — i.e., chromosomal DNA from sugar beet can be exported and expressed in a different plant species ([Nat. Commun.–Catoni 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-07775-w)).
  This two-stage process (virus captures host DNA → virus delivers it to another host) is precisely the proposed mechanism by which viruses mediate HGT between plants that share viral pathogens.
- **RNA viruses can also package host material.** Single-stranded RNA plant viruses such as **cucumber necrosis virus** and **brome mosaic virus** have been shown to **encapsidate host RNAs** into virions, another plausible route of inter-plant genetic exchange ([Nat. Commun.–Catoni 2018, and references therein](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-07775-w)).
- **Historical integrations corroborate the vector role.** The repeated discovery of geminivirus-related sequences fixed in plant nuclear genomes (tobacco, yams, and others above) is consistent with circular viral DNA intermediates — exactly like the minicircles — occasionally integrating into host chromosomes ([Nat. Commun.–Catoni 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-07775-w)).
- **Note on a second "bridge" role:** parasitic dodders also transmit viruses between plants (known since Bennett, 1944), meaning parasite and viral routes can compound each other at the same interface ([PMC–interspecific plasmodesmata review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8049502/)).

## 5. Comparative Synthesis and Conclusions

Across the four partner systems, several unifying patterns emerge from the pre-2021 record:

- **Physical intimacy is the master variable.** Every prolific HGT system involves sustained, close cellular contact: haustorial fusion (parasitic plants), endophytic or pathogenic growth within host tissue (fungi), wound-site colonization (*Agrobacterium*), and systemic infection (viruses). The **germline barrier** — the need for foreign DNA to reach cells that yield gametes — explains why HGT remains far rarer in plants than in microbes, and why most fixed events are ancient or associated with vegetatively propagated or easily regenerating tissues.
- **The type of transferred material differs by system.** Parasitic-plant systems move bulk **genomic DNA, mitochondrial DNA, and mRNA**; the bacterial route moves a **defined single-stranded T-DNA** through an evolved secretion channel; the viral route integrates **viral genomes or virus-packaged host DNA/RNA**; fungal transfers appear to involve **genomic DNA fragments** (intron-bearing) liberated by dying hyphae or other contact mechanisms.
- **Both directions of exchange are documented** for parasitic plants (host→parasite and parasite→host) and for fungi (fungus→plant and plant→fungus), whereas *Agrobacterium* transfer is effectively **one-way by design**, and viral endogenization is virus→plant with the reverse (plant→virus→plant) now experimentally demonstrated through minicircles.
- **Transferred genes are frequently functional and adaptive**, not just junk: haustorial-expressed genes in *Cuscuta* and Orobanchaceae (convergent theft), the *Fhb7* resistance gene from *Epichloë* now deployed in wheat breeding, expressed *IbT-DNAs* selected during sweet potato domestication, and expressed endogenous *rep* genes in yams that may confer virus resistance.

**Bottom line.** Before 2021, the plant world was already known to be thoroughly permeated by horizontal gene transfer, and parasitic or infectious relationships are its principal highways. Parasitic plants steal and donate genes across their haustorial bridges at genome scale; fungi and plants exchange offense- and defense-related genes in both directions; *Agrobacterium* has been engineering crops — including every cultivated sweet potato — for millennia; and viruses both fossilize themselves in plant chromosomes and shuttle host DNA between species. Far from being sealed evolutionary lineages, plant genomes are mosaics whose parasitic liaisons have left readable, functional, and sometimes agronomically decisive signatures.
