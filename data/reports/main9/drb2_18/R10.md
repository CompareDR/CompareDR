# Horizontal Gene Transfer in the Plant World: A Pre-2021 Synthesis, with Emphasis on Parasitic Relationships

Across parasitic, pathogenic, and symbiotic interactions, plants exchange DNA with other plants, with bacteria, with fungi, and with viruses. "Horizontal gene transfer" (HGT) means the non-sexual movement of genetic material between organisms with stable integration into the recipient genome. The four sections below organize the major pre-2021 findings, with named species, named genes, and the primary literature that documents them.

## 1. Between Parasitic Plants

A parasitic plant attaches to its host via a **haustorium** — a modified organ that fuses the parasite's vasculature with the host's xylem and phloem. The haustorium creates a continuous cellular and molecular channel through which nutrients, mRNAs, proteins, viruses, and DNA can flow in both directions. Plant mitochondria, which are recombinogenic and fusogenic and which possess active DNA-uptake machinery, dominate the documented plant-to-plant HGT records; chloroplasts do not, because they lack these properties.

**Cuscuta (dodder) and Plantago (plantain).** The clearest mechanistic case is Mower, Stefanović, Hao, Gummow, Jain, Ahmed & Palmer (BMC Biology 8:150, 2010), who showed that three mitochondrial genes — **atp1, atp6, and matR** — moved together from the parasitic genus *Cuscuta* (donor: *C. gronovii*) into a small clade of *Plantago* species (recipients: *P. coronopus*, *P. macrorhiza*, *P. subspathulata*). The transfer was DNA-mediated: the foreign copies retain un-edited cytidines at ancestral C-to-U RNA editing sites, which would not be true if the donor molecule had been mRNA. After integration, all three xenologs degraded into pseudogenes in *Plantago*; the mitochondrial atp1 locus then underwent gene conversion between the native and foreign copies, generating intragenic mosaicism [1].

**Striga hermonthica (witchweed) and Sorghum bicolor.** The first documented **nuclear** HGT into a parasitic plant came from Yoshida, Maruyama, Nozaki & Shirasu (Science 328:1128, 2010), then at the RIKEN Plant Science Center in Yokohama. The gene **ShContig9483** in the eudicot parasite *S. hermonthica* is a monocot-specific, 448-amino-acid coding region that clusters phylogenetically with sorghum (its natural grass host), not with any eudicot. A 13-adenine poly-A tract at the 3′ end of the genomic region suggests the donor molecule was a retro-processed mRNA, not genomic DNA. The same gene is present in the related cowpea witchweed *S. gesnerioides* but absent in *Orobanche minor*, indicating the transfer occurred after *Striga* diverged from *Orobanche* but before *S. hermonthica* and *S. gesnerioides* speciated [2,3].

**Orobanchaceae at genome scale.** Yang, Zhang, Wafula et al. (PNAS 113(45):E7010–E7019, 2016) looked across three parasitic Orobanchaceae species — the hemiparasite *Striga hermonthica*, the generalist *Triphysaria versicolor*, and the holoparasite *Phelipanche aegyptiaca* — together with the free-living outgroup *Lindenbergia*. They found **52 high-confidence HGT events**, almost all from host lineages (Fabaceae or Poaceae), and showed that the number of events scales with parasitic dependence: holoparasites > hemiparasites > free-living relatives. Transferred genes are preferentially expressed in the **haustorium** and are enriched in functions that plausibly support the parasitic lifestyle. Intron evidence indicates that genomic DNA fragments, not retro-processed mRNAs, account for most of the transfers [4]. A complementary whole-genome survey by Kado & Innan (Genome Biology and Evolution 10(12):3196–3210, 2018) — within the user's pre-2021 window — sequenced five Orobanchaceae and identified **106 HGT genes**, all confined to the two obligate parasites *Orobanche minor* (22 genes) and *Aeginetia indica* (84 genes); none in the three facultative parasites (*Pedicularis keiskei*, *Phtheirospermum japonicum*, *Melampyrum roseum*). About half of these genes still have intact reading frames, and several physically cluster in the host reference genome, indicating that fragments **>100 kb** were transferred as DNA [5].

A working summary: HGT between parasitic plants and their hosts is concentrated in obligate parasites, biased toward haustorium-expressed genes, and travels by at least two molecular routes — DNA fragments that preserve introns, and mRNAs that may be retro-processed after entry. The two mechanisms can operate in the same group of plants.

## 2. Between Fungi and Plants

Fungi ↔ plant HGT is **bidirectional**. The fungus-to-plant direction is best documented by resistance-gene transfers; the plant-to-fungus direction is best documented by comparative genomics of plant-pathogenic fungi that share a host niche.

**Fhb7 in Thinopyrum elongatum from Epichloë.** Wang, Sun, Ge, Zhao, Hou, Wang, Lyu, Chen, Xu, Guo, Xu & Bai (Science 369:eaba5435, 2020) cloned the broad-spectrum Fusarium head blight (FHB) resistance gene **Fhb7** from the perennial wheatgrass *Thinopyrum elongatum*. *Fhb7* encodes a **glutathione S-transferase (GST)** that detoxifies the trichothecene mycotoxins (DON, T-2, HT-2, NIV) produced by *Fusarium graminearum* and relatives by conjugating glutathione onto the epoxide group of the toxin. *Fhb7* has no close plant homolog; its closest match (~97% DNA identity) is in **Epichloë** endophytic fungi that live asymptomatically within temperate grass species. The simplest explanation is that *Fhb7* moved from an *Epichloë* endophyte into the *Thinopyrum* lineage and was retained because it conferred Fusarium resistance. The gene has since been introgressed into wheat by wide hybridization and confers FHB + crown rot resistance without yield penalty — a working example of how an ancient fungus-to-plant HGT can be re-deployed in modern breeding [6]. (A 2020 paper, but a cornerstone pre-2021 case in the modern literature.)

**LpBGNL in Lolium perenne.** A second, more ancient case is the **β-1,6-glucanase-like gene (LpBGNL)** of perennial ryegrass (*Lolium perenne*), which is ~90% identical at the DNA level to corresponding genes in **Epichloë** endophytes and has no close plant homolog. Because asexual *Epichloë* species form intimate, long-term symbioses with temperate grasses, the close physical contact is a plausible conduit for transfer.

**Cross-kingdom parallels (fungi and oomycetes).** Although oomycetes are not plants, the cross-kingdom case is informative: Richards et al. (PNAS 108:15258, 2011) documented that the plant-parasitic oomycetes *Phytophthora ramorum*, *P. sojae*, and *P. infestans* together harbor ~33 genes acquired from fungi by HGT. The transferred set is enriched in secreted effectors and cell-wall-degrading enzymes — supporting the broader principle that **shared plant-host niche** is the strongest predictor of inter-eukaryote HGT.

A working summary: where a fungus lives inside a plant (endophyte) or shares the same host (pathogen), HGT can move in either direction. Adaptive retention — measured by the over-representation of cell-wall-degrading and detoxification functions among transferred genes — is what makes these transfers visible.

## 3. Between Bacteria and Plants (Agrobacterium)

Among bacteria, only one genus — **Agrobacterium** (*A. tumefaciens* and *A. rhizogenes*) — is known to transfer DNA into plant nuclear genomes in nature. The mechanism is the same one exploited in the laboratory: a segment of the bacterial **Ti (tumor-inducing) or Ri (root-inducing) plasmid**, called the **T-DNA**, is processed by the *vir* (virulence) operon, exported into the plant cell, imported into the nucleus, and integrated by non-homologous recombination. The T-DNA carries genes (e.g. *iaaM*, *iaaH* for auxin biosynthesis; *rol* genes for root proliferation) that re-program plant development, and opine-synthesis genes (*mis*, *acs*, *nos*) that force the plant to produce nutrients the bacterium can use. Until the 1980s, T-DNA was thought to exist only in infected tissue; we now know it has been transferred, inherited through the germline, and sometimes domesticated in multiple plant lineages.

**Nicotiana glauca (tree tobacco) as prototype.** White, Ghidossi, Gordon & Nester (Nature, 1983) reported that uninfected *N. glauca* plants carry sequences homologous to the Ri-plasmid T-DNA of *A. rhizogenes* — a region they named "**cellular T-DNA**" (cT-DNA). Furner et al. (1986) confirmed it in geographically separated varieties, implying the insertion predates the species. Suzuki et al. (Plant J 32:775–787, 2002) finished the picture: the *N. glauca* cT-DNA is an **imperfect inverted repeat** (7,968-bp left arm, 5,778-bp right arm) containing **NgrolB, NgrolC, NgORF13, NgORF14**, and two mikimopine synthase homologs (**NgmisL, NgmisR**). The NgMisR protein expressed in *E. coli* retains mis activity (it converts L-histidine + α-ketoglutarate to mikimopine), and the cT-DNA is transcribed in all *N. glauca* organs. The donor was a mikimopine-type *A. rhizogenes* similar to pRi1724 [7,8]. Surveys by Intrieri & Buiatti (2001) showed that 15 of 42 *Nicotiana* species carry cT-DNA. Deep sequencing of *N. tomentosiformis* (Chen et al., Plant J 80:669–682, 2014) revealed **four distinct cT-DNAs** in that diploid ancestor, one of which (TB-**mas2′**) is an intact mannopine synthase gene expressed in tobacco roots — clear evidence of selection on the inserted sequence [7].

**Linaria vulgaris (common toadflax).** Matveeva, Bogomaz, Pavlova, Nester & Lutova (MPMI 25:1542–1551, 2012) screened 127 dicot species for *rolB/rolC/ORF13/ORF14* and *tms1/tmr* and detected T-DNA only in *L. vulgaris* (Plantaginaceae). The cT-DNA is present as **two tandem copies in an imperfect direct repeat** and contains **LvORF2, LvORF3, LvORF8, LvrolA, LvrolB, LvrolC, LvORF13, LvORF14, Lvmis**, plus a partial **acs** at the left end. The site of insertion resembles a Ty3/gypsy retrotransposon. All Russian populations tested (from Moscow to Chelyabinsk, ~4,000 km apart) carry the same genes, with sequence polymorphism. *LvrolC* has an intact ORF, but the other rol/ORF/mis genes carry stop codons or frameshifts, and RT-PCR detected **no expression** in internodes, leaves, or roots. The donor was again a mikimopine-type *A. rhizogenes*, but the integration event is independent of the *Nicotiana* one [8,9].

**Ipomoea batatas (sweet potato) as a naturally transgenic food crop.** Kyndt, Quispe, Zhai, Jarret, Ghislain, Liu, Gheysen & Kreuze (PNAS 112(18):5844–5849, 2015) discovered two T-DNA regions in the cultivated sweet potato genome while assembling small-RNA data. **IbT-DNA1** (an inverted repeat of ~21,564 bp) carries ORFs homologous to *A. tumefaciens*-derived **iaaM** (tryptophan-2-monooxygenase), **iaaH** (indole-3-acetamide hydrolase), **C-prot**, and **Acs** (agrocinopine synthase); it is present in **all 291 tested cultivars** but in none of the close wild relatives. **IbT-DNA2** carries ORFs homologous to *A. rhizogenes*-derived **ORF14, ORF17n, RolB/RolC, ORF13, and ORF18/ORF17n**; it is present in 45 of 217 genotypes that span cultivated and wild species. Both T-DNAs are transcribed at detectable levels in different tissues, are transmitted from parent to progeny, and the fact that IbT-DNA1 is fixed in every cultivar but absent in wild relatives strongly suggests it was **selected during sweet potato domestication** [10]. The discovery reframes the cultural debate over transgenic food: a crop eaten for millennia is itself a natural transgenic.

A working summary: *Agrobacterium* has inserted its T-DNA into the germline of at least three independently evolved plant lineages (*Nicotiana*, *Linaria*, *Ipomoea*), and in sweet potato the inserted genes are expressed and apparently selected for. These are the clearest pre-2021 cases of natural, heritable, expressed bacterium-to-plant gene transfer in multicellular eukaryotes.

## 4. Between Viruses and Plants

Plant viruses in the family **Caulimoviridae** (the pararetroviruses) do not encode an integrase, but viral DNA still ends up in plant nuclear genomes — by **illegitimate recombination** with host DNA during the repair of double-strand breaks. Once integrated, the sequences are called **endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs)**. They are usually fragmented, sometimes rearranged, often transcriptionally silenced, and in a few cases still **reactivatable** to produce infectious episomal virus.

**Tobacco and TEPRV.** Jakowitsch et al. (1999) found up to **~1,000 copies** of a previously uncharacterized pararetrovirus integrated into the nuclear DNA of healthy *Nicotiana tabacum*. The copies have defective ORFs but show 91–98% nucleotide similarity despite their ancient origin, which (Mette et al., EMBO J 21:461, 2002) is taken as evidence of **selection for homology-dependent virus resistance** — the integrated copies are methylated, the exogenous virus cannot be isolated, and the TEPRV enhancer is silenced when re-introduced into tobacco but active in heterologous species such as *Arabidopsis* or onion that lack TEPRV [11].

**Petunia hybrida and PVCV.** Richert-Pöggeler, Noreen, Schwarzacher, Harper & Hohn (EMBO J 22:4836–4845, 2003) showed that **Petunia vein clearing virus (PVCV)** is a constituent of the *P. hybrida* nuclear genome. Some integrants are arranged in **tandem arrays**; when stress, wounding, hybridization, or ageing relaxes the host's DNA-methylation control on these tandem copies, a full-length episomal PVCV genome is transcribed and the plant develops vein-clearing disease. This is the cleanest experimental demonstration that an EPRV can be reactivated to produce infectious virus, and it shows that the *horizontal* (episomal) and *vertical* (genomic) forms of a pararetrovirus can be the same molecule expressed from different templates [12].

**Banana (Musa) and eBSV.** Harper et al. (1999), Ndowora et al. (1999), and Gayral, Iskra-Caruana et al. (J Virol 82(13):6697–6710, 2008) characterized integrated copies of **Banana streak virus (BSV)** — a badnavirus — in *Musa balbisiana*. Four BSV species (BSGFV, BSImV, BSMysV, BSOLV) are integrated; three of them (eBSGFV, eBSImV, eBSOLV) are **infectious** under stress: in vitro tissue culture or interspecific crosses trigger the release of a functional episomal BSV from the integrated template, causing outbreaks of banana streak disease. Both BSGFV and BSImV integrated only **~640,000 years ago**, well after the *M. acuminata* × *M. balbisiana* speciation event ~4.5 Mya. Plants carrying infectious eBSVs can nevertheless be **resistant** to super-infection by the same virus, presumably through epigenetic silencing [13].

**Tobacco and TVCV.** Lockhart et al. (2000) showed that **Tobacco vein clearing virus (TVCV)**, a *Cavemovirus* in the Caulimoviridae, is reactivatable from integrated copies in hybrid *Nicotiana* plants under stress. Together with PVCV and eBSV, TVCV is the third classical case of an EPRV that can switch from a vertically inherited, transcriptionally silent genomic state to a horizontally infectious episomal state.

Beyond these footprints, viruses can also **act as carriers** for nucleic acid movement between plants — a second route by which plant-to-plant gene exchange is enabled in nature.

**Umbraviruses as piggyback carriers.** Umbraviruses (e.g. **Groundnut rosette virus, Carrot mottle virus, Pea enation mosaic virus-2**) are unusual in that they lack a coat protein and rely on a *helper virus* (a polerovirus or luteovirus in the Luteoviridae) for encapsidation and aphid transmission. In return, the umbravirus provides the cell-to-cell movement function that lets the helper escape phloem limitation. **PEMV-2** is essential for the systemic spread of PEMV-1; it also allows even **potato leafroll virus** to spread out of the phloem into mesophyll and become manually transmissible. The umbravirus is, in effect, a movement-protein "vehicle" for a heterologous viral genome, and the principle extends to non-viral RNAs: the *tlaRNA* (tombusvirus-like associated RNA) satellites of poleroviruses are encapsidated by the polerovirus coat protein and gain systemic movement and aphid transmissibility "for free" (Robinson & Murant 1999; see also the TuYV/CMoV/CRLV complex reviewed in *Front Microbiol* 2023) [14].

**Grafting as a parallel carrier.** Although not a virus, natural grafting provides a comparable conduit for plant-plant HGT. Stegemann & Bock (Science 324:649–651, 2009) showed that complete **chloroplast genomes** can cross a graft junction between two tobacco lines, and Stegemann, Keuthe, Greiner & Bock (PNAS 109:2434–2437, 2012) extended this to **sexually incompatible species** (*N. tabacum* / *N. glauca* / *N. benthamiana*). The transferred plastid genomes entirely replace the resident plastid genome in the recipient cells, and the new plastid type is inherited by the next generation. Natural root and stem grafts (in strangler figs, ivy, and many trees) make this a plausible route for organellar HGT in the wild [15].

A working summary: pararetroviruses leave abundant, mostly silent, sometimes reactivatable footprints in plant genomes; umbraviruses and the grafting junction are two natural mechanisms that can carry nucleic acids — viral or organellar — from one plant into another, across species boundaries.
