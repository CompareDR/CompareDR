# Summary Table of Reviewed Studies

**Search and selection method.** I conducted a structured search for original empirical studies published from **January 1, 2020 through August 31, 2023** examining online learning, emergency remote teaching (ERT), or digitally delivered instruction in higher education during COVID-19. Searches combined terms such as *COVID-19*, *pandemic*, *online learning*, *emergency remote teaching*, *higher education*, *university*, *effectiveness*, *academic performance*, *satisfaction*, *comparative*, *longitudinal*, and *randomized*, with targeted searches for cross-sectional surveys, before/after or historical-cohort comparisons, longitudinal designs, and randomized experiments. Discovery was through scholarly web indexes, followed by verification against **original publisher pages, PubMed/PMC, journal sites, and institutional repositories**; reviews, commentaries, K–12 studies, studies without an empirical higher-education outcome, and papers published after August 2023 were excluded. The protocol also specified a highest-priority exclusion of the article by Wentao Meng, Lei Yu, Chen Liu, Nengchao Pan, Xiawen Pang, and Yunyun Zhu and its three supplied URLs; that article was **not opened, consulted, quoted, or used as evidence**, even though a search engine surfaced its title in results.  The synthesis below is therefore based on independently accessed original empirical studies only.

“Effective,” “ineffective,” and “neutral” are review-level classifications of the study’s principal result, not necessarily terminology used by the authors. “Neutral” includes statistically equivalent outcomes or genuinely mixed findings in which advantages and disadvantages offset one another.

# Effectiveness Analysis

Taken together, the evidence supports a **conditional rather than universal effectiveness conclusion**: online higher education during COVID-19 was capable of producing learning outcomes comparable to, and sometimes better than, face-to-face instruction, but the abrupt emergency transition often reduced satisfaction, interaction, course completion, and performance for vulnerable students. The strongest causal test directly comparing modalities, Cheung et al.’s 725-student randomized experiment, found no overall difference on five assessment measures, whereas the large Virginia community-college quasi-experiment found a modest 3%–6% reduction in course completion after an unplanned mid-semester switch. These are not contradictory results so much as evidence that **planned synchronous online instruction under controlled conditions is different from emergency migration of an existing face-to-face course**. [[18]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full)

**Cross-sectional perceptual surveys were the most negative class of evidence.** Adnan and Anwar’s Pakistani students emphasized internet cost, access and lost interaction; Coman et al.’s Romanian sample emphasized technical failures and instructors’ limited adaptation; Means and Neisler found much lower satisfaction after the U.S. emergency switch; and Polish medical students regarded online learning as equivalent for knowledge but substantially inferior for clinical and social skills. [[19]](https://doi.org/10.33902%2Fjpsp.2020261309) These designs capture students’ lived experience well, but they usually ask respondents to contrast a **new, compulsory, crisis-driven environment with a familiar pre-pandemic classroom**. Their outcomes therefore combine the effect of delivery mode with pandemic anxiety, home conditions, technological disruption, instructor inexperience, and nostalgia or preference for the prior mode. Retrospective comparisons are also vulnerable to recall and common-method bias. Consequently, they tend to yield more negative judgments than designs using objective achievement data. [[20]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8594957/)

**Comparative observational studies produced substantially more heterogeneous results.** Zheng et al. found equivalent or better grades in 16 of 17 online dental courses, and Iglesias-Pradas et al. observed higher aggregate academic performance under emergency remote teaching; by contrast, Foo et al.’s propensity-matched medical PBL study found lower online performance across participation, communication, preparation, critical thinking and group skills. [[21]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z) This divergence is substantively plausible: lecture- and knowledge-heavy subjects can be transferred online more readily, whereas PBL, clinical practice, laboratories and other interaction-intensive activities lose important components when physical collaboration disappears. The observational comparisons also face cohort, grading and assessment confounding: pandemic-era instructors sometimes changed assessment formats, deadlines or grading practices, so higher grades do not necessarily mean greater learning. Iglesias-Pradas et al. explicitly worked with course-level grade distributions rather than an invariant standardized achievement test, illustrating that limitation. [[22]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8631572/)

**Longitudinal and fixed-effects evidence points to modest average effects with substantial heterogeneity.** Chisadza et al. followed performance within the same South African course across the lockdown transition and showed that good Wi-Fi predicted better post-transition performance, while difficulty adapting predicted worse results. [[23]](https://www.studocu.com/en-us/document/clear-creek-independent-school-district/ap-us-government-politics/retrieve-it-was-a-book-report-for-the-crash-course/88089077) Mostafa et al., linking survey responses to institutional records, found that continuing students weathered the transition better than freshmen and transfers, showing that institutional familiarity and accumulated study routines mattered. [[12]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0264947) Bird et al.’s much larger difference-in-differences analysis is particularly informative because it compared modality changes within instructors/courses and in a separate specification within students: both approaches indicated a statistically significant but relatively modest decline in completion after the abrupt switch, especially among academically weaker students. [[24]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/23328584221081220) These methods reduce some cross-sectional selection bias, and their more moderate conclusions suggest that strongly negative student perceptions did not always translate one-for-one into equivalent losses in measured achievement.

**Randomized trials gave the least support to the proposition that online delivery is intrinsically ineffective.** Cheung et al.’s direct randomized comparison found no overall performance difference between synchronous online and face-to-face statistics instruction, although small face-to-face classes performed better. [[16]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full) Dietrich et al.’s randomized individualized-design intervention improved some motivational outcomes but not course performance, while Hardt et al.’s peer-mentoring RCT improved motivation, study behavior and exam registration without increasing credits on average. [[25]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563221001424) The methodological implication is important: when **content, instructors, assessments and assignment to modality are controlled**, the performance gap shrinks or disappears; when the exposure is an abrupt institutional shutdown affecting technology, routines, social life and mental health simultaneously, negative effects are easier to detect. RCTs therefore estimate a cleaner instructional effect, while emergency-transition studies estimate the combined effect of online delivery **plus the conditions under which it was imposed**.

The overall evidence is therefore best summarized as **moderately effective for knowledge transmission and conventional assessed learning, but less reliably effective for interaction-intensive, practical, clinical and socially mediated outcomes**. There is no defensible single effect size because outcomes range from satisfaction and perceived learning to GPA, course completion, clinical competence and standardized course scores, and the reviewed designs are too heterogeneous for meaningful statistical pooling without imposing strong comparability assumptions. The review should consequently be read as a systematic qualitative synthesis rather than a meta-analysis. [[26]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7899848/)

# Analysis of Influencing Factors

**Infrastructure.** The most direct mechanism is that unreliable connectivity, inadequate devices and expensive data interrupt exposure to instruction and raise the effective cost of participation. A student who repeatedly disconnects from synchronous teaching loses explanations, feedback and peer discussion; students relying on a phone rather than a computer also face constraints in writing, manipulating files and using specialized software. Chisadza et al. provide unusually concrete outcome evidence: South African students with good Wi-Fi performed better after the transition than students relying on mobile data or zero-rated access, and transition difficulty was associated with lower performance. [[10]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8250490/) Means and Neisler likewise found that more than one in six U.S. undergraduates experienced frequent connectivity or hardware/software problems serious enough to interfere with continued learning, while Coman et al. identified technical problems as the most prominent difficulty in their Romanian sample. [[27]](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/2496) Infrastructure therefore acts not merely through “student satisfaction” but through **lost instructional time, impaired participation and unequal access to high-bandwidth learning activities**.

**Instructional design.** Online delivery was more effective when instructors redesigned pedagogy instead of simply reproducing a classroom lecture on a videoconference platform. Tsang et al. found that course design independently predicted perceived learning outcomes, and instructor–student dialogue predicted student initiative. [[7]](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/11/8/446) Means and Neisler found that the number of recommended online instructional practices incorporated into a course significantly predicted satisfaction with learning. [[28]](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1287125.pdf) Muthuprasad et al.’s students specifically preferred recorded classes combined with quizzes, pointing toward chunking, replayability and formative assessment as useful design features. [[3]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291120300905) The mechanism is that explicit structure, active tasks, accessible materials, timely feedback and opportunities to check understanding compensate for the loss of cues and spontaneous clarification available in a physical classroom. Conversely, Coman et al. found that instructors’ weak technical skills and teaching styles not adapted to online environments were major barriers. [[29]](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/24/10367)

**Lack of social interaction.** Social disconnection affects learning through at least three pathways: fewer opportunities for clarification, weaker peer knowledge construction, and reduced belonging/accountability. Bączek et al. found that Polish medical students regarded online learning as dramatically less effective for social competence and reported reduced teacher interaction, social isolation and—especially for senior students—loss of patient interaction. [[30]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7899848/) Means and Neisler reported that students particularly missed immediate instructor feedback and collaboration with peers. [[31]](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/2496) Most importantly, this mechanism is not supported only by complaints: in Zheng et al.’s dental-school study, **engagement with faculty and classmates statistically predicted perceived online-course effectiveness**, while Tsang et al. found that **student–student dialogue predicted perceived learning outcomes** and instructor–student dialogue predicted initiative. [[32]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z) Foo et al.’s lower PBL scores in participation, communication and group skills provide an objective-performance analogue: modes whose learning mechanism depends heavily on collaborative interaction appear especially difficult to reproduce remotely. [[33]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02575-1)

**Negative emotions.** Anxiety, isolation, frustration and low motivation can consume attention and undermine self-regulation, producing procrastination, weak concentration and disengagement even when course materials are technically available. Buttler et al.’s structural model is particularly informative: positive emotional response was a significant predictor of perceived learning and willingness to continue attending an online university, while professor support and caring also contributed to learning outcomes. [[34]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7905384/) Means and Neisler found that students across backgrounds struggled to remain motivated after the emergency transition. [[31]](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/2496) Yet emotional mechanisms should not be overstated as universal causal explanations: Mostafa et al. found that students’ direct emotional experience with COVID-19 illness among friends or family was not significantly associated with GPA change once other variables were modeled. [[12]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0264947) Thus, affect appears most consequential when it manifests as **learning-related motivation, stress and self-regulation**, rather than merely exposure to pandemic events.

**Convenience and flexibility.** Online learning can reduce commuting, permit replay of recorded material, allow students to work at their own pace, and make attendance possible when health, geography or scheduling would otherwise prevent it. In Bączek et al., 69% valued being able to stay home, 69% valued continuous access to materials, and 64% valued self-paced learning; 73% found e-learning enjoyable despite its disadvantages. [[30]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7899848/) Muthuprasad et al. similarly identified flexibility and convenience as major attractions, with 70% willing to opt for online classes during the pandemic. [[3]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291120300905) Buttler et al. found that a relaxed schedule positively predicted grades and willingness to attend when instruction remained online. [[34]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7905384/) The mechanism is therefore a reduction in **time and location constraints**, which can increase access and permit better pacing; but excessive flexibility can also reduce external structure, making self-discipline more important. Hardt et al.’s RCT reinforces this interpretation: structured online peer mentoring improved study behavior and motivation, suggesting that some students need externally supplied organization to convert flexibility into productive learning rather than disengagement. [[35]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9251909/)

The principal caveat is that most pandemic studies measured **emergency remote teaching**, not mature, intentionally designed online education. Convenience samples, self-reported outcomes, retrospective comparison with pre-pandemic classes, simultaneous changes in assessment and grading, unequal access to technology, and discipline-specific requirements all limit generalization. Even the strongest randomized evidence is locally specific—Cheung et al. studied statistics courses at one Hong Kong university, while the online-design RCTs in Germany studied particular interventions rather than the entire online-versus-face-to-face contrast. [[36]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full) The most defensible interpretation for January 2020–August 2023 is therefore that **online learning itself was not uniformly inferior; effectiveness depended heavily on infrastructure, deliberate instructional design, preserved social interaction, students’ emotional/self-regulatory capacity, and whether flexibility was supported by sufficient structure**.
