## 2. Effectiveness Analysis

### 2.1 Overall conclusion: online learning was conditionally effective, not uniformly effective

The reviewed evidence does **not** support a simple conclusion that online learning during COVID-19 was either broadly inferior or broadly superior to face-to-face learning. The strongest conclusion is that **online learning was conditionally effective**: it could sustain learning and, in some courses or for some students, equal or outperform face-to-face instruction, but its effectiveness depended heavily on course design, instructor presence, student preparedness, infrastructure, and the distinction between planned online learning and emergency remote teaching.

The most persuasive achievement evidence comes from comparative and controlled studies rather than satisfaction surveys. The King Saud University study is especially important because its interpretation changed after statistical adjustment. Unadjusted results initially suggested lower online scores in most courses, but after controlling for gender, class size, and admission scores, online learning was associated with higher performance in three of five freshman core courses, face-to-face learning was favored in one course, and one course showed no format effect ([King Saud University study, 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-023-02590-1)). This demonstrates that raw comparisons are often misleading because students and courses are not randomly assigned to modalities.

Similarly, broader pre- and pandemic-adjacent comparative evidence indicates that modality effects can be statistically detectable but practically small. In a large university dataset of more than 5,000 courses, online versus face-to-face format had a grade difference of less than 0.07 GPA points on a 4-point scale after controlling for demographic and academic selection factors, suggesting practical equivalence for many students and courses ([Online Learning Consortium study](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/454)). However, that same evidence shows strong heterogeneity: prior GPA was the dominant predictor of performance, and higher-GPA students may perform even better online while lower-GPA students may do worse online than in face-to-face sections ([Online Learning Consortium study](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/454)).

The evidence from emergency remote teaching is more negative on satisfaction and engagement than on grades. Students in Türkiye reported only moderate satisfaction with ERT, consistent with other pandemic-era studies showing moderate or declining satisfaction; dissatisfaction was linked to rapid course conversion, insufficient adaptation, and students’ preference for familiar face-to-face habits ([Taşkın et al., 2023](https://cjlt.ca/index.php/cjlt/article/download/28242/20716)). The DePaul survey similarly found that students struggled with time management, family and work responsibilities, inadequate home study environments, and reduced interaction with instructors and peers ([Lancaster & Carroll, 2021](https://offices.depaul.edu/student-affairs/support-services/access-and-attainment/aa-research-lab/Documents/Student%20Remote%20Learning%20Report.10.7.21.pdf)). Yet even these studies do not show that online learning was inherently ineffective; rather, they show that **crisis-driven remote instruction often lacked the design quality, interaction, and support systems required for effective online education**.

### 2.2 Cross-sectional perceptual surveys: useful for experience, weaker for causal effectiveness

A large share of pandemic-era evidence consists of cross-sectional surveys asking students how satisfied they were with online or remote learning. These studies are valuable because satisfaction is closely linked to engagement and persistence, but they are limited as evidence of learning effectiveness.

The Malaysian study of 2,097 undergraduates found that remote learning satisfaction was positively associated with instructor performance, student educational goals, the learning platform, workload, online assessment, and learning support ([Malaysian private HEI study, 2023](https://ejournal.usm.my/apjee/article/view/apjee_vol38-no-1-2023_9)). This is informative because it identifies actionable predictors, but it does not prove that online learning caused better academic outcomes. Similarly, Fuchs’s Thailand study found that younger freshmen struggled more with emergency remote teaching, illustrating differential adaptation by year level ([Fuchs, 2021](https://www.ijlter.org/index.php/ijlter/article/view/3906)).

Perceptual surveys often produced more negative findings because they captured the lived experience of a sudden crisis: isolation, poor internet, family responsibilities, anxiety, unfamiliar technology, and rushed course redesign. Students were not merely evaluating “online learning”; they were evaluating emergency instruction under pandemic conditions. This explains why surveys often reported moderate satisfaction or dissatisfaction even when academic performance studies found neutral or mixed grade effects.

### 2.3 Comparative observational studies: stronger on achievement but vulnerable to selection bias

Comparative observational studies are stronger than simple satisfaction surveys when they use administrative grades or assessment scores, but they remain vulnerable to selection bias unless they control for student and course characteristics. The King Saud University study shows this clearly: unadjusted comparisons suggested online underperformance, but regression adjustment reversed or moderated the interpretation ([King Saud University study, 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-023-02590-1)).

This methodological lesson is crucial. Online sections may differ from face-to-face sections in student composition, class size, instructor, assessment format, course level, and student prior achievement. Without adjustment, online learning can appear ineffective simply because more at-risk students enrolled in online formats—or appear effective because more self-regulated students selected online sections. Comparative studies with controls therefore provide a more credible but still non-randomized estimate of effectiveness.

### 2.4 Randomized and quasi-experimental studies: strongest evidence, but narrower interventions

Randomized trials and quasi-experimental studies provide the strongest causal evidence, though often for specific interventions rather than online learning as a whole. Hardt, Nagler, and Rincke’s RCT of one-on-one remote peer mentoring during a COVID-driven transition found improvements in motivation, study behavior, and exam registrations, but no average increase in earned credits. Effects were strongest for the most able students and more pronounced for men ([Hardt et al., 2020/2022](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ces/ceswps/_8671.html)). This suggests that online-learning effectiveness can be improved by structured support, but support interventions may not automatically translate into completed credits for all students.

Quasi-experimental mentoring and first-year transition programs also suggest that structured support matters. A Chilean propensity-score-matched study found that peer mentoring improved grades, attendance, and retention for underrepresented first-year students, with especially large retention gains among female, daytime, and technical-professional students ([Venegas-Muggli et al., 2021](https://www.evidencebasedmentoring.org/the-impact-of-peer-mentoring-on-the-academic-success-of-underrepresented-college-students/)). Although not exclusively an online-learning study, it is highly relevant because pandemic online learning increased the need for belonging, self-organization, and institutional navigation support.

### 2.5 Evidence quality remains uneven

The pandemic-era evidence base is mixed and thinner in high-quality causal studies than the volume of publications might suggest. TASO’s rapid review found post-pandemic studies using RCT, quasi-experimental, and empirical designs, but concluded that stronger causal evidence was still needed, especially in the UK context ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)). This supports the central conclusion: **online learning during COVID-19 was neither a failure nor a guaranteed substitute for face-to-face teaching; it was effective when supported by strong design, interaction, infrastructure, and student support, and less effective when delivered as minimally adapted emergency remote teaching**.

## 3. Analysis of Influencing Factors

### 3.1 Infrastructure: access, devices, internet, and LMS reliability

Infrastructure was one of the most direct determinants of online-learning effectiveness. Its mechanism is straightforward: unreliable internet, inadequate devices, high data costs, and inaccessible learning platforms reduce students’ ability to attend live sessions, submit work, participate in discussions, watch recordings, and access support. Infrastructure problems also increase cognitive load because students must spend effort solving technical problems rather than learning.

The CJLT study explicitly identifies technical problems as a factor affecting satisfaction and argues that LMS usefulness and ease of access are basic requirements for online learning ([Taşkın et al., 2023](https://cjlt.ca/index.php/cjlt/article/download/28242/20716)). The DePaul report provides concrete student-level evidence: participants reported inadequate home study environments, weak internet, lack of fully functioning laptops, and difficulty accessing campus resources such as labs, libraries, faculty office hours, tutoring, study groups, and computers ([Lancaster & Carroll, 2021](https://offices.depaul.edu/student-affairs/support-services/access-and-attainment/aa-research-lab/Documents/Student%20Remote%20Learning%20Report.10.7.21.pdf)). These infrastructure barriers made remote learning less effective, particularly for students with fewer financial and family resources.

Evidence from resource-constrained settings reinforces this mechanism. A Mozambique study of Google Classroom during the 2020–2021 academic year found moderate satisfaction, with internet connectivity quality as the most significant predictor of satisfaction and device type also influential; qualitative challenges included connectivity problems, mobile data costs, social isolation, technical difficulties, limited interaction, and workload concerns ([Comia, Alberto, & Guerra, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1742339/full)). Although published after the focal review period, it directly studies pandemic-era learning and illustrates why infrastructure is not a peripheral technology issue but a core equity and learning issue.

### 3.2 Instructional design and teaching presence

Instructional design was a major differentiator between effective online learning and weak emergency remote teaching. Planned online learning requires alignment among learning outcomes, content, activities, assessment, feedback, and interaction. Emergency remote teaching often lacked this alignment because instructors and institutions had little time to redesign courses.

Teaching presence—design and organization, facilitation, and direct instruction—is repeatedly identified as a strong predictor of satisfaction, perceived learning, and sense of community in online courses ([Shehzad & Charles, 2023](https://www.cedtech.net/download/exploring-the-impact-of-instructor-social-presence-on-student-engagement-in-online-higher-education-13823.pdf)). The UAE mixed-methods study found that instructor social presence positively influenced student engagement and identified concrete practices such as clear instructions, visible due dates, instructor contact information in LMS announcements, dry runs of online activities, and personalized course design ([Shehzad & Charles, 2023](https://www.cedtech.net/article/exploring-the-impact-of-instructor-social-presence-on-student-engagement-in-online-higher-education-13823)).

The mechanism is that strong instructional design reduces ambiguity, supports self-regulation, and makes expectations visible. Instructor presence reduces transactional distance: students know what to do, when to do it, how to get help, and whether the instructor is actively monitoring learning. This explains why the Malaysian study found instructor performance, platform quality, workload, assessment, and learning support all related to satisfaction ([Malaysian private HEI study, 2023](https://ejournal.usm.my/apjee/article/view/apjee_vol38-no-1-2023_9)).

### 3.3 Lack of social interaction and weak belonging

Reduced social interaction negatively affected effectiveness by weakening motivation, belonging, help-seeking, peer learning, and persistence. Online learning can include rich interaction, but many pandemic courses were rapidly converted lectures or administrative “read, respond, repeat” formats.

The DePaul report provides clear qualitative evidence: students missed interaction with professors and peers, felt a loss of the college experience, and described remote learning as less mentally engaging ([Lancaster & Carroll, 2021](https://offices.depaul.edu/student-affairs/support-services/access-and-attainment/aa-research-lab/Documents/Student%20Remote%20Learning%20Report.10.7.21.pdf)). The CJLT study similarly reports that students longed for face-to-face education and that satisfaction was linked to engagement indicators such as LMS logins, live-course attendance, and recorded-course views ([Taşkın et al., 2023](https://cjlt.ca/index.php/cjlt/article/download/28242/20716)).

Instructor social presence can partially compensate for reduced physical presence. The UAE study argues that teacher social presence contributes to connectedness, engagement, retention, and reduced attrition ([Shehzad & Charles, 2023](https://www.cedtech.net/download/exploring-the-impact-of-instructor-social-presence-on-student-engagement-in-online-higher-education-13823.pdf)). Peer mentoring evidence points in the same direction: remote peer mentoring improved motivation and study behavior in Germany, while Chilean peer mentoring improved grades, attendance, and retention for underrepresented students ([Hardt et al., 2020/2022](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ces/ceswps/_8671.html); [Venegas-Muggli et al., 2021](https://www.evidencebasedmentoring.org/the-impact-of-peer-mentoring-on-the-academic-success-of-underrepresented-college-students/)).

### 3.4 Negative emotions, stress, and motivation

Negative emotions reduced effectiveness by undermining attention, persistence, self-regulation, and willingness to participate. Pandemic online learning occurred amid illness risk, social isolation, financial instability, family responsibilities, and uncertainty. These conditions made learning more emotionally demanding than normal online courses.

A systematic review of engagement factors during pandemic online learning found that student self-efficacy, emotion, motivation, personality, peer interaction, self-care, instructor readiness, course design, technology usefulness, and demographics all shaped engagement ([Limbu, 2025](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/4221)). The DePaul report provides concrete examples: students reported stress, difficulty concentrating, difficulty balancing family and school responsibilities, and the feeling that online learning placed more responsibility on students to teach themselves ([Lancaster & Carroll, 2021](https://offices.depaul.edu/student-affairs/support-services/access-and-attainment/aa-research-lab/Documents/Student%20Remote%20Learning%20Report.10.7.21.pdf)).

The mechanism is that stress and anxiety consume working memory and reduce executive functioning. In online learning, where students must manage time, technology, communication, and independent study, emotional strain can quickly translate into missed sessions, incomplete assignments, and disengagement. This is why mentoring interventions that targeted motivation and study behavior had positive process effects even when they did not immediately raise earned credits ([Hardt et al., 2020/2022](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ces/ceswps/_8671.html)).

### 3.5 Convenience and flexibility

Convenience and flexibility were among the strongest positive features of online learning. Online learning allowed students to access materials anywhere and anytime, review recorded lectures, avoid commuting, and fit study around work and family obligations. This mechanism can increase participation for students whose constraints make fixed face-to-face attendance difficult.

The CJLT article notes that online learning’s convenience and flexibility have made it part of contemporary education and expanded access to teaching content ([Taşkın et al., 2023](https://cjlt.ca/index.php/cjlt/article/download/28242/20716)). DePaul students also reported that some adjusted well because remote learning increased time flexibility and made some courses more accessible ([Lancaster & Carroll, 2021](https://offices.depaul.edu/student-affairs/support-services/access-and-attainment/aa-research-lab/Documents/Student%20Remote%20Learning%20Report.10.7.21.pdf)).

However, flexibility has a dual effect. It benefits self-regulated students but can disadvantage students with weaker time management, lower prior academic preparation, or unstable home environments. This aligns with evidence that prior GPA is a dominant predictor of online course grades and that higher-GPA students may perform better online while lower-GPA students may do worse than in face-to-face formats ([Online Learning Consortium study](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/454)). Therefore, flexibility should not be treated as automatically beneficial; it must be paired with structure, reminders, formative assessment, and accessible support.

### 3.6 Student preparedness, prior achievement, and support systems

Student preparedness strongly moderated online-learning effectiveness. Prior online learning experience itself may be less important than expected: in a study of 878 online learners, the number of previous online courses was related only to social presence and emotional engagement, with small effect sizes, and was unrelated to satisfaction, teaching presence, and cognitive presence ([Duha et al., 2022](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/2949)). This suggests that institutions should not assume students will become effective online learners simply through exposure.

More important are academic self-regulation, prior achievement, and support. First-generation and underrepresented students often need structured transition support, especially when online learning reduces informal access to campus norms and resources. First-year seminars that scaffold academic tasks, build belonging, and connect students to institutional resources are associated with higher GPAs and persistence among first-generation students ([Vaughan et al., 2014](https://firstexperiences.utah.edu/_resources/documents/first-generation-college-student-achievement-first-year-seminar-a-quasi-experimental-design.pdf)). During pandemic online learning, similar supports—mentoring, advising, tutoring, proactive instructor communication—were not optional add-ons but mechanisms for making online learning equitable.
