# Online Learning in Higher Education During the COVID-19 Pandemic (January 2020–August 2023): A Systematic Review of Effectiveness and Influencing Factors

This review synthesises empirical higher-education studies of online, remote, and blended learning published between January 2020 and August 2023. Most of the evidence reflects **emergency remote teaching (ERT)** — the rapid, unplanned crisis shift to remote instruction — rather than intentionally designed online learning, a distinction that bounds every causal interpretation below ([Hodges et al., 2020](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2020/3/the-difference-between-emergency-remote-teaching-and-online-learning)). A study is coded **Effective**, **Neutral**, or **Ineffective** on its dominant outcome, tagged as self-reported/perceptual or objective/measured.

## 1. Summary Table of Reviewed Studies

The 15 empirical studies span 11 countries/regions, sample sizes from 22 to 13,447, and four design buckets. Cross-sectional perceptual surveys form the largest cluster; only two RCT/quasi-experiments and one longitudinal study meet the highest causal-evidence standards. Verdicts split into seven Effective, five Neutral, and three Ineffective, with several mixed or conditional findings.

## 2. Effectiveness Analysis

### Overall conclusion

Online learning during the pandemic was **moderately effective for satisfaction and perceived learning but neutral-to-modestly negative for objectively measured academic achievement**. The two verdicts diverge systematically, and that divergence is the central interpretive fact of the literature. For achievement, the largest randomised estimate finds a penalty of 0.215 standard deviations (SD) for online students ([Kofoed et al., 2024](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077)), a randomised Hong Kong trial finds no overall difference but a face-to-face advantage in small classes ([Cheung et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full)), and the strongest positive quasi-experimental estimate (+0.22 SD) comes from Chinese *middle schools*, outside the higher-education boundary ([Clark et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9760097/)). For satisfaction, large surveys report acceptable experiences ([Simsek et al., 2021](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1303630.pdf); [She et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.743936/full)), and a meta-analysis estimates that only 13.3% of students held negative attitudes ([Liu, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9564387/)). The [TASO (2023)](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf) rapid review captures the asymmetry: a small number of studies suggest online learning can maintain or improve outcomes for some groups, but "overall, the move to online learning appears associated with worse student outcomes." Because nearly all evidence concerns ERT, this verdict describes crisis-mode delivery more than online pedagogy in general.

### Findings by research methodology

#### Cross-sectional perceptual surveys

Perceptual surveys measure something different from achievement: their dominant verdict is moderate-to-positive on satisfaction. Among 13,447 Turkish students, satisfaction was moderate ([Simsek et al., 2021](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1303630.pdf)); among 1,504 Chinese students, satisfaction was driven by interaction through academic self-efficacy and engagement ([She et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.743936/full)); among 400 Hong Kong students, attitude was most strongly predicted by peer interaction and course design ([Chu et al., 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/13/9/5038)); and among 808 students across six Balkan countries, satisfaction correlated with all three Community of Inquiry presences ([Arsenijević et al., 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/13/4/364)). Two surveys returned negative verdicts where access failed: Indonesian students' dominant themes were "ineffective" and "unpleasant" ([Saputra et al., 2021](https://www.eu-jer.com/student-perceptions-of-online-learning-during-the-covid-19-pandemic-in-indonesia-a-study-of-phenomenology)), and students across five developing countries faced structural digital-divide barriers, with gendered inequalities worsening outcomes ([Mathrani et al., 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14767724.2021.1981253)). None of these designs contains a counterfactual — a satisfaction rating is a judgement about one condition only, so students can be "satisfied" with a course that yields little learning gain. Perceptual verdicts also vary by subgroup: Simsek et al. found Medicine/Health Sciences students less satisfied than Science/Engineering and Social Sciences students, with gender differences.

#### Cross-sectional comparative studies

Comparative studies occupy a middle position and report consistently more neutral verdicts. Three comparison strategies exist, each with a different validity threat. In simultaneous-cohort studies, online and in-person sections run in the same term with the same instructor and materials: [Gulati et al. (2023)](https://zilles.cs.illinois.edu/papers/gulati_online_vs_in-person_asee_2023.pdf) found formative assessments statistically equivalent, exams 2.4 percentage points higher in-person after GPA control, and a final-average gap of 1.2 points that lost significance (p = 0.067). In historical-control studies, pandemic results are compared with pre-pandemic results: [Zheng et al. (2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z) found the online dental cohort equally or more likely to earn an A in 16 of 17 courses, while [Ortiz et al. (2022)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1361384) found no statistically significant difference in course learning outcomes over six years. In retrospective self-comparison studies, students recall both modalities: [Photopoulos et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9769479/) found students preferred face-to-face and reported higher engagement and understanding, but this was perceptual with no performance counterfactual. Supporting comparative evidence shows the same neutrality: [Iglesias-Pradas et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8631572/) found performance *increased* during ERT across 43 telecommunication-engineering courses but attributed this to organisational factors, with no difference by class size or delivery mode. Because none of these designs randomly assigned students, selection effects remain the central threat: students self-select into sections; historical controls confound modality with cohort ability, assessment changes, and the emergency context. A pre-pandemic review of 91 comparative studies found 41% favoured online, 18% face-to-face, and 41% found no significant difference ([Stevens et al., 2021](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/online-university-education-is-the-new-normal-but-is-face-to-face/)), and [TASO (2023)](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf) concluded it was "not possible to untangle the effect of a module being taught online, from the effect of it being taught in the context of the pandemic." The cautious conclusion is that measured performance differences were generally small and often non-significant, but a genuine modality effect cannot be cleanly separated from selection and emergency-context confounding.

#### Longitudinal and repeated-measure studies

Only one core study used a longitudinal design: [Sulla et al. (2022)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.825047/full) surveyed 176 Italian undergraduates across two waves and found that grit predicted final-exam grades through self-efficacy, with psychological distress moderating the first stage of the mediation. Stable individual traits, not a time trend, differentiated performance — so no uniform "learning curve" is demonstrated. Administrative-panel evidence sharpens the picture: [Rodríguez-Planas (2021/2022)](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/14113/covid-19-and-college-academic-performance-a-longitudinal-analysis) found top-performing lower-income students' grades fell 5% and earned credits 11% in spring 2020 relative to higher-income peers, while bottom-quartile lower-income students' GPA rose 9% — a gain the author attributes to credit/no-credit grading rather than learning. Post-period anchors (outside this review's window) reinforce the pattern: [Wladis et al. (2024)](https://cwladis.net/publications/) found worse ERT outcomes for two-year college students, men, and Black and Hispanic students *without* prior online experience, and no differential trend among students with prior experience, implying that prior experience — not accumulated time in remote learning — buffered the shock. Because verified panels cover essentially the spring 2020 transition, later recovery is untested, and no verified repeated-cross-sectional study tracks population-level adaptation.

#### Randomised controlled trials and quasi-experiments

The highest-causal-evidence studies converge on a narrower, generally less favourable verdict than perceptual surveys. [Kofoed et al. (2024)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077) randomised 551 West Point students in a required Introductory Economics course and found final grades for online students dropped by 0.215 SD, concentrated among below-median-ability students; online students also reported difficulty concentrating and feeling less connected to instructors and peers. [Cheung et al. (2023)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full) randomised 725 students across seven statistics courses at the University of Hong Kong and found no statistically significant overall difference, with a face-to-face advantage emerging only in classes of 25 students or fewer; a Pass/Fail grading option significantly lowered performance. Both trials measured objective performance, were conducted in quantitative subjects, and lasted one semester, so the corpus provides no experimental evidence on discussion-based or humanities courses. Clark et al.'s quasi-experiment (+0.22 SD) sits outside the higher-education boundary but demonstrates that even causal designs can return positive estimates under different contexts and identification strategies.

### Why retrospective perceptual surveys diverge from randomised trials

The systematic gap between the two design families follows from four separable mechanisms:

1. **What is measured.** Surveys typically measure satisfaction or perceived learning; RCTs measure grades. These are only loosely correlated, so a student can report liking online learning while scoring lower. She et al.'s satisfaction mediation and Kofoed's parallel perception-and-performance decline show that surveys and RCTs often target different constructs rather than one being simply "wrong."
2. **Comparison standard.** Retrospective surveys ask students to compare current online learning against a *remembered* in-person baseline, with no prospective measurement. Memory is reconstructive: students may idealise the in-person baseline or anchor on its worst features, inflating or deflating the perceived difference. The pandemic's universal shift removed the self-selection that had contaminated pre-pandemic comparisons, creating a natural experiment for identifying who actually benefited or struggled ([Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/what-researchers-learned-about-online-higher-education-during-the-pandemic/)).
3. **Selection.** Students who choose online delivery differ in prior ability, motivation, and home environment; observational comparisons cannot separate modality effects from these confounders, whereas randomisation balances them. Kofoed et al. explicitly note that self-selection of students, instructors, and institutions made causal inference difficult before their trial.
4. **Emergency context as an effect modifier.** ERT bundled modality with pandemic distress, instructor inexperience, and infrastructure inequality. TASO emphasises that course-design quality "was not possible with the emergency switch to remote learning," and identifies digital poverty as the largest barrier — so modality was never tested in isolation.

Cheung et al.'s neutral overall headline and Clark et al.'s positive quasi-experimental estimate directly disconfirm any simple claim that "RCTs uniformly show online learning is worse": the verdict depends on identification strategy, outcome measure, class size, prior ability, and context.

| Cross-sectional comparative | Neutral-to-Effective; direction depends on control type | Non-random assignment; historical controls confounded by cohort and assessment change; closer to a counterfactual than perception | Zheng; Gulati; Ortiz |

| RCT / quasi-experiment | Neutral-to-Ineffective on measured achievement | Randomisation breaks selection; measures objective performance; short duration and ERT confound limit external validity | Kofoed; Cheung |

### Confidence, gaps, and limits on interpretation

Confidence in the overall verdict is **moderate-to-low**. The causal evidence base is thin — two higher-education RCTs, both single-institution and single-subject — and is dominated by cross-sectional self-report surveys. Ten of fifteen studies rely on perceptual outcomes; completion and retention are not systematically measured. The corpus is geographically concentrated (East Asia, North America, Europe), with Africa and Latin America under-represented, and almost all studies examine ERT rather than designed online provision, so results may not transfer to post-pandemic online learning. Several figures cited here were verified at publisher abstract/record level because full texts were inaccessible in the review window. Two headline meta-analytic estimates measure different constructs and must not be conflated: [Di Pietro (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36987429/) estimates the pandemic's *overall* learning deficit at about 0.19 SD (closures, disruption, distress), not an online-versus-face-to-face effect, whereas the post-period meta-analysis by [Xiong et al. (2025)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10494820.2024.2375347) estimates a modest positive online-versus-face-to-face effect (g = 0.336, 95% CI 0.139–0.534) once designed online and blended formats are pooled. That distinction cautions against reading the ERT-era verdict as a permanent property of online learning.

## 3. Analysis of Influencing Factors

The five factors do not operate independently. The verified evidence is consistent with a sequential chain — **infrastructure gates what instructional design can deploy; design determines social presence; social presence buffers negative emotions; and emotions feed back into engagement and learning** — with convenience/flexibility moderating every arrow. These are testable hypotheses, not established facts, because the corpus is dominated by cross-sectional perceptual surveys.

### Infrastructure and the digital divide

Infrastructure operated through a sequential causal chain: **access** (device, broadband reliability, platform stability, electricity, usable home learning space, institutional IT support) → **participation** (logging in, joining live sessions, downloading materials, submitting assignments) → **engagement** (interaction with content, instructors, and peers) → **learning outcomes**. This mirrors Van Dijk's three-level digital divide — access, digital skills and usage, and outcomes — in which the first level acts as a hard gate and the third captures unequal results when the first two are unevenly distributed ([Ireland & Lestari, 2025](https://www.cambridgeassessment.org.uk/Images/745788-digital-divide-a-literature-review.pdf), summarising Van Dijk).

Specific studies illustrate each stage. [Mathrani et al. (2022)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14767724.2021.1981253) surveyed male and female students across India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Afghanistan and found that structural lack of digital media access and supporting services placed female students lower on the access scale; women also reported more stress from added household responsibilities, challenging their capacity to maximise learning outcomes — a mechanism running through both access and the gendered practices that constrain usage even where access exists. [Jamil et al. (2023)](https://research.nottingham.edu.cn/en/publications/the-covid-19-pandemic-and-e-learning-the-digital-divide-and-educa/) (abstract-level; full text not independently verified) studied Pakistani universities and found that most students — especially those from rural and remote areas — lacked proper Internet access and could not use laptops or virtual learning systems, while upper- and middle-class urban students at private universities were less affected, a clean illustration of the third-level divide. [Roy et al. (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14073) modelled eight factors at Gulf University, Bahrain: the model explained 39.2% of variance in perceived effectiveness, and institutional support — encompassing IT infrastructure, IT support services, and faculty development — had a significant positive effect; the authors explicitly name poor connectivity and the non-availability of devices, particularly in rural areas of developing countries, as barriers. At review level, [Koh (2022)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-022-00361-7) found that online access and positive coping did not eradicate infrastructure and home-environment challenges, and [TASO (2023)](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf) identifies digital poverty as "the largest barrier," disproportionately affecting disadvantaged groups. Di Pietro's aggregate estimate of a ~0.19 SD pandemic learning deficit embeds infrastructure-related inequalities but does not isolate them, so attribution remains inferential. Infrastructure provision works best bundled with pedagogical and technical support rather than delivered as hardware alone (Roy et al.), and device-lending interventions were deployed unevenly even in K-12 systems ([CRPE, 2020](https://crpe.org/the-digital-divide-among-students-during-covid-19-who-has-access-who-doesnt/), a context anchor rather than higher-education evidence). The evidence base here is dominated by self-reported perceptual measures and qualitative accounts; objective links between infrastructure quality and measured achievement remain scarce.

### Instructional design and pedagogy

Instructional design was the most decisive *modifiable* factor, operating through the chain **design → interaction → presence → motivation → learning**. The Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework supplies the anchor: *teaching presence* (design, facilitation, direct instruction), *social presence* (effective and open communication, group cohesion), and *cognitive presence* (progressive inquiry from exploration to resolution); sense of community is significantly associated with perceived learning ([Garrison, CoI framework](https://teaching.cornell.edu/sites/default/files/2020-05/Community%20of%20Inquiry%20Framework.pdf)).

Active learning was the strongest design lever. [Roy et al. (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14073) found that active learning and institutional support had statistically significant positive impacts on perceived effectiveness, and recommended "strengthening the pedagogy of active learning across courses." [Chu et al. (2021)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/13/9/5038) found course design among the most salient predictors of student attitude, and [Arsenijević et al. (2023)](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/13/4/364) found satisfaction positively correlated with all three CoI presences. Interaction design matters because social presence must evolve from socio-emotional support toward group cohesion with intellectual focus: [TASO (2023)](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf) reports that courses encouraging active engagement through *planned* student–student interaction and feedback increase attainment, whereas lecture-only replication suppresses presence. Assessment design and timing also matter: poorly timed or overly frequent online assessment can overwhelm students and undermine engagement, while well-designed assessment reinforces learning (Roy et al.; TASO). Instructor training and faculty development improved design quality, platform fluency, and facilitation skills ([Sum et al., 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-022-00364-4)).

Why ERT underperformed relative to intentional design follows from three mechanisms: **no planning time** (design planning "was not possible with the emergency switch," TASO); **transfer rather than transformation** (moving face-to-face materials online rather than engineering them for the medium); and **variable faculty preparation** (faculty largely self-explored strategies without structured support, Sum et al.). The converging design lessons are: prioritise active learning and planned student–student interaction over content transmission; build structured instructor feedback and presence into course design; align assessment with online affordances and manage timing to avoid fatigue; invest in pedagogical — not merely technical — faculty development; and treat online course design as a distinct professional activity rather than a translation of face-to-face materials.

### Lack of social interaction and isolation

The evidence separates two phenomena that are often conflated: **structural isolation** — the physical separation imposed by lockdowns, campus closures, and social-distancing rules — and **design-induced isolation** — the absence of interaction channels in lecture-only or low-participation online formats. Both were associated with lower effectiveness, through partly different mechanisms, and neither was reliably offset by virtual social contact.

Three pathways connect reduced interaction to lower effectiveness. First, **reduced social presence lowers belonging and motivation, producing disengagement**: [Chu et al. (2021)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/13/9/5038), surveying 400 students across 11 Hong Kong universities, found that peer interaction and course design had the most salient impact on students' attitude toward online learning, while interaction with instructors had no statistically significant effect — a finding grounded in self-determination theory, which treats relatedness as a basic psychological need whose frustration undermines motivation. Second, **the loss of informal peer learning weakens conceptual clarification**: spontaneous question-and-answer, study groups, and corridor discussions have no automatic online equivalent, and [Su et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8661517/) identified learner–learner and learner–instructor interaction among six factors shaping online learning experiences, while [Juntunen et al. (2022)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01443410.2022.2135686) reported that remote teaching changed peer interaction through increased social isolation, linking it to motivation and psychological well-being. Third, **isolation elevates stress, which impairs cognition**: [Hager et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8990489/) found that loneliness serially affected depression through boredom and repetitive negative thinking among 199 U.S. college students — rumination and low mood consume attentional resources that would otherwise support study.

Virtual social interaction did not substitute for co-presence. [Towner et al. (2022)](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/a832eaac-c49d-4c0e-89fd-b33468d32935) found that larger households — an index of lower objective isolation — were associated with lower loneliness (b = −3.01, t(79) = 2.60, p = .011) and higher happiness (b = 22.86, t(75) = 3.30, p = .001), but, contrary to expectations, there was no association between loneliness and the frequency of virtual social interactions, and none between virtual interaction frequency and happiness or depression. What mattered was objective co-residence, not mediated contact — a finding that cautions against assuming online collaboration tools automatically offset isolation. [Photopoulos et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9769479/) found that 60% of students preferred face-to-face teaching, 31% preferred remote teaching, and 9% expressed no preference, with higher engagement, learning, and understanding reported in the classroom. The boundary here is important: most evidence is perceptual and cross-sectional, and under ERT, structural and design-induced isolation were confounded and cannot be cleanly separated.

### Negative emotions, stress, and mental health

Negative emotional states generally impaired learning through three mechanisms: **attentional depletion** (worry competes with course content for cognitive resources), **reduced self-efficacy**, and **cognitive load**. Across 30,383 students in 62 countries, [Aristovnik et al. (2020)](http://www.covidsoclab.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Aristovnik-et-al.-2020-COVID-19-and-Higher-Education-Students-Sustainability.pdf) found boredom, anxiety, and frustration during the move online, with deficient computer skills and a heavier perceived workload blocking any perception of improved performance. A meta-analysis of 40 papers (n = 98,558) pooled a 13.3% rate of negative attitudes (95% CI 10.0–17.5) and a 12.7% rate of poor online-learning performance (95% CI 9.6–16.8), with poor performance concentrated in the early pandemic (p = 0.006) and negative attitudes significantly predicting poor performance (p = 0.026) ([Liu, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9564387/)).

The most robustly supported pathways are reduced self-efficacy and attentional depletion. [Al-Nasa'h et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8662340/) found online-learning self-efficacy positively associated with satisfaction while general anxiety and fear of COVID-19 were negatively related, with the highest-satisfaction profile combining high self-efficacy, moderate anxiety, and low fear. [Sulla et al. (2022)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.825047/full) showed that grit affected final-exam grades through self-efficacy, but that psychological distress moderated this mediation — the protective effect of grit weakened as distress rose. [Juntunen et al. (2022)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01443410.2022.2135686) found that strain negatively predicted engagement and positively predicted exhaustion and depressive symptoms, while "positively ambitious" motivational profiles were most adaptive. [Hager et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8990489/) traced loneliness → boredom → repetitive negative thinking → depression, and [Towner et al. (2022)](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/a832eaac-c49d-4c0e-89fd-b33468d32935) found depressive symptoms rose sharply after the pandemic onset (mean increase 8.35, 95% CI [6.97, 9.73]). Buffers existed but were uneven: [Wang et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8755984/) found emotional competence predicted performance for adolescents but not for the college subsample, where only online-learning readiness remained significant.

The literature does not cleanly separate pandemic-context distress (fear of COVID-19) from online-learning-specific distress (study strain), and the strongest longitudinal evidence rests on a single 176-student sample. Direct bidirectional tests were not located: Liu established that negative attitudes predict poor performance, consistent with a feedback loop in which struggle deepens distress and distress deepens struggle, but the reverse path was not directly tested.

### Convenience, flexibility, and the autonomy trade-off

Convenience and flexibility were the most consistently reported benefits of pandemic online learning and a leading driver of satisfaction, yet they functioned as an **enabler rather than an active ingredient** of learning. Three benefit mechanisms recur: **autonomy support** (control over when, where, and how to study), **time savings and location independence**, and **replayability and self-paced review**. [She et al. (2021)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.743936/full) found that flexibility, accessibility, and convenience gave students more control over content and time, and that academic self-efficacy translated into engagement and satisfaction in the self-directed distance-education mode. [Elshami et al. (2021)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10872981.2021.1920090) identified flexibility as one of the two highest areas of student satisfaction among 370 UAE medical and health-sciences students. [Zheng et al. (2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z) found that 80% of 482 dental students wanted to continue some online instruction post-pandemic, and that the online cohort earned equivalent or better grades in 16 of 17 courses — but crucially, perceived engagement with faculty and classmates predicted perceived effectiveness. For working students and caregivers, flexibility was a necessity rather than a luxury: [Froman et al. (2020)](https://www.mtsac.edu/research/images/RIE-Covid-19-Student-Survey.pdf) documented that 36.3% of students had work hours or pay reduced and many struggled to maintain a regular study schedule at home.

The trade-off is that the same features supporting autonomy can undermine structure. [Han (2022)](https://uknowledge.uky.edu/edp_etds/107/) (a doctoral dissertation rather than a peer-reviewed article) studied 1,216 undergraduates in 35 fully asynchronous sections and found time management was the most challenging aspect at both time points, with self-efficacy for self-regulated learning and academic procrastination forming a bidirectional loop: higher self-efficacy predicted lower later procrastination and better performance, while higher procrastination predicted lower later self-efficacy and worse performance. [TASO (2023)](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf) concludes that asynchronous learning places responsibility for the study schedule entirely on students, and that low-income and academically at-risk students were most negatively affected — flexibility widening, rather than narrowing, self-regulation gaps. The global survey by Aristovnik et al. adds a workload offset: a higher perceived workload prevented students from perceiving improved performance despite convenience. The reconciliation is that convenience operates on the satisfaction and access pathway, not directly on the learning-gain pathway: Roy et al. found that active learning and institutional support — not flexibility alone — were the significant positive predictors of perceived effectiveness.

### Integrative view

The five factors form a chain rather than a set of independent predictors: infrastructure gates design; design determines social presence; social presence buffers negative emotions; emotions transmit into engagement and performance; and flexibility moderates every arrow — poor connectivity rules out synchronous interactive formats, asynchronous flexibility supports reflection but can fragment interaction, and self-paced study helps high-self-regulation students while harming low-self-regulation students. The evidence is strongest for the infrastructure and design preconditions and for the moderating roles of prior preparation and income; it is weakest for the interaction → emotions → effectiveness chain, which rests on convergent cross-sectional and perceptual evidence rather than panel or experimental tests. Learner readiness sits upstream: [Armah et al. (2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844023030104) found that learner presence significantly predicted cognitive, social, and teaching presence, while social and teaching presence directly predicted online course satisfaction.

| Factor | Role in the chain | Strongest evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Infrastructure | Necessary precondition (hard gate) | Mathrani; Roy; TASO; Koh |
| Instructional design | Active ingredient (interaction, feedback, assessment) | Roy; Chu; TASO |
| Social interaction | Active ingredient and mediator (presence → belonging) | Chu; Towner; Garrison CoI |
| Negative emotions | Mediator (attention, self-efficacy, cognitive load) | Kofoed; Liu; Sulla |
| Convenience / flexibility | Moderator (sign and size of other pathways) | Han; TASO; Roy |

The most directly actionable levers are instructional design and institutional support, both of which Roy et al. and TASO identify as significant and modifiable. Infrastructure is necessary but costly and slow; flexibility is easy to adjust but double-edged without self-regulation scaffolding; and negative emotions are best addressed indirectly, through design and interaction, rather than through standalone interventions.
