# Effectiveness of Online Learning in Higher Education During COVID-19: A Systematic Review of Empirical Studies (January 2020 – August 2023)

## 2. Effectiveness Analysis

Synthesizing the 16 studies shows that whether online learning during the pandemic is judged "effective" depends almost entirely on *how* effectiveness is defined and measured. Organizing the studies by methodology makes this pattern visible.

### 2.1 Cross-sectional perceptual surveys without a comparison group

This is by far the most common design in the field (studies 1, 2, 4, 8, 14, 15, plus the perceptual component of 10–13). Students are asked to rate their own satisfaction, perception of effectiveness, or preference on Likert-style scales. These studies divide roughly into two camps:

- Studies in developing or lower-income contexts (1, 2) tend to report **ineffective or mixed** outcomes, with infrastructure and cost repeatedly cited as the binding constraint.
- Studies in middle- and high-income contexts, or in countries with a long pre-pandemic history of online learning (4, 8, 14, 15), tend to report **effective** outcomes, with satisfaction levels typically between 53% and 85%.

Critically, these surveys almost never ask the same question as an RCT would (i.e., "did students learn more or less?"). They ask how students *feel* about the experience. A high satisfaction score therefore can co-exist with objectively lower learning gains, which is exactly the divergence the field is trying to explain.

### 2.2 Cross-sectional comparative studies (online vs. face-to-face recall)

Studies 3, 11, 13, and 16 explicitly ask students to compare their online experience to in-person learning. **All** of them conclude that online learning is *less effective* than face-to-face instruction, particularly for skills development, clinical/practical competencies and social interaction. Even the studies that report high satisfaction (e.g., 73% enjoyable in Bączek et al.; 85% satisfied in the Tencent/Kolb study) explicitly note that the online experience is "not as good as" in-person teaching. The dominant signal in this category is therefore **ineffective relative to face-to-face**.

This design has well-known weaknesses. Students are asked to compare a *forced* emergency online experience against a *voluntary* past in-person experience, often in a discipline that relies on physical labs, clinical placements, or hands-on practice. The comparison is therefore doubly biased: it suffers from negative affect toward the disruption, and from a discipline mismatch (e.g., medicine and STEM labs are intrinsically harder to move online than economics or literature).

### 2.3 Longitudinal / quasi-longitudinal studies

Genuine longitudinal studies in this period are scarce. The closest match is Selco & Habbak (2021), which surveyed 584 STEM students at the same institution in both Spring 2020 (the first, fully chaotic lockdown term) and Fall 2020 (after faculty had received training and a Learning Management System rollout). They found a shift from acute disruption toward partial effectiveness: 61% of students identified benefits in flexibility, convenience, productivity and material accessibility, even as themes of belonging, organization and disconnection persisted.

A second quasi-longitudinal element appears in the Bao (2020) Peking University case study, which tracked the institutional rollout of online learning across more than 4,400 courses and roughly 44,700 students in Spring 2020. Although descriptive rather than quantitative, it documents that a large, research-intensive Chinese university *was* able to deliver all of its courses online within weeks, supported by six instructional strategies (chunked delivery, contingency planning, teaching-assistant support, voice-led instruction, active learning, blended online–offline study).

### 2.4 Randomized controlled trials

RCTs are the gold standard for causal claims about online vs. face-to-face learning. Only two RCTs of meaningful size were published in this window in higher education:

- Kofoed et al. (2021) at the U.S. Military Academy at West Point randomized 551 students in a required introductory economics course to online or in-person sections. Online students scored **0.215 standard deviations lower** on the final grade, with the largest effect among academically at-risk students. A post-course survey attributed the loss to difficulty concentrating in class and weaker connections to instructors and peers.
- Lam et al. (2023) at the University of Hong Kong randomized 725 students in seven statistics courses to synchronous face-to-face or synchronous online sections and found **no statistically significant overall difference** in learning outcomes. The Pass/Fail grading option and larger class size did, however, depress performance.

The two RCTs are not identical in design. Kofoed et al. compared an asynchronous/synchronous online section to in-person; Lam et al. compared *synchronous* online to in-person. The consistent reading is that high-quality synchronous online instruction can be roughly equivalent to in-person, but the *emergency* asynchronous pivot that most institutions actually executed produced measurable learning losses. This is the most important methodological lesson in the field.

### 2.5 Why methodology drives the conclusion

The apparent contradiction in the literature is largely a methodological artifact:

- **Self-report surveys** (the majority of evidence) capture the *experience* of online learning. When institutions prepare well, students like it; when they do not, students dislike it. Either way, satisfaction ≠ learning.
- **Comparative recall surveys** stack the deck: students are asked to compare a sudden, poorly prepared, often device-cluttered experience to their idealized memory of campus life, so they almost always rate online worse.
- **Longitudinal evidence** shows that the experience gets better as institutions adapt, suggesting that some of the "ineffective" findings in early 2020 reflect a one-off shock rather than a stable property of online learning.
- **RCTs** are rare but point in the same direction: emergency online learning in 2020 produced modest-to-large learning losses (Kofoed), while well-designed synchronous online learning can be neutral (Lam).

For policy and pedagogy, the practical reading is that online learning during the pandemic was *less effective* than in-person learning in the narrow sense of academic achievement, but *not ineffective* in the broader sense of supporting continued education. Whether it "worked" depends on what universities optimized for: completion, satisfaction, or measured achievement.

## 3. Analysis of Influencing Factors

The empirical literature converges on a consistent set of factors that positively or negatively influenced online learning effectiveness during the pandemic period. The five factors below appear across most of the reviewed studies; for two of them (infrastructure and social interaction) specific cited studies illustrate the mechanism.

### 3.1 Infrastructure (negative factor in lower-resource settings; precondition everywhere)

Infrastructure was the most frequently cited determinant of effectiveness. The mechanism is direct: when students cannot connect reliably, access course material, or run required software, learning cannot occur. Network congestion was the single most common problem across the reviewed studies.

- Adnan & Anwar (2020) found that 17.5% of Pakistani students had limited or no internet access, with signal strength and the cost of mobile data the main barriers; this is the central reason most of their sample judged online learning ineffective [1].
- Muthuprasad et al. (2021) reported that broadband connectivity in rural India was a major obstacle even though 70% of students were willing to try online classes [2].
- Li et al. (2021), in a national survey of 99,559 Chinese medical students, found that **network congestion was the single most common problem, reported by 76.6% of students**, even though overall satisfaction was high [8].
- Cranfield, Tick, Venter, Blignaut & Renaud (2021) compared students in South Africa, Wales and Hungary and attributed the largest cross-country gap in the online experience to differences in home learning environment and digital infrastructure [12].

The cumulative mechanism: poor infrastructure shortens effective study time, forces students to fall back on mobile phones (which compresses task complexity), and increases stress. It also makes instructor-side interventions (live lectures, breakout rooms, video feedback) harder to deliver, compounding the effects of poor instructional design.

### 3.2 Lack of social interaction (strong negative factor)

The loss of face-to-face social interaction with instructors and peers was the second most consistently reported negative factor. The mechanism operates through two channels:

1. **Constructivist channel.** Learning is socially constructed; without peer discussion and instructor dialogue, knowledge construction is shallower. Brown et al. (2023) identified a "Lost sense of community" theme across 13 focus groups in Welsh higher education and reported that students were "largely satisfied" with course quality but described significant loneliness and isolation [9].
2. **Engagement and motivation channel.** Reduced social contact lowered engagement, increased distraction, and weakened student–instructor relationships.

The Kofoed et al. (2021) RCT provides the cleanest causal evidence: in their post-course survey, online students explicitly reported that they *struggled to concentrate* and *felt less connected* to instructors and peers, which the authors linked directly to the 0.215 SD fall in final grades [5]. Bączek et al. (2021) similarly found that Polish medical students rated online learning as significantly less effective than face-to-face for *social competences* and *skills*, even when knowledge acquisition was comparable [3]. Almahasees et al. (2021) likewise identified "lack of interaction and motivation" as a leading challenge in Jordanian universities [11].

The mechanism, in short: emergency online learning largely preserved content delivery but weakened the social and motivational scaffolding that supports deep learning.

### 3.3 Negative emotions (negative factor; partly a consequence of social isolation)

Pandemic-era online learning coincided with — and partly caused — sharp rises in student stress, anxiety, loneliness and depression. The mechanism is partly biological (uncertainty, lockdown fatigue), partly social (loss of peer networks), and partly technological (Zoom fatigue, screen time, information overload).

Conrad et al. (2022) found that information overload and perceived technical-skill requirements were the strongest predictors of students' perceived *difficulty* with online learning at a Canadian university, which in turn depressed satisfaction [13]. Brown et al. (2023) and Selco & Habbak (2021) both identified themes of exhaustion, distraction, isolation and mental-health strain as a leading downside of the online experience, alongside the recognized positives of flexibility [9][10]. Cross-sectional work in China (Liu & Lin, 2022) and in the UK (Brown et al., 2023) further tied online-only learning to elevated loneliness, social anxiety and reduced psychological well-being. The mechanism is bidirectional: negative emotions degrade attention and memory, and weakened attention and memory in turn deepen those negative emotions, generating a feedback loop.

### 3.4 Instructional design (positive factor when strong; negative factor when absent)

The pedagogical design of the online course and the preparedness of instructors turned out to be a decisive moderator. The mechanism is that well-designed online instruction provides structure, accountability and active engagement, while poorly designed instruction simply transplants a lecture onto a screen.

Sharma et al. (2020) found that, in a sample of 434 Nepalese medical students, the strongest predictors of satisfaction were "learners' dimension" (perceived usefulness) and the *instructors' characteristics and course management* — i.e., the design and delivery of the course [14]. Selco & Habbak (2021) similarly identified "belonging, organization and transparency, and the need for real-world applications" as the dominant themes students wanted from online instruction [10]. Bao (2020) summarized Peking University's successful pivot around six instructional strategies: chunked delivery, contingency planning, teaching-assistant support, voice-led instruction, active learning, and combining online and offline self-study [7]. Almahasees et al. (2021) reached a similar conclusion in the Jordanian context: both faculty and students agreed that *the online modality is useful during the pandemic, but only if instructional design is intentionally adapted* [11].

The mechanism: when instructors are trained in online pedagogy, design for engagement, chunk content, give prompt feedback, and treat students' emotional state as part of the design, online learning approaches in-person effectiveness (as Lam et al.'s 2023 RCT also suggests [6]). When none of these are present, the online experience degrades into "video lecture + quiz," which neither sustains engagement nor produces deep learning.

### 3.5 Convenience and flexibility (positive factor; conditional on self-regulation)

The single most consistent *positive* finding across the literature is that students valued the flexibility and convenience of online learning. The mechanism is that students could learn at their own pace, replay recorded lectures, save commuting time, and balance study with caregiving or paid work.

Muthuprasad et al. (2021) reported that students explicitly cited flexibility and convenience as the main attractions of online learning, alongside cost and time savings [2]. Selco & Habbak (2021) found that 61% of STEM students reported benefits from flexibility, convenience, increased productivity, and the time previously spent commuting [10]. Almahasees et al. (2021) likewise identified "self-learning, low costs, convenience, and flexibility" as the most-cited advantages in their Jordanian sample [11]. Bączek et al. (2021) noted that 69% of Polish medical students valued continuous access to online materials and 64% valued learning at their own pace [3].

The benefit, however, is conditional on self-regulation. Selco & Habbak (2021) and Almusharraf & Khahro (2020) both note that flexibility can become a double-edged sword: students with strong time-management skills thrive, while those without them fall behind. The mechanism is straightforward — flexibility reduces friction but also reduces external structure, so the net effect depends on the student's ability to self-monitor.

### 3.6 Synthesizing the factors

Taken together, the empirical record during January 2020 – August 2023 suggests:

- **Infrastructure and social interaction are the binding constraints in lower-resource contexts** (Pakistan, India, rural and sub-Saharan African settings), where they jointly determine whether online learning is even feasible [1][2][12].
- **In higher-resource contexts, instructional design and emotional well-being become the binding constraints.** Once connectivity and devices are adequate, the next biggest predictors of effectiveness are how the course is designed and how supported students feel socially and emotionally [3][9][10][13][14].
- **Flexibility and convenience are real benefits**, but they amplify, rather than compensate for, the other factors. They help students who already have self-regulation and infrastructure, and they leave the others behind.

For higher-education institutions, the practical implication is that an effective post-pandemic online strategy is not primarily a technology problem: it is a problem of *pedagogical design, social scaffolding, emotional support, and equitable infrastructure access* — exactly the bundle of factors this empirical review identifies as decisive.
