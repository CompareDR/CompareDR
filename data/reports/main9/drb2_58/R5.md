# The Effectiveness of Online Learning in Higher Education during the COVID-19 Pandemic (January 2020 – August 2023): A Systematic Review

**Direct answer (TL;DR):** Across 17 empirical studies published between 2020 and 2023, pandemic-era online learning in higher education was **moderately effective but highly conditional**. Studies that measured *perceptions* mostly reported positive or mixed results, studies that compared *grades* were split between improvement, equivalence, and decline, and the one randomized controlled trial found **no overall difference** between synchronous online and face-to-face learning. Effectiveness was systematically shaped by five factor groups: **infrastructure**, **instructional design**, **lack of social interaction**, **negative emotions**, and **convenience/flexibility** — the first four mostly as constraints, the last as the chief advantage.

**Scope and method of this review.** This review covers empirical studies of online learning effectiveness in higher education conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic period (January 2020 – August 2023). Studies were identified through keyword searches of scholarly databases (queries combining "online learning effectiveness", "higher education", "COVID-19", "student performance", "satisfaction", "randomized controlled trial", "longitudinal", and related terms) and were included if they (i) were published 2020–2023, (ii) studied higher-education students, (iii) reported empirical data on learning effectiveness or its determinants, and (iv) provided verifiable methodological details (country, sample, design, outcome). Seventeen studies spanning Asia, Europe, Africa, North America, Australia, and multi-country global samples met these criteria. Each study was read in full text where openly accessible, and its design and headline statistics were extracted for synthesis. The findings are classified as **effective** (online learning performed as well as or better than the comparator, or was predominantly positively evaluated), **ineffective** (online learning performed significantly worse or was predominantly negatively evaluated), or **neutral/mixed** (no significant difference, or simultaneous positive and negative results).

**Reading the table.** Three features of this evidence base matter for everything that follows. First, the studies are geographically broad — covering East Asia, South Asia, the Middle East, Eastern and Southern Europe, sub-Saharan Africa, North America, and Australia — so the synthesis is not hostage to any single national context. Second, the outcome variables fall into two families that behaved very differently: **subjective perceptions** (satisfaction, perceived learning, perceived effectiveness) and **objective performance** (grades, examination marks, competency scores). Third, the designs range from the weakest causal footing (one-shot perceptual surveys) to the strongest (a randomized controlled trial), and — as Section 2 shows — the answer to "was online learning effective?" depends heavily on which of these designs asked the question.

## Section 2: Effectiveness Analysis

### 2.1 Overall conclusion

Taken as a whole, the evidence indicates that emergency online learning during the pandemic was **neither the disaster that early commentary feared nor the equal of well-designed face-to-face education**. Of the 17 studies, six reported predominantly positive results ([Khan et al., 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/13/1/57); [Gonzalez et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0239490); [Chang et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8339598/); [Su & Guo, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8661517/); [Zheng et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z); [Ali et al., 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/13/7/578)), three reported predominantly negative results ([Foo et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02575-1); [Hamdan et al., 2021](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/IJEM-11-2020-0513/full/html); [Butnaru et al., 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/18/18/9665)), and eight reported neutral, mixed, or strongly conditional results. The distribution of findings is not random, however: it maps closely onto **research methodology**, and only secondarily onto the intrinsic quality of online teaching.

The overall pattern can be summarized in three statements. Perceptual survey studies — which asked students how they felt about online learning — produced a **majority of positive or mixed-positive verdicts**, with flexibility and material access praised even where interaction was criticized. Grade-comparison studies produced **genuinely contradictory results**, with some cohorts improving (Spain, the United States, South Africa on average) and others declining (Hong Kong medical PBL, Australian undergraduate anatomy). And the most rigorous designs — the longitudinal study and the randomized trial — converged on **null or conditional effects**, suggesting that once confounders are controlled, the mode of delivery itself explains surprisingly little of the variation in learning outcomes.

Findings on online-learning effectiveness by research methodology

### 2.2 Cross-sectional perceptual surveys

The most common design in this literature was the one-shot perceptual survey, typically analyzed with Likert-scale descriptives, factor analysis, or structural equation modelling. Nine of the 17 studies fall in this category. Their headline characteristic is that they measure **what students felt**, not what students learned. The results skew positive-to-mixed: Indian students strongly endorsed the ease of sharing materials (73.4%) and flexibility (61.3%) of e-learning ([Khan et al., 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/13/1/57)); Pakistani students overwhelmingly agreed that online learning helped their studies (88.96%) and was a good alternative during the crisis (85.67%) ([Ali et al., 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/13/7/578)); and Chinese students' perceived learning outcomes and satisfaction were driven upward by platform quality and interaction design ([Su & Guo, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8661517/)). Even the large multi-country surveys found reasonable satisfaction with teaching staff — 57.6% satisfied or very satisfied globally ([Aristovnik et al., 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/20/8438)).

Yet the same design family also generated the lukewarm and negative verdicts, which is what makes it "mixed" overall rather than uniformly rosy. Hong Kong students across 11 universities rated their perceived learning outcomes (means 3.20–3.51) and satisfaction (3.69–3.83) **below the midpoint** of a 7-point scale ([Tsang et al., 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/11/8/446)); Jordanian students reported a strikingly low mean satisfaction score of 45.14 (SD 25.62) ([Hamdan et al., 2021](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/IJEM-11-2020-0513/full/html)); and Romanian students reported diminished wellbeing, demotivation, and concentration problems under online education ([Butnaru et al., 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/18/18/9665)). The Korean qualitative survey captures the ambivalence of this design family in a single dataset: the same students who named comfortable environment (27.0%) and time utilization (26.0%) as the top advantages also named network instability (34.1%) and unilateral interaction (17.3%) as the top complaints ([Shim & Lee, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7550861/)).

Two biases discipline how much weight these studies can bear. The first is **self-report bias**: perceived learning correlates weakly with actual learning gains, so a satisfied student is not necessarily a student who learned more. The second is **framing bias**: surveys administered during a deadly pandemic invited students to compare online learning not with an ideal classroom but with no education at all, which plausibly inflated appreciation — 80% of the American dental students in one study wanted some online learning retained even after the pandemic ([Zheng et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z)). These designs are excellent for identifying *which factors* students experienced as helpful or harmful, and much of Section 3 rests on them; they are weak instruments for estimating the *causal effect* of the online mode itself.

### 2.3 Cross-sectional comparative studies (grade and performance comparisons)

The second family compared actual performance — usually course grades or examination marks — between an online cohort and a face-to-face cohort, or within the same students before and after the forced transition. These six studies produced the **widest dispersion of results** in the review, and their disagreements are instructive. On the positive side, American dental students learning online in summer 2020 were equally or more likely to earn an A than the face-to-face 2019 cohort in 16 of 17 comparable courses ([Zheng et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z)); Spanish undergraduates scored significantly higher in post-confinement assessments than in the two pre-pandemic years, including on activities whose format never changed (e.g., 8.1 vs. 6.5–6.7 on one online activity) ([Gonzalez et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0239490)); and Taiwanese dental students who experienced both modes in the same semester rated online learning as more effective for time use, screen clarity, and convenience (all p<0.05) ([Chang et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8339598/)).

On the negative side, Hong Kong medical students taught problem-based learning over Zoom scored significantly lower than propensity-matched face-to-face peers — 37.5 vs. 39.0 overall (p<0.001), with deficits in all five assessed domains including participation, communication, and group skills ([Foo et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02575-1)). The Australian causal-inference study split the difference within a single paper: face-to-face learning improved **undergraduate** anatomy marks by 8.3% but worsened **postgraduate** marks by 8.1% relative to online learning, implying that the effect of mode depends on learner maturity and prior self-direction ([Diong et al., 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-022-00027-6)). The South African study likewise found average grades rose about 5.7 points after the lockdown transition, but the average concealed large distributional effects tied to Internet access quality ([Chisadza et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8250490/)).

Why did cohort comparisons disagree so sharply? Because none of them could fully separate the **mode of delivery** from three confounders that moved in different directions across contexts: (i) **assessment changes** — many courses replaced proctored closed-book exams with open-book or continuous assessment, mechanically raising grades, a possibility Gonzalez et al. partly ruled out using unchanged-format activities but most studies could not; (ii) **grading leniency and pass/fail policies**, which the Hong Kong RCT later quantified at −9.45 points for pass/fail grading ([Cheung et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full)); and (iii) **course-type fit** — interactive, skill-based formats such as PBL and laboratory work depend on the very social channels that video platforms degrade, which is exactly where Foo et al. found the largest deficits, whereas lecture-based content translated to video almost losslessly, which is where Zheng et al. and Chang et al. found parity or gains. The comparative studies therefore answer a narrower question than they appear to: not "is online learning effective?" but "was *this* course, moved online in *this* way, with *this* assessment, effective for *these* students?"

### 2.4 Longitudinal studies

Only one reviewed study tracked the same students across multiple waves of the pandemic period, and its finding is arguably the most theoretically important in the entire set. In a three-wave longitudinal study of 176 Italian university students, grit predicted later examination marks through the mediator of academic self-efficacy — **but only when psychological distress was low**; the grit × distress interaction was significant (t=−2.65, p=0.01), meaning that under high distress the otherwise positive pathway from perseverance to performance collapsed ([Sulla et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.825047/full)). Longitudinal measurement is what makes this conclusion possible: because self-efficacy and distress were measured before the examination marks, the study can speak to temporal ordering rather than mere association.

The scarcity of longitudinal designs in this literature is itself a finding. Cross-sectional surveys taken at a single pandemic moment cannot distinguish stable effects of online learning from transient emotional states, novelty effects, or adaptation over time. The Italian result demonstrates why this matters substantively, not just methodologically: the effectiveness of the *same* online learning environment varied over time as a function of students' evolving psychological state, so any one-shot estimate is an average across a moving target. A literature richer in longitudinal evidence would very likely have reported fewer unconditional verdicts and more conditional ones of the form "effective for these students, at these times, under these emotional conditions."

### 2.5 Randomized controlled trials

The strongest causal evidence in the review comes from a single stratified randomized experiment across seven statistics courses at the University of Hong Kong, in which 725 students were randomized to face-to-face or synchronous online (Zoom) sections of the same course with the same instructor and materials. The result was a clean null: **no statistically significant difference between modes in any of the five assessment outcomes** — participation, assignments, midterm, final examination, or final grade ([Cheung et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full)). Two moderators qualified the null: face-to-face teaching outperformed online teaching in **small classes of 25 or fewer students** (+5.99 points on the final weighted grade, p=0.029; +6.70 on the final exam, p=0.042), and pass/fail grading depressed performance by 9.45 points regardless of mode.

The contrast between this trial and the perceptual surveys is the methodological crux of the review. The RCT randomizes away self-selection (students who choose or thrive online differ from those who do not), holds content and instructor constant, and measures objective outcomes — and it finds that the *average* effect of moving a lecture-based course online is approximately zero. Meanwhile the surveys, which measure remembered satisfaction and relative preference without a counterfactual, found students praising flexibility and complaining about isolation in the same questionnaire. These are not contradictory findings: they measure different constructs. But they mean that **student satisfaction is a poor proxy for learning gains**, and that the headline divergence in this literature — enthusiasm in surveys, nulls in the trial — is largely an artifact of what each design is capable of seeing.

### 2.6 Why methodology shaped the reported results

Synthesizing across the four design families, three systematic mechanisms explain why methodology determined the answer a study produced. First, **construct substitution**: perceptual surveys replace learning with satisfaction, and satisfaction was buoyed by factors (flexibility, saved commuting time, safety from infection) that are genuinely valuable but orthogonal to mastery — which is why surveys asked students to compare online learning with their past in-person experiences and frequently returned favorable or at least forgiving verdicts, while the RCT, measuring assessment scores under randomization, returned a null. Recall-based comparison questions also invite **contrast and nostalgia effects**: students compare a stressful present with an idealized pre-pandemic past, and depending on which dominates, the same experience yields the below-midpoint ratings in Hong Kong ([Tsang et al., 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/11/8/446)) or the strong endorsement in Pakistan ([Ali et al., 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/13/7/578)).

Second, **confounding by assessment and selection**: the comparative grade studies could not fully separate the teaching mode from concurrent changes in assessment format, grading generosity, and student composition, which is why they produced both the largest gains ([Gonzalez et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0239490)) and the largest losses ([Foo et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02575-1)) in the review — the dispersion itself is diagnostic of uncontrolled variation rather than of a true effect in either direction. Third, **aggregation across conditions**: average effects conceal the moderators that both the most rigorous studies detected — class size in the RCT, distress in the longitudinal study, learner level in the causal-inference study. The methodological hierarchy thus does not merely change *how confident* we should be; it changes the *substantive answer*: less rigorous designs ask "did students like it or do better this year?", while rigorous designs ask "does the mode itself cause differences in learning?" — and the answer to the second question is "on average, no; under specific conditions, yes." This is the pandemic-era confirmation of the long-standing "no significant difference" phenomenon in distance education research, now with the crucial addition of well-identified boundary conditions.

## Section 3: Analysis of Influencing Factors

Five factor groups recur across the 17 studies as determinants of whether online learning worked. Their mechanisms of influence are summarized in the figure below and analyzed in turn; for infrastructure and social interaction, specific studies are examined in detail as required.

Five factor groups and their mechanisms of influence

### 3.1 Infrastructure

**Mechanism of impact.** Infrastructure — device availability, Internet connectivity quality, platform stability, and a suitable study space — operates as a **threshold condition and a continuous multiplier** at the same time. As a threshold, it gates participation: a student who cannot sustain a video connection cannot attend a synchronous class at all, so no pedagogical quality downstream can compensate. As a multiplier, connection quality determines *time-on-task and cognitive continuity*: unstable audio and video fragment attention, interrupt assessment, and force repeated re-entry into learning materials. Because access to devices and broadband tracks household income and geography, infrastructure is also the primary channel through which online learning **converted existing social inequality into educational inequality** during the pandemic.

The South African study provides the clearest causal estimate of this mechanism: after controlling for student fixed effects, students who relied on Wi-Fi scored approximately **4.5 points higher** on a 100-point scale than students who relied on mobile data, and students who found the online transition difficult lost 2.1 points — effects large enough to explain most of the otherwise puzzling variation in post-lockdown grades ([Chisadza et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8250490/)). The Korean study shows the same mechanism from the experiential side: network instability was the single most common complaint (34.14% of students) and network stabilization the most demanded improvement (43.31%) ([Shim & Lee, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7550861/)). At global scale, only **59.9%** of the 30,383 surveyed students had a good Internet connection at home — and only **29.2%** in Africa — while nearly half lacked a quiet place to study ([Aristovnik et al., 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/20/8438)). Conversely, where infrastructure was essentially universal, as among the Taiwanese dental students who all owned adequate devices, infrastructure ceased to bind and the online mode was rated *more* effective than the classroom for lecture-type content ([Chang et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8339598/)). This pattern — large negative effects in South Africa and Pakistan, negligible effects in Taiwan and Hong Kong — shows infrastructure acting less as a fixed disadvantage of online learning than as a **boundary condition** on its effectiveness.

### 3.2 Instructional design

**Mechanism of impact.** Where infrastructure permits participation, instructional design determines what participation is worth. The mechanism runs through **cognitive engagement and self-regulated study**: well-structured content, clearly aligned assessment, and deliberately designed interaction opportunities focus attention and support the continuous, spaced study that online formats demand, whereas a lecture camera pointed at a slide deck offloads all regulatory work onto the student. The SEM-based surveys quantify this precisely. In the Chinese study, learner–content interaction was the **strongest predictor** of both perceived learning outcomes (β=0.268) and satisfaction (β=0.248), followed by system quality and course design, while self-discipline also contributed significantly (β=0.126 on outcomes) ([Su & Guo, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8661517/)). In the Hong Kong study, course design significantly predicted perceived learning outcomes (β=0.313) and student–student dialogue was the strongest single predictor (β=0.438), while generic university support had no significant effect — what mattered was not institutional cheerleading but the designed texture of the course itself ([Tsang et al., 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/11/8/446)).

The same mechanism explains the positive outliers among the grade-comparison studies. The Spanish performance gains occurred in courses built around adaptive self-assessment platforms that forced continuous practice — an instructional design that converted the flexibility of online learning into spaced repetition, exactly the study-strategy change the authors documented ([Gonzalez et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0239490)). Assessment design is part of this factor too: the Taiwanese students rated online *learning* highly but online *examinations* as significantly less fair (2.62 vs. 3.21, p<0.001) after a third of them failed to upload answers ([Chang et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8339598/)), and the Hong Kong RCT found that replacing graded with pass/fail assessment cost students 9.45 points regardless of delivery mode ([Cheung et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full)). Instructional design is therefore best understood as the factor that decided whether the *same* technology produced learning or merely attendance.

### 3.3 Lack of social interaction

**Mechanism of impact.** The removal of co-present peers and instructors degraded effectiveness through three channels: reduced **participation and accountability** (nobody notices a silent, camera-off student), weakened **belonging and motivation**, and the loss of **collaborative knowledge construction** — the questioning, explaining, and negotiating through which much understanding is actually built. The clearest performance evidence comes from the Hong Kong medical study, where moving problem-based learning to Zoom produced significantly lower scores in precisely the social domains: participation, communication, preparation, critical thinking, and group skills all declined, driving the overall deficit of 37.5 vs. 39.0 points ([Foo et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02575-1)). Because PBL *is* social interaction, this study functions almost as a pure stress test of the mechanism: strip the interaction from an interaction-based pedagogy and measured performance falls.

Survey evidence corroborates the mechanism from the perception side. In the 11-university Hong Kong study, student–student dialogue was the **strongest predictor of perceived learning outcomes** (β=0.438), ahead of course design and instructor–student dialogue ([Tsang et al., 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/11/8/446)); in the American dental study, perceived engagement with faculty and classmates was the dominant predictor of perceived online effectiveness (p=0.00) ([Zheng et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z)); and in Jordan, learner–learner and learner–content interaction were among the few significant predictors of the otherwise low satisfaction ([Hamdan et al., 2021](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/IJEM-11-2020-0513/full/html)). The Korean students' complaints about "unilateral interaction" (17.29%) ([Shim & Lee, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7550861/)) and the Romanian students' isolation-driven demotivation and inefficiency ([Butnaru et al., 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/18/18/9665)) trace the same deficit. Notably, the RCT's class-size moderator closes the loop causally: face-to-face teaching outperformed online only in classes small enough (≤25 students) for rich personal interaction — implying that what large online lectures lose is not the lecture but the interaction ([Cheung et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full)).

### 3.4 Negative emotions

**Mechanism of impact.** Anxiety, boredom, frustration, and loneliness impaired effectiveness by **consuming the cognitive and motivational resources that self-regulated online learning depends on**. Working memory absorbed by worry is unavailable for processing content; dulled motivation raises the subjective cost of logging in, persisting, and participating; and isolation amplifies both. The pandemic made this mechanism visible at scale: among 30,383 students worldwide, the most frequently experienced emotions were boredom (45.2%), anxiety (39.8%), and frustration (39.1%), and boredom in particular significantly *reduced* students' satisfaction with their university in the regression analysis ([Aristovnik et al., 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/20/8438)). The Romanian SEM results show the emotion→learning pathway directly, with students reporting that they could not concentrate during online courses, felt more stressed, and became less efficient learners in isolation ([Butnaru et al., 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/18/18/9665)).

The decisive evidence that negative emotions moderate *learning itself*, not merely satisfaction, is the Italian longitudinal study: grit predicted exam marks through self-efficacy only among students with **low** psychological distress, and the pathway vanished under high distress (interaction t=−2.65, p=0.01) ([Sulla et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.825047/full)). The practical meaning is important: emotional distress did not simply add a constant penalty to every student's outcome — it **disabled the personal strengths** (perseverance, self-belief) that normally drive achievement. Negative emotions thus acted as a moderator that flattened individual differences in effort, and they help explain why average effects in this literature are so often null while within-sample variation is large: the same online course was effective for emotionally stable students and ineffective for distressed ones, and 2020–2022 contained an unusual number of the latter.

### 3.5 Convenience and flexibility

**Mechanism of impact.** The countervailing positive factor was convenience and flexibility, which improved effectiveness by **reallocating scarce time and attention toward study**. Eliminating commutes returned hours each week; asynchronous recordings allowed self-pacing, repetition of difficult segments, and study at individually optimal times; and home environments reduced the friction of attendance. This mechanism shows up consistently in the perceptual studies: Indian students rated flexibility in time and space (61.3%) and ease of material sharing (73.4%) as the leading benefits of e-learning ([Khan et al., 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/13/1/57)); Korean students named comfortable environment (27.04%) and time utilization (26.02%) as the top advantages ([Shim & Lee, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7550861/)); Taiwanese dental students rated online learning significantly better than the classroom specifically on efficient time use and convenience ([Chang et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8339598/)); and the Spanish authors attributed their measured grade improvements to a confinement-induced shift toward continuous rather than last-minute study ([Gonzalez et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0239490)).

The flexibility dividend, however, was **conditional on self-regulation**, which is why it coexisted with complaints rather than cancelling them. Time that is saved can be reinvested in study or dissipated, and the online format raises the demand for exactly the self-discipline that determines which happens — the Chinese study found self-discipline a significant direct predictor of learning outcomes (β=0.126) ([Su & Guo, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8661517/)), and the Jordanian study found self-regulated learning a significant predictor of satisfaction ([Hamdan et al., 2021](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/IJEM-11-2020-0513/full/html)). Convenience therefore functioned less as an unconditional benefit than as a **resource transfer**: it removed structural constraints and handed students discretion, amplifying the outcomes of both strong and weak self-regulators. This is the deep reason the same students could simultaneously praise flexibility and report worse concentration — and why 80% of the American dental students wanted online elements retained post-pandemic even in a discipline built on manual skill ([Zheng et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z)).

### 3.6 How the factors interacted

The five factors did not operate independently, and the interactions explain most of the apparent contradictions in the effectiveness literature. Infrastructure set the feasibility frontier; instructional design determined how much of that frontier was exploited; social interaction and negative emotions jointly governed the motivational budget available for self-regulation; and convenience supplied the extra time that the other factors determined the use of. A useful way to read the review's most rigorous results is through these interactions: the RCT found no average mode effect precisely because its courses were well-designed, its students well-connected Hong Kong undergraduates with devices, and its interaction losses materialized only in small classes ([Cheung et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full)); the South African grade rise coexisted with a connectivity penalty because infrastructure varied within a single cohort ([Chisadza et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8250490/)); and the Italian pathway from effort to achievement existed only below the distress threshold ([Sulla et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.825047/full)).

For policy and practice, the implication is that "is online learning effective?" is permanently the wrong question, and the pandemic evidence replaces it with two better ones: **effective for whom**, and **under what supporting conditions**. The answers this review supports are concrete. Effectiveness requires, in rough order of necessity: universal broadband-quality access and devices (infrastructure); deliberately structured content with aligned, integrity-protected assessment (instructional design); engineered peer and instructor interaction, especially in small-group and skills-based courses (social interaction); monitoring and mitigation of student distress (negative emotions); and preservation of the flexibility dividend through recordings and asynchronous materials (convenience). Where these conditions held — as in the Hong Kong statistics courses, the Spanish adaptive-assessment courses, and the Taiwanese lecture content — online learning matched or beat the classroom. Where they failed — as for mobile-data-dependent South African students, Zoom-taught medical PBL groups, and distressed or isolated students everywhere — it did not. The pandemic's natural experiment thus ends with a conditional verdict: online learning in higher education was as effective as the infrastructure, design, interaction, and emotional support wrapped around it — and no more.
