# Gut Microbiota Interactions With Colorectal Cancer Treatment: Evidence Through September 30, 2024

## Scope and research method

This review covers publicly available findings available **on or before September 30, 2024** concerning interactions between the gut or tumor-associated microbiota and colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment. I prioritized CRC-specific human prospective cohorts, randomized trials, translational studies linking human observations to animal or ex-vivo experiments, and mechanistic animal studies; systematic reviews were used chiefly to assess consistency and limitations across the primary literature. The requested treatment domains were surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy, and microbiota-directed future approaches. 

**Critical exclusion applied throughout:** I did **not** open, view, read, cite, quote, or use *“The Interplay Between the Gut Microbiota and Colorectal Cancer: A Review of the Literature”* by Marco Cintoni et al., and I did not use any of the four specified MDPI, PubMed, PMC, or ResearchGate URLs. No conclusion below is derived from that article.

Evidence is labeled **Human**, **Human + mechanistic**, or **Preclinical** so that associations observed in patients are not presented as if they establish causality. A recurrent limitation across the field is that fecal microbiota, mucosa-associated microbiota, and intratumoral microbiota are biologically different compartments, and many studies use small cohorts, 16S sequencing rather than shotgun metagenomics, heterogeneous chemotherapy or radiotherapy regimens, and different definitions of response or toxicity. Reviews available by the cutoff similarly emphasized heterogeneity and the need for prospective validation. [[1]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39047017/)

## Evidence table

| Treatment Area | Specific Modality | Positive Impacts/Advantages | Negative Impacts/Complications |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Surgery** | **Colorectal resection and anastomotic healing** | **Human + mechanistic:** Preoperative microbiome composition appears to influence wound healing. In a prospective CRC cohort, greater preoperative dysbiosis correlated with higher postoperative diarrhea, overall complications, and anastomotic leakage; leak rose from 1.5% in the least dysbiotic category to 11.1% in the most dysbiotic category. [[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34823504/) A translational CRC study found that patients who developed anastomotic leak had a distinct preoperative microbiota and higher mucosal inflammatory mediators; transfer of feces from leak patients to mice reproduced impaired healing and increased MIP-1α, MIP-2, MCP-1, and IL-17A/F, supporting more than a purely correlative relationship. [[3]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36585238/) **Mechanistic preclinical evidence:** *Enterococcus faecalis* can degrade collagen and activate host MMP9 at anastomotic sites; blocking its collagenolytic phenotype prevented leak in experimental models without necessarily eradicating the organism. [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25947163/) | Surgery itself, bowel preparation, altered diet, antibiotics, hospitalization, stomas, and postoperative complications can cause major ecological disruption. In a prospective study of 62 CRC patients, postoperative complications were associated with long-lasting differences in microbiota detectable for up to 24 months, although confounding by antibiotics and heterogeneous complications could not be excluded. [[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33809741/) Human surgical isolates also support enrichment of collagenolytic organisms such as *E. faecalis* in leak-associated settings, but a single organism is not sufficient to explain all leaks. [[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34255574/) |
| **Surgery** | **Perioperative microbial ecology / pathogen suppression** | **Human:** Microbiome-based risk stratification may eventually identify patients prone to poor anastomotic healing. Observational and translational data support potentially protective communities containing butyrate-producing organisms and potentially harmful collagenase-producing pathobionts, making the perioperative microbiome a plausible intervention target. [[7]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37743721/) Clinical studies of perioperative microbiota manipulation have reported fewer infectious complications and faster bowel recovery in some settings, but approaches, antibiotics, probiotic formulations, and surgical protocols differ substantially. [[8]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35739383/) | Aggressive antimicrobial manipulation can also remove beneficial commensals and select resistant organisms. The mechanistic literature shows that standard perioperative intravenous antibiotics do not necessarily eradicate collagenolytic *E. faecalis* at anastomotic tissue. [[9]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25947163/) Therefore, the goal is unlikely to be nonspecific “sterilization” of the bowel; selective control of virulence functions or restoration of ecological resilience may be more rational, but this remains investigational. [[10]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29658991/) |
| **Chemotherapy** | **5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) / fluoropyrimidines** | **Human:** A randomized CRC trial of 150 patients receiving 5-FU-based adjuvant therapy found that *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG supplementation reduced severe diarrhea and abdominal discomfort, suggesting that modifying the microbiota can improve treatment tolerance even when the antitumor drug is unchanged. [[11]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17895895/) **Mechanistic:** 5-FU itself has direct antibacterial activity against CRC-associated *Fusobacterium nucleatum*. In vitro and translational work also showed that other intratumoral bacteria can chemically reduce/inactivate 5-FU, thereby protecting both *F. nucleatum* and cancer cells; this provides a plausible mechanism by which microbial composition could alter local drug exposure. [[12]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10790632/) | **Human:** Fluoropyrimidines can damage the intestinal epithelial barrier and are associated with microbiome shifts accompanying diarrhea. In a prospective CRC cohort, patients who developed diarrhea after fluoropyrimidine-containing chemotherapy showed reduced α-diversity, decreased Firmicutes and *Bifidobacterium*, and increased Bacteroidetes after treatment; the cohort was small, so these findings are associative rather than causal. [[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37231829/) **Human + mechanistic:** *F. nucleatum* was enriched in tumors from patients who experienced recurrence after chemotherapy and promoted resistance to 5-FU in CRC models through TLR4–MYD88 signaling, loss of miR-18a*/miR-4802, and activation of autophagy. [[14]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5767127/) |
| **Chemotherapy** | **Oxaliplatin / FOLFOX (5-FU + leucovorin + oxaliplatin)** | **Human:** Metagenomic/metabolomic analysis of rectal cancer patients receiving FOLFOX found that pretreatment microbial profiles differed between responders and nonresponders; a subset of eight bacterial species separated groups, and responders showed larger treatment-associated microbiome shifts. This suggests possible predictive biomarkers, although the cohort was only 37 patients and requires external validation. [[15]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32153743/) **Preclinical:** Commensal microbes can contribute to platinum-drug antitumor activity by supporting myeloid-cell reactive oxygen species responses; this is a broader tumor-model finding rather than definitive CRC clinical evidence and should be treated as mechanistic support, not proof of benefit in patients. | Oxaliplatin-containing regimens contribute to mucositis, diarrhea, appetite changes, and broader ecological disruption, making it difficult to separate the effect of oxaliplatin from 5-FU, antibiotics, diet, or cancer itself in clinical cohorts. Animal studies comparing cytotoxics found regimen-specific alterations, with gut effects differing among 5-FU, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan. [[16]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/5532424/) *F. nucleatum*-associated autophagy-mediated chemoresistance applies to oxaliplatin as well as 5-FU in experimental CRC systems. [[17]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5767127/) |
| **Chemotherapy** | **Capecitabine** | **Human:** A prospective multicenter cohort of 33 metastatic CRC patients found that three cycles of capecitabine did **not** significantly change overall microbial α-diversity, β-diversity, or major taxonomic abundance and did not identify a baseline microbiome associated with response; this is useful negative evidence against assuming a universal capecitabine microbiome signature. [[18]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34801414/) A related human study found that fecal valerate and caproate declined during treatment and that baseline iso-butyrate correlated with tumor response, generating hypotheses about microbial metabolites rather than establishing a therapeutic mechanism. [[19]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37027066/) | The absence of a strong community-level signal in the 33-patient study does not rule out strain-level functions, microbial drug metabolism, or effects in selected patients. Antibiotic exposure produced individual microbiome perturbations, illustrating an important confounder. [[18]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34801414/) As of the September 30, 2024 cutoff, robust prospective evidence linking a specific capecitabine-associated microbiome to improved survival or treatment response was lacking. [[20]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34801414/) |
| **Chemotherapy** | **Irinotecan / FOLFIRI** | **Human + biochemical:** The microbiome offers a highly plausible toxicity biomarker and intervention target. Human fecal microbiomes vary substantially in their ability to reactivate irinotecan metabolites, creating “high-turnover” and “low-turnover” metabotypes that could potentially identify patients at greater risk for toxicity. [[21]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29104759/) Selective microbial β-glucuronidase inhibition is therefore attractive because it could reduce intestinal SN-38 exposure without blocking systemic antitumor activation of irinotecan. | **Mechanism:** Hepatic SN-38 is glucuronidated to SN-38G and excreted into the intestine; bacterial β-glucuronidases can reconvert SN-38G to active SN-38 in the lumen, increasing mucosal injury and delayed diarrhea. Human microbiome functional variability supports this mechanism. [[21]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29104759/) **Clinical intervention uncertainty:** A small randomized pilot suggested probiotic benefit, but a subsequent multicenter phase III study in 242 CRC patients found no significant reduction in grade 3/4 diarrhea with *Bifidobacterium BB-12* plus *L. rhamnosus* GG (11.8% placebo vs 7.9% probiotic; p=0.38), showing that mechanistic plausibility does not guarantee clinical benefit from generic probiotics. [[22]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26051570/) |
| **Immunotherapy** | **PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint blockade in MSI-H/dMMR CRC** | **Human:** The strongest CRC-specific clinical evidence available by the cutoff came from MSI-H/dMMR gastrointestinal cohorts dominated by CRC. In a multi-omics study of 77 patients, including 59 CRC cases, baseline microbial and metabolite profiles were associated with primary resistance; results were tested in an independent cohort of 39 and experimentally in mice. This supports microbiota profiling as a candidate response biomarker. [[23]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38194971/) A 2024 systematic review found recurrent associations between ICI response and taxa including Ruminococcaceae, *Faecalibacterium*, *Akkermansia*, Bifidobacteria, Lachnospiraceae, and Prevotellaceae, but only a handful of CRC studies met inclusion criteria. [[24]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39047017/) | Microbiome-response signatures are not yet sufficiently reproducible to direct routine CRC immunotherapy. The taxa associated with benefit differ between cohorts, and antibiotic use, geography, diet, sequencing methods, MSI status, prior therapy, and sampling time can alter findings. [[25]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38194971/) The data therefore support a biomarker-development field, not a clinically validated “good microbiome” prescription. |
| **Immunotherapy** | ***Fusobacterium nucleatum* and checkpoint blockade** | **Human + preclinical, context-dependent:** Unexpectedly, some studies suggest *F. nucleatum* can **sensitize** CRC to checkpoint blockade. A 2021 study linked high *F. nucleatum* with better PD-1-blockade response and showed in CRC mice that the bacterium activated STING, increased PD-L1 expression and IFN-γ⁺ CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, enhancing PD-L1 blockade. [[26]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34795206/) A Cancer Cell study made publicly available online on **September 19, 2024**, thus within the requested cutoff, found that intratumoral *F. nucleatum* could enhance anti-PD-1 efficacy in MSS CRC through bacterially derived butyrate, HDAC3/8 inhibition, increased TBX21 activity and reduced CD8-cell exhaustion. [[27]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39303724/) | **Contradictory human + mechanistic evidence:** A 2023 CRC study found the opposite relationship: nonresponders to immunotherapy had more *F. nucleatum* and succinate; *F. nucleatum*-derived succinate suppressed cGAS–IFN-β signaling, impaired CD8⁺ T-cell trafficking, and caused anti-PD-1 resistance in mice. Metronidazole lowered *F. nucleatum*/succinate and restored sensitivity experimentally. [[28]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37130518/) The conflict is biologically important rather than a trivial discrepancy: strain-level metabolic capacity, tumor versus luminal localization, MSS/MSI context, metabolite production, and immune state may determine whether *F. nucleatum* is detrimental or beneficial. The evidence does **not** justify deliberate *F. nucleatum* administration in patients. [[29]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37130518/) |
| **Radiotherapy** | **Pelvic radiotherapy / rectal-cancer chemoradiotherapy: toxicity** | **Human:** Pelvic radiation repeatedly alters gut community composition, and lower diversity or particular dysbiotic profiles have been associated with diarrhea, fatigue, and radiation enteropathy. A rectal-cancer proof-of-concept study of 31 patients found differences in *Bacteroides* and *Blautia* between those who developed clusters of symptoms and those who did not; baseline microbiome features predicted symptom clustering with about 74% accuracy, but the sample was small. [[30]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32700552/) Reviews of pelvic-radiotherapy cohorts found a recurring relationship between dysbiosis and gastrointestinal toxicity, while also emphasizing substantial inter-study heterogeneity. [[31]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34938654/) | Radiation injures epithelium, changes oxygen and nutrient conditions, increases permeability, and can trigger inflammatory microbial translocation; these ecological changes may amplify mucositis, diarrhea, and chronic enteropathy. [[32]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8685326/) Because many rectal-cancer patients receive concurrent fluoropyrimidines, the observed microbiome changes cannot always be attributed to radiation alone. [[33]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34068216/) |
| **Radiotherapy** | **Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT): tumor response** | **Human:** In 45 rectal-cancer patients, pretreatment fecal β-diversity differed between complete responders and nonresponders; Bacteroidales taxa were more abundant in nonresponders, while *Duodenibacillus massiliensis* was linked to complete response. [[34]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32315676/) A prospective longitudinal study of 39 locally advanced rectal-cancer patients likewise found microbiome profiles and enterotypes that distinguished good from poor nCRT response. [[35]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36967379/) Tumor-tissue sequencing in another cohort found *Ruminococcaceae* bacterium and *Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron* enriched in complete responders. [[36]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37161675/) | **Human + mechanistic:** One of the strongest causal studies implicated *Bacteroides vulgatus*. In a longitudinal nCRT study with 353 fecal samples, *B. vulgatus*-linked nucleotide biosynthesis was associated with resistance; nonresponsive tumors upregulated DNA-repair and nucleoside-transport pathways. Supplementing nucleosides or administering *B. vulgatus* protected cancer cells from 5-FU or irradiation in experimental systems, providing a mechanism by which microbial metabolites could blunt combined chemoradiotherapy. [[37]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36563680/) nCRT itself reduced microbial diversity, so response-associated taxa may be both causes and consequences of treatment. [[37]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36563680/) |
| **Future Outlook** | **Probiotics, prebiotics and synbiotics** | **Human:** This is the most clinically developed microbiota-directed strategy in CRC, especially for supportive care. A randomized postoperative CRC trial found six-strain *Lactobacillus/Bifidobacterium* supplementation was well tolerated and reduced several circulating inflammatory cytokines, though it did not establish an anticancer effect. [[38]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31340751/) The POSTCARE randomized trial found microbiota shifts, lower zonulin, and a trend toward improved anterior-resection symptoms after perioperative probiotics. [[39]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32664289/) A 2024 meta-analysis of ten RCTs comprising 1,276 CRC surgical patients found lower rates of diarrhea, surgical-site, urinary, and pulmonary infections with peri/postoperative probiotics, but no clear reduction in hospital stay. [[40]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38231290/) In chemotherapy, the 150-patient 5-FU trial found less severe diarrhea with *L. rhamnosus* GG. [[11]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17895895/) | Effects are **strain-, dose-, regimen-, and endpoint-specific**; “probiotic” is not one intervention. The large irinotecan phase III trial was negative for its primary severe-diarrhea endpoint despite earlier pilot benefit. [[41]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10438450/) A Cochrane review and later systematic reviews found inconsistent certainty across chemotherapy/radiotherapy studies, reflecting small trials and heterogeneity. [[42]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30168576/) As of September 2024, prebiotic evidence in CRC treatment was considerably less mature than probiotic evidence; some trials were still protocols rather than completed efficacy studies. [[43]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37046334/) No probiotic/prebiotic regimen had established an improvement in CRC recurrence, progression-free survival, or overall survival sufficient for routine oncologic use. [[44]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38807764/) |
| **Future Outlook** | **Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)** | **Preclinical:** In CRC-bearing mice, FMT combined with anti-PD-1 produced better tumor control and survival than either alone; multi-omics implicated shifts in *Bacteroides* species and immunomodulatory metabolites. [[45]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35911731/) **Human, early clinical:** RENMIN-215 was a 20-patient, open-label, single-arm phase II study in refractory MSS metastatic CRC combining FMT, tislelizumab, and fruquintinib. Median PFS was 9.6 months, median OS 13.7 months, ORR 20%, and DCR 95%; responders developed distinct post-treatment microbiomes with higher diversity. [[46]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38024475/) These results provided an important proof of concept that intensive microbiota manipulation can be incorporated into CRC immunotherapy trials. | The RENMIN-215 design **cannot isolate the effect of FMT** from tislelizumab, fruquintinib, or their interaction; the cohort was small, uncontrolled, and highly selected. Ninety-five percent of patients had at least one treatment-related adverse event and 30% had grade 3–4 events, although these cannot be attributed specifically to FMT. [[46]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38024475/) FMT also carries theoretical and documented risks of transmitting pathogens or antimicrobial-resistance determinants, making rigorous donor screening essential. In radiation injury, small non-CRC pelvic-radiation studies and case reports suggested improvement in chronic radiation enteritis after FMT, but these are supportive extrapolations rather than CRC-specific proof. [[47]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32044171/) As of the cutoff, FMT remained investigational for CRC and should be considered a clinical-trial strategy rather than routine care. [[48]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38035338/) |

## Mechanistic synthesis

Across treatment classes, the microbiota can affect CRC therapy through at least four recurring mechanisms. First, microbes can **chemically transform drugs or metabolites**: bacterial β-glucuronidases reactivate irinotecan-derived SN-38 in the intestinal lumen, while intratumoral microbial metabolism can reduce 5-FU exposure. [[49]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29104759/) Second, microbiota-derived metabolites can change tumor or immune-cell behavior. The best CRC examples include *B. vulgatus*-linked nucleotide production that protects against 5-FU/radiation, *F. nucleatum*-derived succinate that suppresses cGAS–IFN-β signaling, and—under a different experimental/clinical context—*F. nucleatum*-derived butyrate that reduces CD8-cell exhaustion through HDAC3/8–TBX21 signaling. [[50]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36563680/)

Third, microorganisms influence **epithelial barrier integrity and tissue repair**. This is most compelling in surgery, where collagenolytic *E. faecalis* can directly degrade healing matrix and activate host MMP9, while dysbiotic patient microbiota can transfer a poor-healing inflammatory phenotype to experimental animals. [[51]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25947163/) The same barrier principle applies to chemotherapy and radiation: epithelial injury alters nutrient availability and microbial niches, and dysbiosis can then intensify mucosal inflammation and permeability. [[52]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37231829/)

Fourth, the microbiota can alter **antitumor immunity**. Human MSI-H/dMMR data link microbial and metabolic profiles with checkpoint-blockade resistance, while mouse transfer experiments show that at least some response phenotypes can be transmitted by fecal communities. [[53]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38194971/) The conflicting *F. nucleatum* literature is especially instructive: the same nominal species can be associated with chemotherapy resistance, immune suppression, or enhanced checkpoint sensitivity depending on strain, metabolite production, anatomical compartment and tumor context. [[54]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5767127/)

## Human versus preclinical evidence

The **most clinically secure microbiome-related conclusions by September 2024** concern treatment toxicity rather than improved tumor control. Human randomized data support the possibility that selected probiotics reduce diarrhea or infectious complications after CRC surgery or fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy, although not every formulation works and a large irinotecan trial was negative. [[55]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38231290/) Human observational evidence also consistently links perioperative dysbiosis with complications, microbiome composition with chemoradiotherapy response, and specific microbial/metabolic signatures with checkpoint-inhibitor outcomes. [[56]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34823504/)

Causal claims are strongest where human observations were paired with transplantation, bacterial supplementation, genetic/biochemical manipulation, or metabolite experiments. Examples include *E. faecalis* collagenase/MMP9 in anastomotic failure, *F. nucleatum*–autophagy in chemoresistance, *B. vulgatus* nucleotide production in nCRT resistance, and *F. nucleatum* metabolites in opposing checkpoint-response phenotypes. [[57]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25947163/) Even in these cases, successful manipulation in mice does not establish that altering the organism in a patient will improve survival.

FMT was the most ambitious translational strategy to reach a CRC-specific interventional trial by the cutoff. RENMIN-215 was encouraging but cannot establish FMT efficacy because all patients received FMT together with a PD-1 inhibitor and an antiangiogenic drug and there was no randomized control arm. [[46]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38024475/) Thus, FMT should be viewed as **proof of feasibility and a hypothesis-generating therapeutic platform**, not an established treatment for MSS CRC.

## Key uncertainties and translational priorities

The largest problem is **causality versus correlation**. Cancer stage, primary-tumor site, diet, bowel preparation, antibiotics, proton-pump inhibitors, prior chemotherapy, surgery, stomas, age and geography can all alter the microbiome while also correlating with treatment outcome. Small microbiome cohorts are therefore highly vulnerable to confounding. The capecitabine cohort that found no major response-associated community signature illustrates why negative studies are important and why positive taxa from small cohorts should not be overinterpreted. [[18]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34801414/)

A second problem is **taxonomic resolution**. Species names alone are often inadequate: different strains of the same organism can carry different metabolic pathways, virulence factors or immunologic properties. The opposing CRC checkpoint-blockade findings involving *F. nucleatum*—succinate-associated resistance versus STING- or butyrate-associated sensitization—strongly suggest that functional metagenomics, metabolomics and spatial sampling are more informative than simple abundance measurements. [[29]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37130518/)

A third issue is **compartment**. Stool is convenient, but stool organisms may not reflect mucosa-associated or intratumoral microbes. This distinction matters especially for 5-FU metabolism, checkpoint immunotherapy and anastomotic healing, where locally adherent or intratumoral microbes can exert effects that are poorly captured by fecal sequencing. [[58]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10790632/)

The most informative future trials would therefore randomize a clearly defined microbiota intervention, stratify by tumor molecular subtype and treatment regimen, control antibiotic and diet exposure, collect longitudinal stool **and** mucosal/tumor samples where feasible, and pair shotgun metagenomics with metabolomics and immune profiling. Clinical endpoints should include not only diarrhea or microbiome composition but also dose intensity, pathological response, recurrence, progression-free survival and overall survival. The studies available by September 2024 establish biological plausibility and several promising biomarkers, but not enough evidence to recommend routine microbiome testing, FMT, or a particular probiotic regimen as an anticancer standard of care. [[59]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39047017/)

## Overall interpretation

By September 30, 2024, the gut microbiota had emerged as a credible **modifier of CRC treatment toxicity, response and tissue recovery**, but the strength of evidence differed substantially by treatment. Surgery has unusually strong mechanistic evidence linking collagenolytic organisms and inflammatory microbial communities to anastomotic failure; chemotherapy has the clearest examples of direct microbial drug metabolism and metabolite-mediated toxicity or resistance; radiotherapy studies consistently associate microbial composition with gastrointestinal toxicity and nCRT response; and immunotherapy offers the most compelling opportunity for microbiome-based precision treatment but also the most striking evidence of biological context-dependence. [[60]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25947163/)

The clinically most mature microbiota-directed approach was supportive use of selected probiotics/synbiotics, for which randomized CRC trials show reductions in some postoperative and chemotherapy-related complications but inconsistent effects across formulations. [[55]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38231290/) FMT was considerably earlier in development: preclinical studies support enhanced checkpoint activity, and the small RENMIN-215 trial showed that FMT can feasibly be combined with immunotherapy and antiangiogenic treatment in refractory MSS metastatic CRC, but it did not establish the independent efficacy of FMT. [[61]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35911731/)

Accordingly, the microbiome was best regarded at the September 2024 cutoff as a **promising biomarker, toxicity modifier and experimental therapeutic target**, rather than a validated stand-alone component of routine CRC treatment.
