# Gut Microbiota and Colorectal Cancer Treatment: A Research Synthesis (Through September 2024)

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Standard treatment combines surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and—in a minority of patients—immunotherapy. Over the past decade, accumulating evidence has shown that the gut microbiota modulates nearly every stage of this treatment continuum, shaping both efficacy and toxicity. This synthesis compiles the principal findings through September 2024 and organizes them in the requested table, followed by brief context and a future-outlook section on probiotics/prebiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT).

## Interactions Between Gut Microbiota and CRC Treatment Modalities

The table below maps the interactions between specific bacterial taxa or microbiota-level features and the four mainstream CRC treatment modalities, plus the two future-outlook modalities the user specified. Positive impacts/advantages list situations in which a microbial feature improves therapeutic outcomes or reduces side effects; negative impacts/complications list situations in which the same or another feature undermines efficacy, increases toxicity, or raises clinical risk.

| Treatment Area | Specific Modality | Positive Impacts / Advantages | Negative Impacts / Complications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surgery | Pre-operative gut microbiota composition & peri-operative interventions | Higher abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria (e.g., *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, *Roseburia*) and mucin-protective *Akkermansia muciniphila* is associated with reduced post-operative infectious complications, less ileus, and a shorter hospital stay [1][2]. Peri-operative probiotic/synbiotic regimens (e.g., *Lactobacillus* + *Bifidobacterium* + inulin) reduced major post-operative complications from 48.8% to 28.6% in a 168-patient randomized trial [3]. Butyrate-producing bacteria and dietary butyrate supplementation improved anastomotic healing in murine models and reduced tumor implantation at the anastomotic site [4]. | Low microbial diversity with reduced *Lachnospiraceae* and *Bacteroidaceae* at the anastomotic staple line is associated with anastomotic leak (AL) [5]. Collagenolytic *Enterococcus faecalis* and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* activate host MMP-9 and directly degrade collagen, driving AL pathogenesis [6]. A "pathobiome" state—often triggered by broad-spectrum antibiotics, Western diet, or surgical stress—promotes local tumor cell implantation and is linked to higher local recurrence and shorter disease-free survival after AL [5]. Higher tumor-tissue *Fusobacterium nucleatum* and *Bacteroides fragilis* (ETBF) abundance are associated with increased cancer-specific mortality and decreased 3-year overall/disease-free survival after curative resection [7]. |
| Chemotherapy | 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) / capecitabine (with or without oxaliplatin, irinotecan) | Certain commensals enhance 5-FU efficacy: *Bifidobacterium bifidum* combined with 5-FU potentiates anti-tumor effects in preclinical models; butyrate-producing bacteria and butyrate itself enhance 5-FU activity through the GPR109a–AKT pathway and suppression of the Warburg effect [8][9]. *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG (LGG) supplementation reduced grade 3/4 diarrhea from 37% to 22% (P=0.027) and decreased chemotherapy dose reductions in 150 CRC patients receiving adjuvant 5-FU (Österlund 2007) [10]. A multi-strain probiotic (Colon Dophilus) reduced severe irinotecan-induced diarrhea from 17.4% to 0% in a pilot trial (Mego 2015) [11]. Oral *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* mixtures reduced irinotecan-induced enterocolitis and lowered antidiarrheal use [11]. | Bacterial β-glucuronidases (produced by *E. coli*, *Bacteroides vulgatus*, *Clostridium ramosum*, and others) deconjugate inactive SN-38G back to active SN-38 in the gut lumen, causing delayed, dose-limiting diarrhea in 60–90% of irinotecan-treated patients [12][13]. *F. nucleatum* induces 5-FU/oxaliplatin resistance through the TLR4/MYD88 pathway, which selectively downregulates miR-18a\* and miR-4802 and upregulates autophagy via ULK1 and ATG7 (Yu et al., *Cell* 2017) [14]. Additional mechanisms: F. nucleatum also activates the Hippo/BCL2 axis to inhibit GSDME-mediated pyroptosis, induces BIRC3-mediated apoptosis blockade, and triggers E-cadherin/β-catenin/GPX4 to suppress oxaliplatin-induced ferroptosis [15]. *B. vulgatus*–mediated nucleotide biosynthesis supplies cancer cells with nucleosides, promotes DNA repair, and confers resistance to 5-FU and irradiation in locally advanced rectal cancer (Teng et al., *Cancer Cell* 2022) [16]. Colibactin-producing *E. coli* drives lipid-metabolic reprogramming linked to multi-drug resistance; *Citrobacter freundii* downregulates NINJ2 and increases oxaliplatin resistance [8]. Chemotherapy itself depletes *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* and expands *Staphylococcus*, *Clostridium*, and *E. coli*, worsening mucositis and infection risk [8]. |
| Immunotherapy | Immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti–PD-1/PD-L1, anti–CTLA-4) | Higher baseline fecal abundance of *Akkermansia muciniphila*, *Bifidobacterium* (longum/breve/infantis/adolescentis), *Faecalibacterium*, *Ruminococcaceae*, *Lachnospiraceae*, and *Prevotellaceae* is consistently associated with improved objective response and survival in ICB-treated cancer patients (Routy et al., *Science* 2018; Gopalakrishnan et al., *Science* 2018) [17][18]. Mechanistically, *A. muciniphila* restores anti–PD-1 efficacy via IL-12–dependent recruitment of CCR9⁺CXCR3⁺CD4⁺ T cells; *Bifidobacterium* species enhance CD8⁺ T-cell priming and IFN-γ production through dendritic-cell activation [17][19]. In the MSS setting, *F. nucleatum* can paradoxically sensitize tumors to PD-L1 blockade by activating STING signaling, inducing PD-L1 expression, and increasing IFN-γ⁺CD8⁺ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (Gao et al., 2021) [20]. Enterotoxigenic *B. fragilis* (ETBF) is required for anti–CTLA-4 efficacy via Th1 immune responses and dendritic-cell maturation (Vétizou et al., *Science* 2015). Butyrate-producing taxa correlate with cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and improved anti-tumor immunity [21]. | Most CRCs are microsatellite-stable (MSS, ~85%), and ICB monotherapy produces objective responses in <5% of these patients [22]. Antibiotic exposure during ICB therapy is associated with shorter progression-free and overall survival, implicating commensal depletion as a clinical liability [17]. In MSI-H tumors, *F. nucleatum* is negatively associated with TIL density and may promote immune evasion via Fap2–TIGIT binding, NK-cell inhibition, and myeloid-derived suppressor-cell expansion [23]. High circulating butyrate and propionate can blunt anti–CTLA-4 efficacy by restraining dendritic-cell maturation and T-cell accumulation in the tumor microenvironment [9]. In the RENMIN-215 trial, MSS-mCRC responders to FMT + tislelizumab + fruquintinib had *low* baseline *Bifidobacterium* and *Actinobacteriota* and *high* *Proteobacteria* and *Lachnospiraceae*—illustrating that the predictive role of any single taxon is highly context-dependent [24]. |
| Radiotherapy | Neoadjuvant / adjuvant pelvic radiotherapy; total neoadjuvant therapy | Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)—especially butyrate from *Lachnospiraceae*, *Ruminococcaceae*, and *Faecalibacterium*—reinforce epithelial tight junctions, reduce radiation-induced apoptosis, and support crypt-stem-cell regeneration [25]. VSL#3 (a multi-strain probiotic containing four *Lactobacillus*, three *Bifidobacterium*, and *Streptococcus salivarius* subsp. *thermophilus*) significantly reduced radiation-induced diarrhea, WHO grade 3/4 diarrhea, daily bowel movements, and loperamide use in 490 patients undergoing postoperative pelvic RT for sigmoid, rectal, or cervical cancers (Delia et al., 2007) [26]. LGG-containing regimens also reduced diarrhea severity in CRC patients receiving 5-FU plus pelvic RT [10]. *Lactobacillus casei* + *Bifidobacterium bifidum* (Infloran) reduced grade 2/3 diarrhea from 45% to 9% in cervical-cancer patients undergoing chemoradiation [27]. A *Faecalibacterium*-rich, *Lachnospiraceae*-rich, *Blautia*/*Roseburia*-dominated baseline consortium is associated with pooled pCR rates of ~44% in locally advanced rectal cancer receiving nCRT, compared with ~21% in dysbiotic controls [28]. FMT was safe and improved symptoms in 3/5 patients with chronic radiation enteritis in a pilot study (Ding et al., 2020) [29]. | Radiotherapy reduces α-diversity, depletes *Firmicutes* (especially *Lachnospiraceae*, *Ruminococcaceae*, *Clostridium* XIVa) and *F. prausnitzii*, and expands *Enterobacteriaceae*, *Bacteroides*, and *Fusobacteria*; loss of *Bifidobacterium* and mucin-protective *A. muciniphila* aggravates mucositis [25][30]. Radiation enteritis affects up to ~80% of patients receiving abdominopelvic RT, and chronic enteritis with strictures, bleeding, and fibrosis develops months to years later in 10–20% [30]. Proteobacteria enrichment and *Bifidobacterium longum* depletion precede grade ≥2 diarrhea during total neoadjuvant therapy [28]. Persistent post-RT *F. nucleatum* positivity correlates with higher relapse risk [31]. The shift from F. nucleatum-positive to F. nucleatum-negative tumors after chemoradiation is itself a marker of improved relapse-free survival. |
| Future Outlook | Probiotics / Prebiotics | A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (1,013 patients) showed oral probiotics significantly reduced the risk and severity of chemotherapy-induced diarrhea and oral mucositis, with benefits most pronounced in Asian patients and those receiving higher bacterial diversity [32]. Another meta-analysis (8 RCTs, 633 patients) showed probiotics halved the risk of chemoradiotherapy-induced diarrhea (RR 0.51, 95% CI 0.38–0.68) [28]. Prebiotic partially hydrolyzed guar gum (PHGG) doubled fecal *Bifidobacterium* counts and significantly reduced diarrhea at day 45 of pelvic RT [33]. Synbiotic combinations (probiotic + prebiotic fiber such as inulin or FOS) are more effective than either alone at restoring *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* and at reducing GI toxicity. Peri-operative synbiotics (e.g., B. animalis subsp. lactis BB-12 + LGG + inulin) improved immune function in resected CRC patients and reduced post-operative septicemia [3][32]. Butyrate and butyrogenic consortia act as chemo- and radiosensitizers in preclinical models by promoting CD8⁺ T-cell cytotoxicity, inducing apoptosis via HDAC inhibition, and protecting normal tissue from radiation damage [9]. Probiotic VSL#3 reduced irinotecan-induced intestinal toxicity in animal models and is being evaluated in adjuvant settings. | Most probiotic studies in CRC are small, single-center, and heterogeneous in strain, dose, and timing. Meta-analyses often show benefit only in subgroup analyses, and large confirmatory trials are still pending. Probiotics do not yet have proven primary anti-tumor efficacy in CRC; the strongest evidence is for toxicity mitigation rather than direct tumor control. Some commercial probiotic strains (e.g., *E. coli* Nissle 1917) harbor the *pks* island responsible for colibactin-induced DNA damage and are therefore not universally safe [1]. In immunocompromised patients on chemotherapy, even conventional *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* can cause opportunistic bacteremia; probiotic-related sepsis has been reported. Antibiotic-induced depletion of commensals can blunt any prebiotic effect, and high inter-individual microbiome variability limits reproducibility. |
| Future Outlook | Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) | The RENMIN-215 phase II trial (20 patients, MSS metastatic CRC refractory to ≥second-line therapy) combined FMT with tislelizumab (anti–PD-1) and fruquintinib (VEGFR inhibitor): median PFS 9.6 months, median OS 13.7 months, ORR 20%, disease control rate 95%, with manageable grade 3/4 adverse events in 30% [24]. Responders showed increased microbial diversity and sustained microbiota engraftment [34]. A published case report documented pathological complete response in a pMMR/MSS mCRC patient receiving FMT capsules plus tislelizumab and bevacizumab after failing FOLFOXIRI [35]. A pilot study in chronic radiation enteritis (5 patients) showed FMT relieved diarrhea, rectal bleeding, abdominal/rectal pain, and fecal incontinence in 3/5 patients without infectious complications, with α-diversity increases and *Blautia* enrichment [29]. Earlier-phase trials in melanoma demonstrated that FMT from anti–PD-1 responders can re-sensitize refractory patients, providing proof-of-concept now being translated to CRC [34]. FMT in CRC may also serve as primary prevention (e.g., NCT06205862 evaluating FMT to reduce adenoma recurrence after endoscopic resection) [36]. | FMT is not yet an approved standard-of-care for CRC; most positive signals come from single-arm phase II trials, case reports, or small pilot studies. Long-term safety, particularly the risk of transmitting pathogens, antibiotic-resistance genes, or triggering autoimmunity, remains a concern, and donor screening is the critical bottleneck. The RENMIN-215 trial lacked a randomized control arm; propensity-score-matched comparison with real-world cohorts helps, but residual confounding cannot be excluded. Engraftment is highly variable and depends on recipient microbiome, antibiotic conditioning, and donor–recipient compatibility, making "one stool fits all" strategies unreliable. Some FMT responders paradoxically have *low* *Bifidobacterium* and *high* *Proteobacteria* baseline profiles, complicating donor selection [24]. In the MSS subgroup where FMT most matters, predictive biomarkers of response are still undefined, and large randomized trials (e.g., NCT07509398 for unresectable CRC, NCT04729322 for anti–PD-1-refractory dMMR) are still ongoing. |

## How To Read the Table

Three cross-cutting patterns emerge from the table.

*First, the gut microbiota acts as a double-edged modulator of nearly every CRC treatment.* Specific bacterial taxa can either amplify or undermine the same therapy depending on context. *F. nucleatum* is the clearest example: it drives chemoresistance and 5-FU/oxaliplatin failure through autophagy, but in the right immunological context it can also sensitize MSS tumors to PD-L1 blockade by activating STING. Similarly, *Bifidobacterium* correlates with ICI response in melanoma and lung cancer, but high baseline *Bifidobacterium* predicted non-response in the MSS-mCRC RENMIN-215 trial. This context-dependence is the central reason no single microbial biomarker has yet entered routine clinical practice.

*Second, dysbiosis is both a cause and a consequence of CRC treatment.* Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery all reduce α-diversity and deplete butyrate producers; the resulting loss of SCFA-mediated barrier and immune homeostasis then worsens the very toxicities the treatment is trying to address. This bidirectional loop explains why interventions that break the cycle—probiotic/synbiotic supplementation, FMT, dietary fiber, or targeted elimination of pathobionts—have shown efficacy across multiple treatment modalities.

*Third, microbial modulation offers complementary, not competitive, routes to improving CRC outcomes.* The strongest evidence through September 2024 is for adjuvant applications: reducing diarrhea and mucositis during chemotherapy, attenuating radiation enteritis, lowering post-operative infectious complications, and potentially re-sensitizing immunotherapy-refractory MSS disease. Probiotics, prebiotics, and FMT are positioned as adjuncts to existing standards rather than replacements.

## Future Outlook In Detail

The "Future Outlook" rows of the table already cover the principal mechanisms and clinical signals. Two structural points warrant additional emphasis.

**Probiotics and prebiotics are closest to clinical translation but face a standardization problem.** Multiple RCTs in CRC (Österlund 2007, Mego 2015, Delia 2007, Chitapanarux, Kotzampassi 2015) demonstrate that specific strains—LGG, VSL#3, Colon Dophilus, Infloran—can reduce treatment-related GI toxicity. Yet strain-level effects differ substantially, the strongest benefits are in Asian cohorts (where baseline microbiome composition differs from Western cohorts), and most trials are not powered for survival endpoints. Prebiotics (PHGG, inulin, FOS) reliably increase *Bifidobacterium* and reduce diarrhea, but the magnitude of clinical benefit is moderate. The next-generation candidate is engineered live biotherapeutic products (LBPs) such as *Akkermansia muciniphila* MucT or defined SCFA-producing consortia; early-phase trials in MSS mCRC are underway.

**FMT is the most ambitious approach and the most uncertain.** The RENMIN-215 results are striking because MSS metastatic CRC has historically been considered immune-refractory, yet the FMT + anti–PD-1 + VEGFR-inhibitor combination produced a 95% disease control rate. The mechanistic hypothesis is that donor microbiota restore a permissive tumor immune microenvironment, allowing the checkpoint inhibitor to overcome MSS-associated immune resistance. The same biology underlies the case report of pCR in a pMMR/MSS patient receiving FMT + tislelizumab + bevacizumab. However, before FMT can be considered standard, randomized phase III trials must confirm efficacy, identify the minimal effective microbial signature in donor stool, define the optimal conditioning regimen, and demonstrate long-term safety. Several such trials (NCT07509398, NCT04729322, NCT06205862) are now enrolling.

A separate but increasingly important question is *targeted* microbiota engineering rather than whole-stool transplantation. Liposome-encapsulated antibiotics (e.g., LipoAgTNZ) can selectively eradicate intratumoral *F. nucleatum* in murine models and convert the tumor microenvironment from "cold" to immunologically active without causing broad dysbiosis. Bacteriophage therapy (e.g., VA7 against *B. fragilis*) and CRISPR-based microbiome editing are similarly under exploration. These precision tools may eventually replace or supplement FMT.
