# Orchestrating the Gut Microbiome: A Synthesis of Microbial Interactions with Colorectal Cancer Therapeutics

## Surgery and the Gut Microbiota: Influence on Anastomotic Integrity and Postoperative Recovery

Colorectal cancer (CRC) surgery represents a significant physiological stressor that profoundly alters the gut ecosystem, creating a complex interplay between the intestinal microbiota and surgical outcomes [[149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12695332/), [150](https://www.surgical.theclinics.com/article/S0039-6109(25)00054-4/fulltext)]. The gut microbiota, which plays a crucial role in surgical colonic healing, can act as both a friend and a foe following resection [[30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36585238/), [57](https://www.frontiersin.org/research-topics/53609/the-microbiome-in-surgery---friend-or-foe/magazine)]. The immediate perioperative period is characterized by rapid changes in microbial communities due to factors including the surgical procedure itself and the administration of prophylactic antimicrobials [[34](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13099-021-00477-7), [111](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8729125/)]. These shifts have been directly linked to critical postoperative complications, most notably anastomotic leak (AL), a devastating event that can necessitate re-operation and prolonged recovery [[132](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1089/sur.2023.003)]. Conversely, evidence suggests that strategic modulation of the microbiome may improve outcomes, such as reducing infectious complications and supporting mucosal integrity [[40](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/5/1451), [57](https://www.frontiersin.org/research-topics/53609/the-microbiome-in-surgery---friend-or-foe/magazine)]. The composition of the gut microbiome before and after surgery is increasingly recognized as a key factor influencing patient recovery and long-term prognosis [[35](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/12/9/e057391), [178](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8002283/)].

The relationship between the gut microbiota and anastomotic healing is multifaceted and central to postoperative safety. A balanced microbial community appears to play a vital role in modulating mucosal proinflammatory cytokines, which are essential for orchestrating the wound-healing process [[84](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11399839/), [179](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2023.1110787/full)]. This suggests that dysbiosis—a state of microbial imbalance—could disrupt this delicate process and compromise the integrity of the surgical anastomosis [[152](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2025.1701650/full)]. Research has shown that the depletion of the normal microbiome can create an opportunity for low-abundance pathogenic strains, such as certain species of *Enterococcus*, to proliferate and cause anastomotic leak [[32](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1743919122005283)]. This highlights the importance of maintaining a healthy, diverse microbial population to prevent opportunistic pathogens from causing harm [[132](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1089/sur.2023.003)]. The gut microbiota's influence extends beyond simple barrier function; it actively participates in regulating intestinal immunity, which synergistically governs anastomotic healing [[149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12695332/)]. This intricate crosstalk means that any disruption to the microbiome can have cascading effects on the local inflammatory environment and, consequently, on the success of the anastomosis [[76](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10741039/)]. Studies focusing on the interaction between the microbiome and dendritic cells further underscore how dysbiosis can alter the immune response and progression of CRC, potentially impacting post-surgical healing dynamics [[76](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10741039/)].

One of the most severe complications following CRC surgery is anastomotic leak (AL), a condition where the joined ends of the colon or rectum fail to heal properly, leading to leakage of intestinal contents into the abdominal cavity [[31](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-65320-w), [132](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1089/sur.2023.003)]. There is substantial evidence linking AL to alterations in the gut microbiota [[150](https://www.surgical.theclinics.com/article/S0039-6109(25)00054-4/fulltext)]. Depletion of the normal commensal microbiome can allow for the overgrowth of pathobionts, which may trigger inflammation and compromise the structural integrity of the anastomosis [[32](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1743919122005283)]. The development of AL is influenced by a combination of factors including surgical trauma, altered intestinal permeability, dysbiosis, and inflammasome activation, all of which are interconnected with the gut microbiota [[152](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2025.1701650/full)]. Strategies aimed at restoring microbiota balance during the perioperative period are therefore being explored as a means to prevent this devastating complication [[150](https://www.surgical.theclinics.com/article/S0039-6109(25)00054-4/fulltext)]. For instance, modulation of the gut microbiota using dietary components like inulin and anti-inflammatory agents like 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) has been shown in mouse models to reinforce the gut barrier and prevent anastomotic leaks, thereby improving oncologic outcomes [[151](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38010363/)]. This body of work solidifies the concept that the gut microbiota is not a passive bystander but an active participant in the healing process, and its manipulation could be a key strategy for improving surgical outcomes [[151](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38010363/)].

Beyond the risk of anastomotic leak, surgery and its associated treatments induce significant dysbiosis that can lead to a range of other postoperative complications. The use of perioperative antibiotics, while intended to prevent infection, contributes to a rapid and profound shift in the gut bacterial communities [[34](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13099-021-00477-7), [111](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8729125/)]. Following surgery, there is often an observed increase in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria, a phylum that includes many known pathogenic bacteria [[87](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10272562/)]. Concurrently, the diversity of the gut microbiota decreases, and fungal communities also change, with reductions in beneficial yeasts like *Candida* and *Saccharomyces* [[87](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10272562/)]. These changes are not merely transient; they are associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications and can persist for extended periods, with some studies noting significant microbial shifts up to 24 months after surgery [[33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10011459/), [178](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8002283/)]. This long-term dysbiosis may even contribute to poorer long-term outcomes, such as cancer recurrence, potentially through mechanisms involving chronic inflammation [[131](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6694/15/6/1913), [147](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/0c04/04611c9090c83ebad05f0828f673cb0d1dfb.pdf)]. Furthermore, the impact of obesity and gut microbiome dysbiosis on the etiology of CRC recurrence after resection has been suggested, reinforcing the link between the post-surgical microbial environment and oncological outcomes [[131](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6694/15/6/1913), [147](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/0c04/04611c9090c83ebad05f0828f673cb0d1dfb.pdf)].

Given the detrimental effects of postoperative dysbiosis, there has been considerable interest in using probiotics to mitigate complications and support recovery. Several meta-analyses and systematic reviews have investigated the efficacy of perioperative probiotic administration in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery [[182](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12029-024-01016-8), [183](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022480425003130)]. The collective evidence suggests that probiotics can be beneficial. One meta-analysis found that probiotics significantly reduced the overall rate of postoperative infections compared to placebo, with a pooled odds ratio of 0.51 [[42](https://www.cancertherapyadvisor.com/factsheets/probiotics-cancer-patient-risk-fact-sheet/)]. Another analysis reported that probiotics were more effective than synbiotics (a combination of probiotics and prebiotics) in reducing postoperative infections, showing a risk ratio of 0.45 [[85](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/15/3066)]. These benefits are thought to arise from the ability of probiotics to maintain intestinal mucosal integrity and reduce bacterial translocation—the movement of bacteria from the gut lumen into the systemic circulation—which is a key driver of postoperative sepsis [[40](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/5/1451)]. Multistrain probiotic formulations have been found to be particularly effective in preventing surgical site infections [[158](https://www.ajmc.com/view/probiotics-prevent-surgical-site-infections-in-patients-with-crc)]. However, the evidence is not uniformly positive across all outcomes. While probiotics show promise in reducing infectious complications, their role in bowel preparation for elective colon cancer surgery is debated. One prospective randomized trial concluded that oral antibiotics are superior to probiotics for preventing surgical-site infections, recommending their use instead [[129](https://www.surgjournal.com/article/S0039-6060(13)00289-4/fulltext), [130](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0039606013002894)]. This discrepancy highlights the complexity of interventions and underscores that the timing, type, and purpose of microbiome modulation are critical variables. Despite these nuances, the general trend supports the use of probiotics to bolster the gut barrier and reduce the burden of infectious morbidity following major abdominal surgery.

## Chemotherapy and the Gut Microbiota: Modulators of Efficacy and Toxicity

Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment, yet its therapeutic window is often narrow, limited by significant gastrointestinal toxicities and the development of drug resistance [[100](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10978024/), [102](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12672-025-03574-0)]. The gut microbiota has emerged as a critical, modifiable factor that profoundly influences both the efficacy and the toxicity of standard chemotherapeutic agents, including 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), oxaliplatin, and irinotecan [[8](https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/S0016-5085(22)01196-9/fulltext), [102](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12672-025-03574-0)]. This complex relationship operates through multiple mechanisms: direct metabolic transformation of drugs, modulation of host immune responses, and alteration of the intestinal epithelial barrier's integrity [[22](https://gut.bmj.com/content/69/10/1867), [23](https://www.explorationpub.com/Journals/etat/Article/1002132)]. Certain bacterial species can enhance chemotherapy's antitumor effects, while others promote resistance and exacerbate side effects [[10](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/retrieve/f87d553d-9aab-4829-85b8-be409d6cafcf/jcm-13-00420.pdf), [99](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0891584924004052)]. Conversely, the administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics can inadvertently impair the efficacy of these same drugs, highlighting the symbiotic relationship between specific microbes and therapeutic success [[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11464465/)]. Understanding these interactions is paramount for developing strategies to optimize treatment outcomes and manage toxicity in CRC patients.

The gut microbiota can directly and indirectly enhance the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents, representing a positive interaction. Microbes possess a vast array of enzymes that can metabolize drugs, sometimes converting them into more potent cytotoxic forms or altering their local concentration within the tumor microenvironment [[22](https://gut.bmj.com/content/69/10/1867), [102](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12672-025-03574-0)]. This metabolic activity is a key mechanism through which the microbiome can boost chemotherapy's effectiveness [[102](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12672-025-03574-0)]. Furthermore, the microbiota plays a crucial role in shaping the host's immune system, which can synergize with chemotherapy. By modulating immune cell populations and cytokine profiles, a healthy and diverse microbiome can create a more favorable environment for antitumor immune responses, which can complement the direct cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy [[23](https://www.explorationpub.com/Journals/etat/Article/1002132), [78](https://egastroenterology.bmj.com/content/1/1/e100007)]. This immunomodulatory function is particularly relevant for drugs whose full therapeutic effect relies on eliciting an immune-mediated response against the tumor [[23](https://www.explorationpub.com/Journals/etat/Article/1002132)]. The positive impact of the microbiome on chemotherapy is not just theoretical; clinical observations suggest that patients who respond well to treatment often exhibit a gut microbial composition conducive to therapy [[67](http://www.jci.org/articles/view/184321/files/pdf)]. For instance, a stable fecal microbiome alpha diversity during neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy was associated with a better pathologic response, suggesting a similar principle may apply to chemotherapy alone [[97](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/116/11/1834/7699791), [143](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/4437/4404a67e1a223ed0f671ff035ad20e58a88a.pdf)]. The restoration of a healthy microbial balance post-treatment may also be important for long-term outcomes, as evidenced by studies showing that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can inhibit CRC progression by reversing gut microbial disorders and ameliorating excessive intestinal inflammation [[72](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2023.1126808/full)].

Despite these potential benefits, the negative impacts of the gut microbiota on chemotherapy are significant and clinically relevant. The most prominent negative interaction is the promotion of chemoresistance. The bacterium *Fusobacterium nucleatum* (Fn) has been consistently identified as a key player in this phenomenon [[99](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0891584924004052), [100](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10978024/)]. Approximately 50% of CRC patients exhibit high levels of Fn, which is strongly associated with chemotherapy resistance and a poor prognosis [[101](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41102802/), [128](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5852398/)]. Fn promotes resistance to both 5-FU and oxaliplatin through several molecular mechanisms. It can target Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR-4) and MyD88 innate immune signaling pathways to protect cancer cells from apoptosis [[10](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/retrieve/f87d553d-9aab-4829-85b8-be409d6cafcf/jcm-13-00420.pdf)]. Additionally, Fn induces the expression of autophagic proteins like ULK1 and ATG7, triggering protective autophagy that allows cancer cells to survive the stress of chemotherapy [[163](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(17)30825-5), [181](https://www.ivanrivasmd.com/fusobacterium-nucleatum-and-colorectal-cancer/)]. It also prevents apoptosis from oxaliplatin and 5-FU by upregulating the expression of ANO1 on colon cancer cells [[180](https://www.dovepress.com/fusobacterium-nucleatum-prevents-apoptosis-in-colorectal-cancer-cells--peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-CMAR)]. High abundance of Fn is not only linked to resistance but also to higher rates of CRC recurrence and poorer survival, establishing it as a significant prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target [[192](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11645352/), [206](https://bpgweb.azurewebsites.net/1948-5204/full/v16/i6/2271.htm), [207](https://www.facebook.com/groups/695263405584468/posts/774014697709338/)]. Other bacteria may also contribute to resistance, making the overall microbial composition a critical determinant of treatment failure [[22](https://gut.bmj.com/content/69/10/1867)].

Another major negative impact of the gut microbiota is its central role in the development of chemotherapy-induced intestinal mucositis (CIM), a common and debilitating side effect experienced by up to 100% of patients receiving chemotherapeutics [[90](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8247/17/8/1020), [168](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8129190/)]. CIM is characterized by inflammatory-driven damage to the intestinal mucosa, leading to severe diarrhea, pain, and malnutrition [[91](https://repository.ubn.ru.nl/bitstream/handle/2066/306397/1/306397.pdf), [114](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6747860/)]. The gut microbiota is implicated in this process through two primary mechanisms: translocation and dysbiosis [[14](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13014-020-01735-9)]. Chemotherapy damages the intestinal epithelial cells and the tight junctions that form the barrier, increasing intestinal permeability [[64](https://www.mdpi.com/2305-6304/11/12/1011), [169](https://www.jcancer.org/v16p0648.pdf)]. This damaged barrier allows bacteria and their components, such as lipopolysaccharide from Enterobacteriaceae, to translocate into the underlying tissue, triggering a robust inflammatory response [[63](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docman/irua/6dab0emotoM59), [140](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7805150/), [200](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/collinsella-aerofaciens)]. Simultaneously, chemotherapy induces dysbiosis, disrupting the natural balance of gut bacteria [[169](https://www.jcancer.org/v16p0648.pdf)]. This dysregulated microbial community can further impair the mucosal barrier's function and sustain a pro-inflammatory state, creating a vicious cycle that worsens mucositis [[169](https://www.jcancer.org/v16p0648.pdf), [186](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s10020-024-00873-0)]. The incidence and severity of diarrhea, a hallmark of CIM, are directly linked to this microbial dysregulation. The efficacy of certain chemotherapies can also be paradoxically reduced by concomitant antibiotic use, which depletes beneficial microbes required for optimal drug activity [[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11464465/)]. This demonstrates that the microbiota's influence extends beyond toxicity to directly affecting the fundamental mechanism of action of anticancer drugs [[22](https://gut.bmj.com/content/69/10/1867)].

The therapeutic use of probiotics to manage chemotherapy-related side effects presents a promising but complex picture. Multiple studies and meta-analyses indicate that probiotic supplementation can be effective in mitigating chemotherapy-induced diarrhea (CID) [[55](https://examine.com/research-feed/study/9QEMb9/), [117](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40629588/)]. A comprehensive analysis of 18 randomized controlled trials provided compelling evidence that probiotics can prevent the onset of diarrhea, shorten its duration, and alleviate gastrointestinal symptoms in patients undergoing chemotherapy [[117](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40629588/)]. Specific strains have shown particular promise; for example, *Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG* consumption was found to reduce the incidence of severe diarrhea and abdominal discomfort in CRC patients receiving 5-FU [[60](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13063-023-07137-y)]. Animal studies have also demonstrated that probiotics like *L. casei* variety rhamnosus can attenuate intestinal injury caused by 5-FU-based chemotherapy [[11](https://academic.oup.com/gpb/article/21/1/84/7274172), [196](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2018.00983/full)], and other strains like CIDCA 133 have been shown to reduce the shortening of small intestine length induced by 5-FU [[197](https://encyclopedia.pub/entry/50749)]. Oral administration of probiotics has been demonstrated to decrease the incidence of both diarrhea and oral mucositis in meta-analyses [[198](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8922230/)]. However, the evidence is not without controversy. Some large-scale analyses have failed to demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in the most severe forms of diarrhea (grade 3/4). One phase III trial found no association between probiotic administration and a reduction in grade 3/4 diarrhea (placebo arm 11.8% vs. probiotic arm 7.9%) [[201](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10438450/)]. Similarly, a pooled analysis of two probiotic trials showed no significant difference in grade 3/4 diarrhea rates (placebo 12.7% vs. probiotics 6.6%) [[202](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1438657/full), [204](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11298351/)]. Another review concluded that probiotics and synbiotics do not have a significant effect on reducing chemotherapy-associated toxicity and diarrhea in CRC patients [[59](https://ijms.sums.ac.ir/article_48720.html)]. This discrepancy suggests that the benefits of probiotics may be highly dependent on the specific bacterial strain(s) used, the dosage, the patient population, and the chemotherapy regimen, indicating a need for more personalized and targeted approaches rather than a one-size-fits-all solution [[190](https://www.cancerbiomed.org/content/early/2026/05/07/j.issn.2095-3941.2026.0133)].

## Immunotherapy and the Gut Microbiota: Key Determinants of Response and Immune-Related Colitis

Immunotherapy, specifically immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has transformed the treatment landscape for a subset of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, particularly those with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) tumors [[48](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2022.1032314/full)]. However, the remarkable success of ICIs is not universal, with many patients failing to respond initially (primary resistance) or ceasing to respond over time (acquired resistance) [[69](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13045-018-0592-6)]. An extensive body of research published up to September 2024 has firmly established the gut microbiome as a pivotal external factor that dictates the efficacy and toxicity of ICI therapy [[66](https://www.irjournal.org/journal/view.php?doi=10.5217/ir.2023.00019), [80](https://norgenbiotek.com/blog/influence-microbiome-immunotherapy)]. The composition and diversity of a patient's gut microbiota can serve as a powerful predictive biomarker for treatment outcomes, with a diverse and specific microbial community being strongly associated with a favorable response [[47](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11268651/), [53](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0307639), [54](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3506&context=ecuworks2022-2026)]. Mechanistically, commensal bacteria and their metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), can modulate the host immune system to enhance antitumor activity [[77](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2023.1158200/full), [108](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-24331-1)]. Conversely, dysbiosis is a key contributor to the most common and severe adverse event of ICI therapy: immune-related colitis, which can be debilitating and may require discontinuation of life-saving treatment [[112](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9648670/), [133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9515466/)].

The positive impact of the gut microbiota on immunotherapy is primarily mediated through its ability to shape and amplify the host's antitumor immune response. A diverse gut microbiome is consistently correlated with improved clinical outcomes in patients receiving ICIs [[62](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-021-01741-x), [67](http://www.jci.org/articles/view/184321/files/pdf)]. Specific commensal bacterial species have been identified in preclinical models and human studies that can promote antitumor immunity [[67](http://www.jci.org/articles/view/184321/files/pdf)]. For example, *Akkermansia muciniphila* and certain species of *Faecalibacterium* have been linked to better responses to anti-PD-1 therapy [[27](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/collinsella-aerofaciens)]. The mechanisms behind this are multifaceted. Gut microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), produced from the fermentation of dietary fiber, can modulate the function of CD8+ T cells, enhancing their anti-tumor activity and improving the efficacy of adoptive immunotherapies [[77](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2023.1158200/full), [108](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-24331-1), [171](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9896517/)]. Butyrate, a specific SCFA, plays a crucial role in enhancing the innate immune response and promoting the activation and differentiation of immune cells [[107](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10919761/), [109](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2607/12/6/1235)]. Furthermore, SCFAs can regulate the mTOR-S6K pathway and induce both effector and regulatory T cells, fine-tuning the immune response to favor tumor clearance [[174](https://escholarship.org/content/qt9qk9m4j7/qt9qk9m4j7.pdf?v=lg)]. Beyond direct immune cell modulation, the microbiota influences dendritic cells, which are critical for presenting tumor antigens to T cells [[76](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10741039/)]. By interacting with these antigen-presenting cells, a healthy microbiome can prime a more robust and sustained antitumor T-cell response [[80](https://norgenbiotek.com/blog/influence-microbiome-immunotherapy)]. This strong association has led to the exploration of the gut microbiome as a non-invasive predictive biomarker, allowing clinicians to stratify patients and potentially select those most likely to benefit from ICI therapy [[47](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11268651/), [54](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3506&context=ecuworks2022-2026)].

While a favorable microbiome enhances ICI efficacy, its dysregulation is strongly implicated in the development of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), most notably immune-related colitis [[45](https://aacrjournals.org/cancerimmunolres/article/12/3/308/734675/Immune-Related-Colitis-Is-Associated-with-Fecal), [112](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9648670/)]. Colitis is one of the most common and serious toxicities of ICI therapy, occurring in 7% to 30% of treated patients [[46](https://www.cghjournal.org/article/S1542-3565(20)30112-9/fulltext)]. In severe or refractory cases, it can be life-threatening and may lead to the permanent termination of cancer therapy [[44](https://www.irjournal.org/journal/view.php?number=1019), [133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9515466/)]. The symptoms often include diarrhea and inflammation of the colon, which can resemble inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [[114](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6747860/)]. Dysbiosis is considered a key factor in driving the pathogenesis of ICI-induced colitis [[112](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9648670/)]. When ICIs remove the "brakes" from the immune system, a dysbiotic microbiome can provide aberrant signals that lead to an uncontrolled inflammatory attack on the intestinal lining [[45](https://aacrjournals.org/cancerimmunolres/article/12/3/308/734675/Immune-Related-Colitis-Is-Associated-with-Fecal)]. The incidence of colitis increases significantly when ICIs are used in combination, reaching up to 13.6% [[65](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11225377/)]. The management of this toxicity is a major clinical challenge, and current first-line treatments involve corticosteroids, which can themselves further disrupt the gut microbiota, potentially undermining the very efficacy of the immunotherapy they are meant to support [[43](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9229182/)]. The complex link between the gut microbiome, host immunity, and cancer makes the variations in an individual's microbiota a critical variable in determining both the therapeutic success and the safety profile of ICI treatment [[66](https://www.irjournal.org/journal/view.php?doi=10.5217/ir.2023.00019)].

An emerging and concerning area of research is the potential negative impact of probiotic supplementation on the efficacy of immunotherapy. While probiotics are widely used to manage side effects, there is growing evidence that their indiscriminate use might be counterproductive for ICI-treated patients [[5](https://www.parkerici.org/the-latest/could-probiotics-reduce-immunotherapys-chances-of-working-in-cancer-patients/), [119](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11968366/)]. The prevailing hypothesis is that a diverse native gut microbiome is often required to mount a strong antitumor response to ICIs [[67](http://www.jci.org/articles/view/184321/files/pdf)]. Introducing a limited number of exogenous probiotic strains could disrupt this complex and finely tuned endogenous ecosystem, potentially reducing a patient's ability to respond to checkpoint inhibitor therapy [[5](https://www.parkerici.org/the-latest/could-probiotics-reduce-immunotherapys-chances-of-working-in-cancer-patients/)]. This concern is supported by some clinical data suggesting that the composition of the gut microbiota might influence the efficacy of immunotherapeutic methods [[118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12239337/)]. As the field moves towards microbiome-based strategies to enhance ICI efficacy, the question of whether to preserve or augment the native microbiome becomes critically important, and the routine co-administration of broad-spectrum probiotics is now viewed with increasing caution [[119](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11968366/)]. The clinical value of combining probiotics with ICIs remains a subject of intense debate, requiring further research to determine if specific, carefully selected probiotic strains could offer benefits without compromising therapeutic response [[119](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11968366/)].

## Radiotherapy and the Gut Microbiota: Mediators of Radiation Enteritis and Therapeutic Response

Pelvic and abdominal radiotherapy is a critical component of the multimodal treatment for locally advanced colorectal cancer, aiming to shrink tumors prior to surgery (neoadjuvant therapy) or to eradicate residual disease postoperatively [[93](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/c348/6aa175436a0845e392037bf471c1f3c91805.pdf), [98](https://www.cell.com/med/fulltext/S2666-6340(24)00261-7)]. However, the therapeutic benefit of radiation is often constrained by its damaging effects on the highly sensitive intestinal tissue, leading to a condition known as radiation enteritis or radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) [[15](https://www.e-roj.org/m/journal/view.php?number=1558), [93](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/c348/6aa175436a0845e392037bf471c1f3c91805.pdf)]. This complication manifests as severe gastrointestinal symptoms, including diarrhea, abdominal pain, and malabsorption, and significantly impairs a patient's quality of life [[92](https://academic.oup.com/proteincell/article/15/2/83/7226836)]. A growing body of evidence demonstrates that the gut microbiota is a central mediator of both the acute toxicity and the long-term response to radiotherapy [[95](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9927011/), [96](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2023.1128774/pdf)]. Radiation exposure causes direct DNA damage to intestinal epithelial cells and disrupts the intestinal barrier, leading to a state of dysbiosis [[64](https://www.mdpi.com/2305-6304/11/12/1011), [140](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7805150/)]. This radiation-induced dysbiosis, in turn, is not merely a consequence but an active driver of the injury, exacerbating inflammation and increasing the bowel's susceptibility to damage [[16](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/10/10/1405), [18](https://ajp.amjpathol.org/article/S0002-9440(24)00077-4/fulltext)]. Conversely, the baseline and dynamic composition of the gut microbiome is emerging as a predictor of the pathologic response to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, suggesting its potential role in personalizing treatment strategies [[97](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/116/11/1834/7699791), [122](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1535610822005608)].

The negative impact of the gut microbiota on radiotherapy outcomes is most evident in its contribution to radiation enteritis, a major dose-limiting toxicity. The primary mechanism involves radiation-induced damage to the intestinal epithelium, which results in apoptosis of epithelial cells, loss of tight junctions, and ulceration [[19](https://gut.bmj.com/content/67/1/97), [64](https://www.mdpi.com/2305-6304/11/12/1011)]. This physical damage compromises the integrity of the intestinal barrier, a condition known as increased intestinal permeability [[186](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s10020-024-00873-0)]. The compromised barrier allows luminal bacteria and their products to translocate into the underlying tissue, activating a potent inflammatory response that drives the symptoms of enteritis [[94](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/15/1/48), [140](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7805150/)]. The gut microbiota plays a dual role in this process. First, radiation itself acts as a powerful dysbiotic agent, causing a significant decrease in microbial diversity and richness [[13](https://www.cell.com/trends/endocrinology-metabolism/fulltext/S1043-2760(23)00108-X), [18](https://ajp.amjpathol.org/article/S0002-9440(24)00077-4/fulltext)]. This is often accompanied by a relative decrease in beneficial probiotic bacteria like *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10102376/)]. Second, the resulting dysbiosis is considered pathogenic and seems to have a causal role in driving the injury [[16](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/10/10/1405)]. The dysbiotic state instigates proinflammatory factors that worsen the radiation-induced damage, creating a vicious cycle of injury and inflammation [[18](https://ajp.amjpathol.org/article/S0002-9440(24)00077-4/fulltext)]. Changes in the abundance of bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) have also been noted, which can further disrupt intestinal health [[20](https://www.facebook.com/danafarbercancerinstitute/posts/the-gastrointestinal-complications-of-radiation-therapy-vary-widely-and-generall/581401640686164/)]. Therefore, the gut microbiota is not a passive victim of radiation but an active participant in amplifying its injurious effects on the bowel [[92](https://academic.oup.com/proteincell/article/15/2/83/7226836), [124](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10326510/)].

In addition to mediating toxicity, the gut microbiota also appears to play a significant role in determining the therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy. Similar to its influence on chemotherapy, the composition of the gut microbiome is associated with the pathologic response after preoperative chemoradiation in patients with rectal cancer [[122](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1535610822005608), [123](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41416-022-01980-7)]. Studies have identified microbial characteristics that differ between patients who achieve a pathological complete response (pCR)—meaning no viable cancer cells are found in the surgically removed tissue—and those with a poor response [[121](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7989702/)]. In one study, patients who achieved a pCR exhibited a stable fecal microbiome alpha diversity during neoadjuvant therapy, whereas the diversity of poor responders tended to decrease [[97](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/116/11/1834/7699791), [143](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/4437/4404a67e1a223ed0f671ff035ad20e58a88a.pdf)]. This suggests that maintaining a resilient and diverse microbiome during treatment may be conducive to a better antitumor effect. The mechanisms are likely similar to those seen in immunotherapy, where microbial metabolites can modulate the local and systemic immune response to enhance tumor cell killing [[95](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9927011/), [96](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2023.1128774/pdf)]. By acting as a regulator of the immunogenicity of CRC, the gut microbiota could potentially be targeted to improve the efficacy of radiotherapy and overcome resistance [[95](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9927011/)]. This positions the microbiome as a novel and potential target for intervention, aiming to convert "cold" tumors into "hot" ones that are more responsive to radiation-induced immunogenic cell death [[95](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9927011/)].

## Future Outlook: Probiotics and Prebiotics as Adjunctive Therapies

Probiotics and prebiotics represent two of the most accessible and widely studied strategies for modulating the gut microbiome to improve colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment outcomes [[165](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304419X23001397)]. Probiotics are live microorganisms intended to confer a health benefit, while prebiotics are substrates selectively utilized by host microbes to foster the growth of beneficial bacteria [[2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016508522011969)]. The rationale for their use is compelling: by introducing beneficial bacteria or feeding the existing ones, it may be possible to restore a healthy microbial balance, mitigate treatment-related toxicities, and potentially enhance therapeutic efficacy [[2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016508522011969), [4](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/11795549231188225)]. Probiotics have shown promise in improving the quality of life for CRC patients and may exert direct anticancer effects by inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and regulating the immune system [[70](https://consensus.app/home/blog/can-probiotics-help-cancer-patients/), [105](https://mednexus.org/doi/full/10.1097/CM9.0000000000001887)]. However, despite a wealth of preclinical and some clinical evidence, the application of these interventions is fraught with challenges, including mixed clinical results, safety concerns, and questions about their impact on complex therapies like immunotherapy [[3](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full), [5](https://www.parkerici.org/the-latest/could-probiotics-reduce-immunotherapys-chances-of-working-in-cancer-patients/)]. The future of their use will likely depend on a more nuanced understanding of strain-specific effects and the development of personalized, precision-based approaches.

The positive impacts of probiotics in the context of CRC treatment are most evident in their ability to manage and prevent treatment-related complications, particularly those arising from chemotherapy and surgery. For chemotherapy-induced diarrhea (CID), a common and debilitating side effect of regimens containing 5-FU and oxaliplatin, multiple meta-analyses have reported that probiotic supplementation can be effective [[55](https://examine.com/research-feed/study/9QEMb9/), [117](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40629588/)]. A comprehensive analysis of 18 randomized controlled trials found compelling evidence that probiotics significantly prevent the onset of diarrhea, shorten its duration, and alleviate gastrointestinal symptoms in patients undergoing chemotherapy [[117](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40629588/)]. Specific strains have demonstrated particular utility; for instance, *Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG* was shown to reduce the incidence of severe diarrhea and abdominal discomfort in CRC patients [[60](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13063-023-07137-y)]. Animal studies have further supported these findings, showing that probiotics like *L. casei* variety rhamnosus can attenuate intestinal injury caused by 5-FU-based chemotherapy [[11](https://academic.oup.com/gpb/article/21/1/84/7274172), [196](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2018.00983/full)]. Similarly, in the surgical setting, perioperative probiotic administration has been associated with a reduction in overall postoperative infectious complications [[41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9699544/), [115](https://www.mdpi.com/1648-9144/58/11/1644)]. The proposed mechanisms include the maintenance of intestinal mucosal integrity and the reduction of bacterial translocation, thereby lowering the risk of sepsis [[40](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/5/1451)]. Beyond symptom management, some observational studies have even suggested a link between probiotic use and lower cancer recurrence rates, although this finding requires further validation [[71](https://www.mdpi.com/2305-6320/5/3/101)]. Probiotics may also help prevent chemotherapy-induced diarrhea [[55](https://examine.com/research-feed/study/9QEMb9/)].

However, the evidence supporting the use of probiotics is not without significant contradictions and limitations. While some studies show a clear benefit in reducing diarrhea, other large-scale analyses have failed to demonstrate a statistically significant effect on the most severe forms of the condition (grade 3/4) [[201](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10438450/), [202](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1438657/full), [204](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11298351/)]. This inconsistency raises questions about the efficacy of probiotics as a universal solution and suggests that benefits may be highly dependent on the specific bacterial strains used, the dosages administered, and the genetic background of the patient [[190](https://www.cancerbiomed.org/content/early/2026/05/07/j.issn.2095-3941.2026.0133)]. Furthermore, the effectiveness of probiotics in preventing chemotherapy-associated toxicity and diarrhea in CRC patients has been questioned in some reviews [[59](https://ijms.sums.ac.ir/article_48720.html)]. Perhaps more concerning is the emerging evidence regarding the potential negative impact of probiotics on immunotherapy. Given that a diverse native microbiome is often a prerequisite for a successful response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), the introduction of a limited number of exogenous probiotic strains could theoretically disrupt this complex ecosystem and blunt the antitumor immune response [[5](https://www.parkerici.org/the-latest/could-probiotics-reduce-immunotherapys-chances-of-working-in-cancer-patients/), [119](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11968366/)]. This has led to a cautious approach, with ongoing debate about the safety and wisdom of co-administering probiotics with ICIs [[119](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11968366/)]. Finally, safety is a legitimate concern, albeit rare. In vulnerable populations, such as severely ill or immunocompromised patients, probiotics have been associated with adverse events, including infections like bacteremia and fungemia [[104](https://www.infectiousdiseaseadvisor.com/features/biotherapeutics-for-the-prevention-of-clostridium-difficile-infection/), [106](https://www.facebook.com/ESCMID/posts/what-happens-after-an-accidental-intravenous-probiotic-injectionn-monnerat-et-al/2908782252488940/), [156](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41392-022-00974-4)]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has reported cases of serious adverse events, including *E. coli* bacteremia, linked to probiotic products, underscoring the need for rigorous quality control and patient selection [[137](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2022.916543/full)].

Prebiotics, which are non-digestible food ingredients that beneficially affect the host by selectively stimulating the growth and/or activity of beneficial gut bacteria, offer a complementary approach to microbiome modulation [[2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016508522011969)]. Unlike probiotics, which introduce live microbes, prebiotics work by nourishing the existing microbial community. They are generally considered safe and have a beneficial effect on the gut microbiome by promoting the growth of commensal microbes like *Lactobacillus* [[2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016508522011969)]. A key source of prebiotics is dietary fiber, which is fermented by gut bacteria to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate, propionate, and acetate [[20](https://www.facebook.com/danafarbercancerinstitute/posts/the-gastrointestinal-complications-of-radiation-therapy-vary-widely-and-generall/581401640686164/), [107](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10919761/)]. These SCFAs are crucial for maintaining gut health, as they provide energy for colonocytes, strengthen the intestinal barrier, and exert anti-inflammatory effects [[79](https://encyclopedia.pub/entry/43283), [188](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612155/)]. Observational studies have linked habitual preoperative dietary fiber intake with a lower risk of complications after CRC surgery, suggesting a protective role for a fiber-rich diet in supporting postoperative recovery [[88](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamasurgery/fullarticle/2781033)]. The effectiveness of prebiotic supplementation as a therapeutic approach in CRC is still unclear and requires further investigation [[3](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)]. Nonetheless, the principle of modulating the gut microbiota through diet remains a promising avenue for prevention and adjunctive therapy [[165](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304419X23001397)]. The combined use of probiotics and prebiotics (synbiotics) has also been explored, with some evidence suggesting probiotics may be more effective than synbiotics in reducing postoperative infections after colorectal surgery [[85](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/15/3066)].

## Future Outlook: Fecal Microbiota Transplantation for Enhancing Cancer Therapy

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) has emerged as a powerful and versatile tool for modulating the gut ecosystem, moving from its established role in treating recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection to a frontier application in oncology [[74](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-1729/12/5/723), [75](https://www.e-ce.org/journal/view.php?doi=10.5946/ce.2021.072)]. FMT involves the transfer of processed fecal material from a healthy donor to a recipient, with the goal of re-establishing a healthy and diverse gut microbial community [[74](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-1729/12/5/723)]. In the context of colorectal cancer (CRC), FMT is being investigated for its potential to overcome therapeutic resistance, mitigate severe treatment-related toxicities, and directly inhibit tumor progression [[135](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/ebiom/article/PIIS2352-3964(24)00002-1/fulltext), [166](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2026.1880573/full)]. Early-phase clinical trials and case series have yielded promising results, demonstrating that FMT can reshape the tumor microenvironment, restore microbial homeostasis, and improve patient outcomes [[39](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(23)00492-3/fulltext), [82](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scitranslmed.abq4006), [167](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/epdf/10.1002/cnr2.70455)]. However, alongside its immense therapeutic potential lies a significant and unavoidable focus on safety. The documented risk of transmitting pathogenic organisms, including antibiotic-resistant bacteria, necessitates stringent donor screening and careful monitoring, making the long-term safety of FMT a critical area of ongoing research [[38](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352396424000021), [176](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352396422003449)].

The most compelling positive application of FMT in CRC treatment is its potential to rescue patients experiencing severe immune-related adverse events (irAEs) from immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy, particularly refractory immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced colitis [[68](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6322556/)]. ICI-induced colitis is a major dose-limiting toxicity that occurs in up to 30% of patients and can be life-threatening, sometimes requiring the cessation of effective cancer treatment [[46](https://www.cghjournal.org/article/S1542-3565(20)30112-9/fulltext), [133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9515466/)]. Standard treatments like corticosteroids can be ineffective in severe cases and may further disrupt the gut microbiota [[43](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9229182/)]. FMT offers a novel biological strategy to address this problem by directly correcting the underlying microbial dysbiosis that drives the inflammation [[68](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6322556/)]. A case series of ICI-associated colitis successfully treated with FMT reported that 83% of patients achieved symptom improvement, with FMT leading to the reconstitution of the gut microbiota [[68](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6322556/), [103](https://megan-halpin-mxlk.squarespace.com/s/Microbiome-alteration-via-fecal-microbiota-transplantation-is-effective-for-refractory-immune-checkp.pdf)]. Another study confirmed the utility of FMT in managing ICI toxicity in a series of 12 patients with advanced cancer, demonstrating its potential as a salvage therapy [[82](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scitranslmed.abq4006)]. Beyond managing toxicity, FMT holds promise for overcoming primary and acquired resistance to immunotherapy. Early-phase trials have shown that FMT from ICI responders can restore sensitivity in non-responders. For instance, a phase II trial in patients with microsatellite-stable (MSS) metastatic CRC—a group notoriously resistant to single-agent ICIs—showed that combining FMT with tislelizumab and fruquintinib led to significant clinical benefits and an acceptable safety profile, suggesting FMT may serve as a "rescue strategy" to overcome resistance [[39](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(23)00492-3/fulltext), [120](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304419X25002185)]. This aligns with findings from esophageal cancer, where FMT from responders restored antitumor activity in germ-free or antibiotic-treated mice, confirming the causal role of the microbiota in ICI efficacy [[27](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/collinsella-aerofaciens)]. FMT has also been shown to inhibit CRC progression by reversing gut microbial disorder and ameliorating excessive intestinal inflammation [[72](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2023.1126808/full)].

Despite these encouraging results, the use of FMT is not without significant risks and challenges, with safety being the paramount concern. The procedure involves transferring trillions of microorganisms from a donor, and while rigorous screening is standard practice, there remains a risk of transmitting undetected pathogens [[38](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352396424000021), [155](https://gut.bmj.com/content/72/9/1642)]. Published reports have highlighted this risk, with two patients in separate clinical trials developing broad-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing *E. coli* bacteremia after undergoing FMT [[81](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9589549/), [176](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352396422003449)]. In another documented case, a patient developed *E. coli* bacteremia transmitted via FMT [[73](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1910437)]. These incidents underscore the critical need for more robust and standardized donor screening protocols to ensure the long-term safety of the procedure [[38](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352396424000021)]. Although FMT is generally well-tolerated in the short term, with most adverse events being mild to moderate, the potential for serious, systemic infections cannot be ignored, especially in the context of cancer patients who may be immunocompromised [[83](https://www.contagionlive.com/view/fmt-for-the-treatment-of-cdifficile-infection-whats-new-in-2020)]. The long-term consequences of permanently altering a patient's gut microbiome are also not fully understood, and this uncertainty calls for continued vigilance and follow-up [[38](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352396424000021)]. Ongoing clinical trials are actively evaluating the safety and feasibility of different FMT delivery methods, such as frozen FMT delivered via retention enema versus lyophilized powder, to optimize the procedure and minimize risks [[37](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05786404)].

The application of FMT in oncology is still in its early stages, with more than 70 clinical trials having proven its safety and efficacy in various contexts [[58](https://www.oaepublish.com/articles/mrr.2023.80)]. Its role is being explored across the spectrum of cancer care, from enhancing the efficacy of existing therapies like immunotherapy to managing complications from radiotherapy [[187](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167814020300232)]. For example, FMT has been investigated as a potential treatment for radiation enteritis, with studies showing it can relieve intestinal symptoms and endoscopic mucosal injury in patients affected by this condition [[187](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167814020300232)]. The overarching goal is to harness the power of the gut microbiome to improve patient outcomes [[167](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/epdf/10.1002/cnr2.70455)]. The success of FMT hinges on identifying the "right" microbial community to transplant. This involves characterizing the fecal, duodenal, and even tumor microbiota of both donors and recipients to find optimal matches that can confer therapeutic benefit [[97](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/116/11/1834/7699791), [121](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7989702/)]. As our understanding of the complex microbial networks involved in cancer immunity deepens, so too will our ability to design targeted FMT strategies. This may involve transplanting defined consortia of bacterial strains rather than whole fecal matter, which could offer greater safety and reproducibility. Ultimately, FMT represents a paradigm shift in cancer care, moving towards a more holistic view of the patient where the microbiome is recognized as a key therapeutic target [[164](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305737225001471), [166](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2026.1880573/full)].
