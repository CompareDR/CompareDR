## Executive Summary

The gut microbiota has become a credible modifier of colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment outcomes because it can influence **drug metabolism, mucosal barrier integrity, inflammation, immune priming, tumor microenvironment composition, and treatment toxicity**. CRC is particularly relevant because the tumor arises in direct contact with dense microbial communities, and several organisms—especially *Fusobacterium nucleatum*, enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis*, and genotoxic *Escherichia coli*—have been mechanistically linked to colorectal carcinogenesis, immune evasion, and treatment resistance ([Wong & Yu, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)).

By September 2024, the clearest human clinical evidence concerned **perioperative probiotics/synbiotics** and treatment-toxicity mitigation. A 2024 systematic review identified **24 randomized controlled trials** of prebiotics, probiotics, or synbiotics in CRC treatment settings: **17 in surgery**, **7 in chemotherapy**, **1 also addressing chemoradiotherapy**, and **none in CRC patients receiving immunotherapy** ([Moreira et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). This distribution is important: it shows that the clinical literature was not evenly mature across treatment areas. Surgery had the strongest trial base; chemotherapy had moderate supportive-care evidence; radiotherapy and immunotherapy had biologically plausible but clinically thinner CRC-specific evidence.

My concrete interpretation is that, as of September 2024, microbiota modulation in CRC should be viewed as **adjunctive and investigational**, with the most immediate clinical value in:  
- reducing postoperative infectious and inflammatory complications,  
- mitigating chemotherapy-associated gastrointestinal toxicity,  
- supporting intestinal barrier recovery,  
- identifying patients at risk of poor response or toxicity, and  
- designing future trials using strain-level and functional microbiome biomarkers.

Routine use of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), generic over-the-counter probiotics, or broad microbiome manipulation to improve tumor response was **not yet justified outside clinical trials**, except where FMT was already established for recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection or was being studied for immune-mediated colitis.

## Biological Foundation: Why the Microbiota Matters in CRC Therapy

CRC is not merely adjacent to the gut microbiome; it develops in a tissue ecosystem continuously shaped by microbial antigens, metabolites, toxins, and inflammatory signals. Multiple CRC-associated microbes have been implicated in tumor initiation and progression. *Fusobacterium nucleatum* is associated with poorer survival and has been reported to promote chemoresistance through autophagy activation, while enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis* can damage epithelial integrity and drive inflammatory signaling. Genotoxic *E. coli* strains can contribute to DNA damage through colibactin-producing mechanisms ([Wong & Yu, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)).

The microbiota can affect therapy through several overlapping mechanisms:

- **Drug metabolism:** Gut bacteria can activate, inactivate, or toxify drugs and metabolites.
- **Barrier function:** Chemotherapy and radiation damage mucosa; dysbiosis can worsen permeability and inflammation.
- **Immune regulation:** Commensals can promote dendritic-cell maturation, T-cell priming, and antitumor immunity, while pathobionts may expand immunosuppressive regulatory T cells or myeloid-derived suppressor cells.
- **Metabolites:** Short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, tryptophan metabolites, and other microbial products can regulate inflammation and tumor-cell behavior.
- **Colonization resistance:** Beneficial microbes can compete with pathogens and reduce postoperative or treatment-related infectious complications.

This means the microbiome is not a single “good” or “bad” factor. Its effect depends on **species, strain, microbial genes, host immune status, tumor subtype, treatment type, and timing**. That is why generic statements such as “probiotics help CRC” are too broad and not clinically adequate.

## Detailed Synthesis by Treatment Area

### 1. Surgery: The Most Mature Clinical Evidence Base

By September 2024, surgery was the CRC treatment area with the largest number of randomized microbiota-modulation trials. The 2024 systematic review found that **17 of 24 RCTs** of pre-, pro-, or synbiotic supplementation in CRC treatment were surgical studies ([Moreira et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). This makes perioperative care the most evidence-supported setting for microbiota-directed adjuncts.

The rationale is straightforward. CRC surgery disrupts anatomy, exposes patients to bowel preparation and antibiotics, alters diet, and can impair gut motility. These factors can produce dysbiosis and reduce colonization resistance, increasing the risk of postoperative infection and inflammatory complications. Probiotics and synbiotics may counteract this by supporting epithelial tight junctions, stimulating mucin production, and reducing pathogen colonization ([Wong & Yu, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)).

However, the evidence does not yet prove that perioperative probiotics reduce CRC recurrence or improve overall survival. The strongest case is for **supportive perioperative outcomes**, not direct anticancer efficacy. My interpretation is that perioperative synbiotic protocols are among the most clinically plausible microbiome strategies, but implementation should still be institution-specific and evidence-based rather than based on generic supplement use.

### 2. Chemotherapy: Toxicity Reduction Is More Established Than Efficacy Enhancement

Chemotherapy changes the microbiota through mucosal injury, altered diet, antiemetics, antibiotics, proton-pump inhibitors, diarrhea, and inflammation. Conversely, the microbiota can influence chemotherapy through drug metabolism, immune modulation, and barrier integrity.

The 2024 systematic review found **7 RCTs** evaluating pre-, pro-, or synbiotic supplementation in CRC chemotherapy settings. Some trials reported clinically meaningful reductions in severe diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, hospital care use, and chemotherapy dose reductions due to bowel toxicity. These outcomes matter because maintaining dose intensity is important in curative-intent adjuvant therapy and disease-control settings ([Moreira et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)).

Yet the evidence was mixed. Kefir, for example, did not clearly reduce mucositis or gastrointestinal complaints in CRC chemotherapy trials reviewed by Moreira et al. This illustrates a critical principle: microbiota interventions are not interchangeable. A fermented food, a commercial probiotic capsule, a synbiotic formula, and a rationally selected live biotherapeutic product may have very different effects.

Mechanistically, chemotherapy-induced mucositis involves inflammation, epithelial injury, barrier dysfunction, and apoptosis. Selected probiotics or their supernatants have been shown in preclinical and clinical contexts to reduce diarrhea and weight loss, restore barrier integrity, and suppress inflammation ([Wong & Yu, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)). Still, by September 2024, the strongest defensible conclusion was that microbiota modulation may reduce **toxicity**, while evidence that it improves **tumor response, progression-free survival, or overall survival** remained insufficient.

### 3. Immunotherapy: Strong Biology, Limited CRC-Specific Intervention Evidence

Immunotherapy is most relevant in CRC for dMMR/MSI-H tumors, where immune checkpoint inhibitors can produce durable responses. The microbiota is highly relevant because checkpoint blockade depends on immune priming, antigen presentation, cytokine tone, and T-cell infiltration—all processes influenced by commensal microbes and microbial metabolites.

Preclinical work had already shown that antibiotic depletion of gut microbiota could impair checkpoint inhibitor efficacy in models including CRC, while selected bacteria such as *Bacteroides*, *Bifidobacterium*, and *Akkermansia muciniphila* were associated with improved immune responses in broader oncology literature ([Wong & Yu, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1); [Frontiers in Immunology, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2024.1471273/full)).

However, the 2024 CRC-focused systematic review found **no RCTs** of prebiotics, probiotics, or synbiotics in CRC patients receiving immunotherapy ([Moreira et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). This gap is important. The microbiome–immunotherapy connection is one of the most exciting research branches, but for CRC it was still not ready for routine microbiome-directed prescribing by September 2024.

My opinion is that microbiome profiling may eventually help identify CRC patients likely to benefit from immunotherapy, especially when integrated with MSI/MMR status, tumor immune contexture, antibiotic exposure, diet, and metabolomics. But in 2024, clinical decisions should still be driven primarily by validated biomarkers such as MMR/MSI status, tumor stage, molecular profile, and guideline-supported regimens.

### 4. Radiotherapy and Rectal Cancer Chemoradiotherapy: Promising but Early

Radiotherapy is especially relevant for rectal cancer. Pelvic radiation can damage intestinal mucosa, producing diarrhea, pain, inflammation, and barrier disruption. The gut microbiota may either worsen or mitigate these toxicities. Some microbes may contribute to inflammatory injury, while others may support epithelial repair and immune balance.

A 2023 review focused on CRC radio- and immunotherapy concluded that the microbiome appears to influence both toxicity and efficacy, and that FMT has been explored as a way to reduce treatment-related toxicity and improve response. However, the authors emphasized that available CRC data were still early, with much evidence coming from preclinical or limited clinical settings ([Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2023.1298264/full)).

The 2024 systematic review found only **one study** involving chemoradiotherapy among CRC patients receiving pre-, pro-, or synbiotic supplementation ([Moreira et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). Therefore, while radiotherapy–microbiome research is mechanistically compelling, it lagged behind surgery and chemotherapy in clinical maturity.

## Future Outlook: What Should Be Prioritized?

### Probiotics and Prebiotics

The future should not be “more probiotics” in a generic sense. It should be **precision microbiome intervention**. That means trials must define:

- strain identity,
- dose,
- viability,
- treatment timing,
- target mechanism,
- patient subgroup,
- baseline microbiome,
- antibiotic exposure,
- diet,
- endpoints beyond symptoms,
- and safety monitoring.

The 2020 Oncogene review noted that probiotics and prebiotics can plausibly reduce inflammation, enhance barrier function, prevent pathogen colonization, and modulate immunity, but also emphasized risk-benefit assessment and patient selection ([Wong & Yu, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)). Prevention trials showed changes in biomarkers such as inflammation and genotoxin exposure but did **not** show strong evidence of reduced tumor occurrence, indicating that mechanistic plausibility does not automatically translate into clinical cancer prevention ([Wong & Yu, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)).

### Fecal Microbiota Transplantation

FMT is more powerful but also less controlled than probiotics. It can restore microbial diversity and metabolic function, but it also introduces a complex biological product containing bacteria, viruses, fungi, metabolites, and potentially harmful genes. Before September 2024, FMT was already established for recurrent *C. difficile* infection, but its use in oncology remained experimental.

FMT’s major advantages are breadth and ecological restoration. Its major disadvantages are donor variability, safety risk, regulatory complexity, and uncertain long-term consequences. The 2020 Oncogene review highlighted FDA safety concerns after transmission of multidrug-resistant *E. coli* through donor stool preparations, including a fatal case in an immunocompromised recipient ([Wong & Yu, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)). That warning is highly relevant for CRC patients, many of whom receive cytotoxic therapy or have mucosal injury.

My position is that FMT is one of the most promising future directions, especially for immunotherapy resistance and severe treatment-related dysbiosis, but it should remain in controlled trials for CRC treatment enhancement until donor screening, product standardization, recipient selection, and efficacy endpoints are better established.

## Integrated Interpretation

Across surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy, one pattern is consistent: the microbiota most clearly affects **treatment tolerance**, while its ability to improve **tumor control** is plausible but less proven in humans.

The strongest CRC-specific evidence through September 2024 supports microbiota modulation as an adjunct to reduce perioperative and gastrointestinal complications. Chemotherapy data support a potential role in reducing diarrhea and bowel toxicity, but products and outcomes vary. Immunotherapy and radiotherapy have strong mechanistic support, but CRC-specific interventional evidence remains early.

A clinically useful framework is:

1. **Do not damage the microbiota unnecessarily.** Avoid nonessential antibiotics, especially around immunotherapy, and manage diarrhea and mucositis early.  
2. **Use microbiota support where evidence is strongest.** Perioperative synbiotics/probiotics may be reasonable in selected institutional protocols.  
3. **Do not overgeneralize probiotic claims.** Strain specificity and patient selection are essential.  
4. **Treat FMT as investigational in CRC oncology.** It is promising but not yet routine for improving CRC treatment efficacy.  
5. **Move toward functional biomarkers.** Future diagnostics should assess not only taxa but also microbial genes, metabolites, and immune effects.

The most defensible conclusion is that gut microbiota is already a meaningful factor in CRC treatment, but microbiome-directed therapy had not yet reached the level of routine precision oncology by September 2024. Its near-term clinical role is supportive-care optimization; its long-term potential lies in biomarker-guided, mechanism-based combination therapy.
