# The LiDAR-First vs. Vision-First Divide in Autonomous Driving

The autonomous vehicle industry has not agreed on a single perception stack. Two camps, one built around active laser ranging and the other around passive cameras and large neural networks, run side by side, each shipping commercial pilots or consumer products. The split is sometimes framed as a technology choice, but it is more accurately a cluster of linked choices about sensor physics, hardware cost, AI architecture, business model, and what "safe" is allowed to mean. What follows walks through the underlying factors driving each side, then looks at the 2025–2026 evidence that the camps are diverging further rather than converging.

## Sensor modalities and the perception problem

Every autonomous vehicle needs to answer three questions about the world around it: where each object is, what it is, and how fast it is moving. The two camps simply disagree about which sensor class can best answer all three.

LiDAR is an active sensor: it emits laser pulses (typically at 905 nm or 1550 nm) and measures round-trip time to build a three-dimensional point cloud. Its strength is direct geometric measurement. The Hesai AT128, a widely deployed automotive unit, returns centimeter-accurate distance at roughly 200 m for low-reflectivity targets, independent of ambient light [1]. Because the 1550 nm wavelength is absorbed by the eye's outer tissues rather than reaching the retina, it can legally emit more power and reach farther than the cheaper 905 nm variants, but the detector technology is more expensive [2]. LiDAR does not read color, cannot on its own distinguish a plastic bag from a child's torso, and its pulses scatter in heavy precipitation; Waymo's engineering team has publicly described fog and snow as conditions where LiDAR "still has to fight to keep up" [3].

Cameras are passive: they capture ambient light and return color, texture, and text. That is exactly the information a human driver uses to read signs, judge lane markings, and interpret a pedestrian's gesture. The cost is severe limitations in distance estimation (2D images must be projected into 3D), in low-light operation, and in direct-glare conditions. In a 2024 Phoenix dust storm (a haboob), a vision-only vehicle had difficulty distinguishing the wall of dust from a solid barrier; multi-sensor vehicles in the same conditions still resolved road edges using radar penetration and LiDAR returns through the lighter dust layers [3].

Radar completes the standard triad. It sends radio waves (typically 77 GHz), returns velocity directly through the Doppler shift, and is largely unaffected by rain, fog, or dust. Its resolution is far coarser than LiDAR's, and it has well-known trouble distinguishing stationary objects from overhead infrastructure, the kind of failure that contributed to a multi-year NHTSA investigation of Tesla Autopilot after a series of crashes against stopped fire trucks on US highways [4].

No single modality is strictly best across all conditions. The split between the camps is therefore not about which sensor "wins." It is about which failure mode each side is willing to accept, and which they expect software to compensate for.

## Cost economics and the hardware bill of materials

The cost gap between the two architectures has been the single most important commercial fact of the past decade, and it has shrunk dramatically.

In 2017, a single mechanical LiDAR unit (the Velodyne HDL-64E used by early robotaxi prototypes) cost roughly $75,000 [5]. By 2025–2026, solid-state units from Chinese suppliers were selling for $200–500 in volume, and Luminar's Iris+ sat at $500–1,000; Hesai shipped 1.62 million units in 2025 alone, with the AT128 priced around $500 [6][7]. Industry analysts expect $300–800 per unit by 2028 as manufacturing scales further [8].

That cost collapse changes the math. For a consumer automaker shipping several million cars a year, even a $500 sensor per car represents a multi-billion-dollar bill of materials. Tesla's HW4 platform, by contrast, runs on 8 cameras and no other perception sensors, with the camera array estimated at roughly $300–400 per vehicle [9]. Waymo's fifth-generation sensor suite (cameras, LiDAR, and radar combined) was estimated at about $12,700 per vehicle as of 2024; its sixth-generation system is more compact and substantially cheaper, but the bill is still in the low five figures [10].

The cost gap matters because it determines which deployment model the architecture can support. A robotaxi with five or more LiDAR units and $20,000+ in sensors can be amortized across a decade of 24/7 service. A consumer car with a $500 sensor per unit becomes a $500 million annual line item at Tesla's production volume, and a comparable cost on a $20,000 economy car is unworkable. LiDAR's price collapse has narrowed the gap but not closed it.

## The 2020–2024 AI architecture turn

The second major factor is the deep-learning architecture that turns camera frames into driving decisions. This is what made pure-vision plausible at all.

The 2D convolutional networks used in early Autopilot (circa 2018) struggled with depth estimation, occluded objects, and unusual weather. Three architecture changes, layered over four years, turned passive cameras into something closer to a geometric sensor.

First, BEV (Bird's Eye View) plus Transformer, introduced around 2020 and now adopted industry-wide, projects multi-camera image features into a unified top-down coordinate frame using self-attention. This solves the problem of cameras "seeing" the world from disjoint angles and produces a coherent, 3D-like scene representation from pure image data [11].

Second, the Occupancy Network, which Tesla introduced in 2022 and which Xpeng, Jiyue, and others have since adopted, divides the space around the vehicle into 3D voxels (small cubes, typically 10–33 cm per side) and predicts which voxels are occupied. The model does not need to recognize what an object is; it only needs to know whether the voxel is free. This is the same information LiDAR returns, recovered from cameras [12].

Third, FSD V12 in early 2024 marked the move to a true end-to-end neural network. The previous modular stack (perception → prediction → planning → control, with hundreds of thousands of lines of C++ rules) collapsed into a single network that ingests camera pixels and outputs steering, throttle, and brake. Tesla reported that more than 300,000 lines of hand-written control code dropped by roughly two orders of magnitude, and the team is now shipping V14, a multi-modal architecture that processes video, navigation, kinematics, and audio in a single forward pass [13][14].

The cumulative effect: by 2025, a sufficiently large neural network could, from camera input alone, recover roughly the same 3D occupancy grid that LiDAR returns. The "human analogy" used by Tesla — humans drive with eyes only, so a neural net should too — sounds philosophical, but it points at a real engineering fact: vision is no longer strictly inferior to LiDAR at the geometric-perception task. The question becomes which error patterns each approach leaves behind, and whether those error patterns are tolerable.

## Robotaxi and consumer-vehicle economics

The LiDAR-first and vision-first camps also reflect two very different business models, and the difference is sharper than the technology debate suggests.

Waymo, the most visible LiDAR-first operator, runs a robotaxi service. By August 2026 the company reported more than 200 million fully autonomous miles across Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Austin, and other cities [15]. A relatively small fleet of vehicles (on the order of 2,500) serves paying passengers in defined urban zones, with high per-vehicle utilization and the ability to absorb $20,000+ in sensor and compute hardware. The service does not need to be cheap; it needs to be safer than a human-driven taxi and to pass regulatory scrutiny. Waymo's published safety data, an 88% reduction in property-damage claims and a 92% reduction in bodily-injury claims across 25.3 million miles in a 2024 Swiss Re reinsurance study, supports the case [16].

Tesla runs an installed-base business. The company has shipped roughly six million vehicles with cameras, and by early 2026 its supervised FSD had logged 8.2 billion miles of driving data [17]. The marginal cost of adding camera-based autonomy to each new car is small; the marginal cost of adding a $1,000 LiDAR is not. The commercial logic is to scale the data flywheel by selling the cars first, then monetize autonomy through software subscriptions and (eventually) a robotaxi service. Tesla's Austin robotaxi pilot, with about 573 Model Y vehicles by April 2026, is the first test of whether the data scale can carry the same regulatory and safety burden Waymo carries [18].

These two economics imply different sensor strategies. A robotaxi can carry a $20,000 sensor stack because the stack is amortized across thousands of revenue-generating hours per vehicle per year. A consumer car cannot. This is also why Mercedes' Drive Pilot, the only L3-certified production system in the US, requires a roof-mounted Valeo SCALA 3 LiDAR and centimeter-precision HD maps: the liability transfer that L3 represents is only acceptable with redundant physical sensing, and Mercedes is willing to charge a $2,500 hardware option on a $120,000 S-Class to make that work [19].

## Safety philosophy and the human analogy

The two camps also hold different beliefs about what safety is, and how it should be demonstrated.

The LiDAR-first position, articulated most directly by Waymo co-CEO Dmitri Dolgov in 2026, holds that "safe, fully autonomous operations at scale require more" than cameras alone; each sensor modality has a different failure profile, and combining them creates overlapping coverage so no single point of sensor failure can create a dangerous blind spot [15]. Waymo's AI lead Vincent Vanhoucke has described sensor fusion not as a vote between competing inputs but as a Bayesian merging of slightly conflicting evidence, analogous to how the brain fuses two eyes' slightly different views of the same scene [3].

The vision-first position, articulated by Elon Musk, Andrej Karpathy, and now He Xiaopeng of Xpeng, holds that a sufficiently capable neural network can extract the same information from cameras that LiDAR measures directly. The argument is grounded in the observation that humans drive well with two eyes and a brain, and that with enough data and compute, the same outcome is achievable with silicon. Musk has called LiDAR a "crutch" repeatedly since 2019 [20].

The "human analogy" is misleading in one important respect, and the LiDAR-first side is right to flag it. Human drivers use vestibular input, proprioception, decades of embodied experience with physics and other road users, and a cognitive model of the world that no current AI system has. Cameras in a car do not reproduce that whole stack; they reproduce only the visual part of it.

The 2025–2026 evidence cuts both ways. Waymo's 200 million-plus miles of driverless operation show that the multi-sensor approach is delivering on safety under defined conditions; an independent 2025 paper in Traffic Injury Prevention covering 56.7 million rider-only Waymo miles found statistically significant reductions in injury-related crashes across every crash category examined [21]. Tesla's Austin robotaxi pilot, by contrast, accumulated 14 reported crashes in its first 800,000 miles, roughly four times the human-driver rate by Tesla's own data, and the FSD system in 2.88 million consumer vehicles is the subject of an active NHTSA investigation opened in October 2025 [22]. The investigation is not a finding of defect, but it is also not the safety record the vision-first camp was promising. Comma.ai, the open-source vision-only aftermarket driver-assist system, faces a parallel NHTSA probe opened in September 2026 after five crashes, three fatalities, and eleven serious injuries, with stopped or slow-moving vehicles as the recurring failure pattern [23].

## 2025–2026 inflection points

Three industry events in 2025–2026 sharpen, rather than resolve, the divide.

First, the collapse of Volvo's LiDAR program. Volvo had equipped its flagship EX90 and ES90 with Luminar Iris LiDAR, but the partnership was terminated in November 2025 after Volvo determined the sensor's data volume exceeded its central compute platform's capacity and the promised features could not ship. Luminar filed for Chapter 11 in December 2025 and entered liquidation in April 2026; Volvo is now compensating owners of LiDAR-equipped cars in multiple countries for the unused hardware [24]. This is the first major Western OEM to publicly retreat from a LiDAR commitment.

Second, Xpeng's full conversion to vision-only. Following an October 2024 announcement, Xpeng removed LiDAR from all in-sale models by 2026 and is now scaling a second-generation VLA (vision-language-action) model with 72 billion parameters and 2,250 TOPS of in-house Turing chip compute, targeting Level 4 capability without LiDAR or HD maps [25]. He Xiaopeng has stated publicly that LiDAR is "no longer necessary" for the consumer-vehicle segment [26].

Third, Waymo's 200-million-mile milestone and continued expansion. By August 2026 Waymo had logged more than 200 million fully autonomous miles and continues to publish peer-reviewed safety data [15]. The multi-sensor thesis is not weakening in the robotaxi segment; it is strengthening.

Meanwhile, the Chinese supplier base has consolidated. Hesai and Huawei together held roughly 67% of China's main-LiDAR market in Q1 2026, with Hesai alone at 41–55% by different cuts of the data [27]. RoboSense and Seyond are second-tier. The 1.62 million LiDAR units Hesai shipped in 2025 went largely into ADAS-equipped production cars priced as low as 80,000 yuan, suggesting that the cost-down trajectory continues even as some Western OEMs walk back their LiDAR plans.

## Trajectory

The most likely outcome over the next three to five years is not convergence on a single architecture, but a sharper split along deployment lines.

Robotaxi services operating at SAE Level 4 in defined urban zones will continue to favor multi-sensor stacks. Regulatory approval for a driverless commercial service still depends on demonstrable redundancy, and LiDAR remains the easiest way to provide depth perception that is independent of lighting and weather. The Waymo template, with HD maps, multi-sensor fusion, and geofencing, is likely to remain the standard for L4 commercial service for the foreseeable future.

Consumer vehicles, sold in millions per year, will continue migrating toward vision-only or vision-primary architectures, with LiDAR retained as a premium safety add-on for flagship models and Level 3 highway features. The cost arithmetic is unforgiving, and Tesla's installed base plus Xpeng's Chinese-market validation are removing the perception that pure vision is an unproven bet.

The middle ground is a hybrid. Mobileye's "True Redundancy" approach runs two independent perception pipelines, a camera-only stack and a radar/LiDAR stack, so that any single failure mode does not take down the system [28]. Mercedes' Drive Pilot uses LiDAR for its certified L3 feature. NIO, Xiaomi, and Li Auto keep LiDAR on their premium trims while building vision-only stacks for mass-market cars. The era when every car has the same perception stack is ending; the era of use-case-specific perception stacks is beginning.

Whether the LiDAR-first or vision-first camp ultimately prevails depends on a question neither side can answer yet: whether neural networks can be trusted, after tens of billions of miles, to handle the long tail of edge cases that humans handle without thinking, and whether regulatory and liability systems will accept that trust. The current evidence says the multi-sensor camp is closer to passing that test for L4 commercial service, and the vision-only camp is closer to passing it for L2/L2+ consumer deployment. Neither has closed the gap on the other's home turf.
