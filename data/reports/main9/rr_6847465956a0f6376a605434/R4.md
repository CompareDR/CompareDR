# Why Some Autonomous Vehicles Prioritize LiDAR and Others Rely on Neural Networks and Cameras

The divide is not “LiDAR versus cameras” but a disagreement about the source of 3D geometry. LiDAR-first stacks treat position and shape as a **measured** quantity produced by active ranging; vision-first stacks treat 3D as an **inferred** quantity produced by deep networks from 2D pixels. Both camps run neural networks—LiDAR-first systems use them on point clouds and camera-LiDAR fusion—so the real line is active ranging versus learned inference. That choice cascades into the operating design domain (ODD), validation philosophy, cost structure, and liability allocation.

## Three positions on the spectrum

The camps differ in which sensor is load-bearing for 3D geometry.

1. **LiDAR-centric fusion.** LiDAR supplies 3D position and shape; cameras add semantic classification; radar adds direct velocity and all-weather range. The safety case is heterogeneous redundancy: independent modalities so that no single sensor’s failure mode is fatal. Waymo’s fifth-generation Driver carries 29 cameras, 5 LiDARs, and 6 radars; the sixth-generation suite cuts this to 13 cameras, 4 LiDARs, and 6 radars while preserving overlapping 360° fields of view out to 500 m in darkness or poor weather ([Waymo 5th-gen blog](https://waymo.com/blog/2020/03/introducing-5th-generation-waymo-driver); [Waymo 6th-gen blog](https://waymo.com/blog/2024/08/meet-the-6th-generation-waymo-driver)). Aurora, Baidu Apollo, Pony.ai, WeRide, Zoox, and Motional occupy the same camp.

2. **Camera-first with optional LiDAR/radar.** Cameras are load-bearing; LiDAR and radar are added for redundancy or higher automation levels. Mobileye’s True Redundancy is the clearest example: two independent standalone systems—a camera-only system and a radar-LiDAR system—each builds a full world model, so either can drive the vehicle alone ([Mobileye True Redundancy](https://www.mobileye.com/technology/true-redundancy/)). NVIDIA’s Hyperion 7.1 reference architecture ships as a Level 2+ camera+radar suite (7 cameras, 8 radars, IMU/GPS) with LiDAR added through the DriveWorks sensor-abstraction layer ([NVIDIA Hyperion 7.1](https://developer.nvidia.com/drive/hyperion-7.1)).

3. **Camera-only end-to-end.** Cameras are the sole load-bearing sensor; a single neural network maps pixels to planning. Tesla’s FSD is the prominent example: an 8-camera suite with radar and ultrasonic sensors removed, pursuing an end-to-end neural architecture ([thinkautonomous.ai](https://www.thinkautonomous.ai/blog/tesla-vs-waymo-two-opposite-visions/)). The label is a tendency, not a fixed commitment: Tesla re-added a radar in Hardware 4 (2023), confirmed by teardown and FCC documentation, though its integration into the FSD stack was not confirmed at the time ([TeslaOracle, 2023](https://www.teslaoracle.com/2023/06/19/tesla-teardown-confirms-the-presence-of-the-new-radar-in-hw4-equipped-vehicles/)).

“LiDAR versus cameras” is therefore a false dichotomy: both camps use cameras and neural networks. “Neural networks are exclusive to vision” is false: LiDAR-first stacks use deep networks for point-cloud detection and camera-LiDAR fusion. The camps also exchange methods. Waymo’s EMMA is an end-to-end multimodal model that processes raw camera inputs and text to output planner trajectories, perception objects, and road-graph elements, and it does not use LiDAR or radar; Waymo reports positive task transfer across planning, detection, and road-graph tasks ([Waymo EMMA](https://waymo.com/blog/2024/10/introducing-emma)). But EMMA is research, not the production Waymo Driver, which remains LiDAR-first; Waymo itself flags EMMA’s lack of LiDAR/radar inputs, long-term video limits, and verification challenges. The production systems remain distinct even as the methods converge.

## Cost history created the split

At the 2007 DARPA Urban Challenge, five of six finishers used lidar, most using Velodyne’s HDL-64E, a 64-laser spinning unit that cost about $75,000, weighed 12.7 kg, and output ~2.2 million points per second ([lidarnews](https://lidarnews.com/automotive-lidar-cost-history-75000-to-200/); [DARPA](https://www.darpa.mil/node/2028)). That price was acceptable for a prize competition and well-funded research fleets, so lidar-first became the default architecture for the L4 industry that grew out of DARPA. Google’s Self-Driving Car Project, begun in 2009, bought the same HDL-64E; an early Prius test car carried ~$150,000 of extra equipment, the lidar its biggest component ([ars technica, 2017](https://arstechnica.com/cars/2017/01/googles-waymo-invests-in-lidar-technology-cuts-costs-by-90-percent/)). The project became Waymo in December 2016 ([Waymo blog, 2016](https://waymo.com/blog/2016/12/say-hello-to-waymo-whats-next-for)). Waymo began attacking the cost early: by January 2017 it announced a ~90% reduction in lidar cost by developing its own units ([ars technica, 2017](https://arstechnica.com/cars/2017/01/googles-waymo-invests-in-lidar-technology-cuts-costs-by-90-percent/)).

Tesla’s path diverged because it was building a consumer car, not a prize vehicle or robotaxi. Hardware 1 (2014) used a Mobileye EyeQ3 with a single camera plus radar and ultrasonics; Mobileye ended the partnership in 2016, citing safety concerns, and Tesla moved to an NVIDIA Drive PX2-based Hardware 2 with eight surround cameras ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_Autopilot_hardware); [Not a Tesla App](https://www.notateslaapp.com/news/3521/how-teslas-fsd-hardware-has-changed-over-the-years)). In 2019 Tesla replaced NVIDIA with its own Hardware 3 “FSD Computer” (144 TOPS) and began dropping radar; in 2021 it stopped installing radar in new vehicles and shifted Model 3/Y to Tesla Vision, followed by Model S/X in 2022 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_Autopilot_hardware); [IEEE Spectrum, 2021](https://spectrum.ieee.org/tesla-places-big-bet-vision-only-self-driving)). In October 2022 it removed ultrasonic sensors from Model 3/Y, replacing them with a vision-based occupancy network ([Autoweek, 2022](https://www.autoweek.com/news/technology/a41544080/tesla-drops-ultrasonic-sensors-autopilot/)). The stated rationale combined a cost argument—Elon Musk’s long-standing position that lidar is unnecessarily expensive—with a technical claim from AI chief Andrej Karpathy that after training on more than 1.5 petabytes of video the vision-only system outperformed its radar-fused predecessor, and a data-scale argument that cheap cameras on hundreds of thousands of cars would generate more real-world data than a small lidar fleet ([IEEE Spectrum, 2021](https://spectrum.ieee.org/tesla-places-big-bet-vision-only-self-driving)). As Cornell’s Kilian Weinberger put it, “cameras are dirt cheap compared to lidar,” so the technology can be put into every car sold ([IEEE Spectrum, 2021](https://spectrum.ieee.org/tesla-places-big-bet-vision-only-self-driving)).

At $200–$500, lidar is no longer “more than the car,” so the original economic argument for camera-only weakens for consumer ADAS—even though Tesla has not adopted lidar. Conversely, lidar-first players have not abandoned lidar; they have added cameras and end-to-end neural models and reduced sensor counts. The split is best understood as a cost-and-availability path dependency now being renegotiated by the lidar cost curve, not a fixed methodological divide.

## Sensor physics: what each modality measures and where it fails

The divide is a bet about which physical gap to engineer around. LiDAR measures metric 3D geometry directly by active ranging but carries no color or semantic content; cameras measure rich 2D semantics and texture but must infer depth indirectly; radar measures Doppler velocity and all-weather range directly but with coarse angular resolution. No single modality simultaneously delivers metric depth, low-light operation, adverse-weather robustness, and semantic classification—which is why sensor fusion is the physical baseline, and why the two camps differ in which gap they accept as their engineering problem.

**LiDAR** is an active optical sensor. The two dominant ranging principles are direct time-of-flight (dToF), which times a short pulse’s round trip, and frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW), which chirps a continuous beam and coherently mixes the return with a local oscillator ([Vik’s Newsletter, 2024](https://www.viksnewsletter.com/p/short-intro-to-automotive-lidar); [indie Semiconductor, 2023](https://www.indie.inc/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/IND-0124-Microtech-Ventures-Whitepaper-LiDAR.pdf)). dToF is simpler and cheaper; FMCW adds direct per-pixel Doppler velocity and better interference immunity but requires more signal processing and a tunable single-mode laser. The physical payoff is metric depth: distance derives from the speed of light and a measured time or frequency shift, producing a dense 3D point cloud with centimetre-level range accuracy ([Hamamatsu, 2017](https://hub.hamamatsu.com/us/en/application-notes/automotive/lidar-a-photonics-guide-to-the-autonomous-vehicle-market.html)). That geometry works in total darkness and bright sun because the sensor supplies its own illumination ([Outsight](https://www.outsight.ai/insights/how-does-lidar-compares-to-cameras-and-radars/)). Wavelength is a tradeoff: 905 nm uses inexpensive silicon photodetectors but sits near the visible band where eye-safety limits cap pulse energy; 1550 nm is retina-safe and allows longer range but requires costlier InGaAs detectors and suffers stronger water-vapor absorption in rain and fog ([Hamamatsu, 2017](https://hub.hamamatsu.com/us/en/application-notes/automotive/lidar-a-photonics-guide-to-the-autonomous-vehicle-market.html)). LiDAR’s physical limits are real but narrower than the marketing: it has lower angular resolution than a camera for fine classification because it returns geometry, not texture or color; it scatters in fog, rain, and snow; and it cannot read traffic lights, lane paint, or brake lights ([Outsight](https://www.outsight.ai/insights/how-does-lidar-compares-to-cameras-and-radars/); [Hamamatsu, 2017](https://hub.hamamatsu.com/us/en/application-notes/automotive/lidar-a-photonics-guide-to-the-autonomous-vehicle-market.html)).

**Cameras** are passive: they detect ambient light reflected from the scene. They produce high-resolution 2D images rich in color, texture, and semantic detail, which is why they excel at classification, lane detection, and traffic-sign reading. But a single 2D image has no direct metric depth: a large distant object and a small near object can project to the same pixel footprint ([Outsight](https://www.outsight.ai/insights/how-does-lidar-compares-to-cameras-and-radars/)). Stereo depth triangulates from a known baseline but degrades with range and is fundamentally baseline-limited; monocular depth is cheaper and more scalable but cannot recover absolute scale without a known reference, and the peer-reviewed literature finds the gap to stereo remains large at the ~100 m ranges needed for highway-speed obstacle detection ([arXiv, 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/1803.09719v4)). Cameras also degrade or fail in darkness, glare, and shadow transitions because they depend on ambient light ([Outsight](https://www.outsight.ai/insights/how-does-lidar-compares-to-cameras-and-radars/)).

**Radar** is an active radio sensor, typically 76–81 GHz. Its defining physical quantity is Doppler velocity: because it measures the frequency shift of the return, it reads an object’s speed and direction directly and instantaneously, even in rain, fog, and snow, where its long wavelength scatters far less than light ([Waymo, 2020](https://waymo.com/blog/2020/03/introducing-5th-generation-waymo-driver); [arXiv radar review, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09304v3)). The physical cost is angular resolution: the long wavelength relative to aperture produces large beam divergence, so conventional radar cannot finely distinguish adjacent objects ([Hamamatsu, 2017](https://hub.hamamatsu.com/us/en/application-notes/automotive/lidar-a-photonics-guide-to-the-autonomous-vehicle-market.html)). 4D imaging radar attacks this with massive MIMO antenna arrays and digital beamforming in elevation as well as azimuth, producing dense point clouds; Continental’s ARS540 measures range, azimuth, elevation, and relative speed at 300 m with 192 virtual channels ([Xilinx/Continental, 2020](https://www.xilinx.com/publications/presentations/continental-ARS540-powered-by-xilinx.pdf)). This narrows but does not close the gap: 4D radar remains coarser than LiDAR in angular resolution and far coarser than cameras in semantic detail.

Several commonly cited weaknesses are engineering, not physics. LiDAR cost, size, and mechanical wear are engineering and manufacturing problems that solid-state architectures and Chinese volume production are attacking. Radar’s low resolution is partly physics (wavelength) and partly engineering (antenna count and processing). Camera depth error is physics (no direct metric measurement) compounded by algorithm choice. Conversely, LiDAR’s darkness capability and radar’s all-weather Doppler are physical advantages that engineering can erode but not erase. The practical consequence: the camps are not choosing between a physically correct and a physically wrong sensor, but between which physical gap is cheaper to engineer around at their target price and ODD.

## Why LiDAR-first: independent geometry and a deterministic safety case

LiDAR’s safety value is not that it sees better than cameras in every condition, but that it supplies independent, metric 3D geometry that covers failure modes cameras cannot, and that this determinism maps more cleanly onto functional-safety validation. That value is real but bounded: LiDAR alone is not sufficient, it degrades in adverse weather, and its angular resolution is below cameras for fine classification.

| Failure mode | LiDAR | Camera | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unclassified / static object | Unique: metric 3D detection independent of class | Weak: depth inferred; unknown objects may be missed | [Waymo Driver](https://waymo.com/waymo-driver/) |
| Low-light / headlight-free | Unique: active ranging works in darkness | Weak: passive, needs light | [Waymo Driver](https://waymo.com/waymo-driver/) |
| Glare / shadow transition | Strong: active, less illumination-dependent | Weak: HDR required; transient saturation | [Waymo Driver](https://waymo.com/waymo-driver/) |
| Cut-in / occlusion | Strong: 3D geometry supports spatial reasoning | Weak: 2D projection loses depth | [Waymo Driver](https://waymo.com/waymo-driver/) |
| Free-space estimation | Strong: direct 3D occupancy | Inferred from semantics | [Waymo Driver](https://waymo.com/waymo-driver/) |
| Fine classification | Weak: lower angular resolution | Strong: high resolution and texture | [PointPainting](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.10150) |
| Adverse weather (fog/rain/snow) | Weak: scatter degrades point cloud | Weak: degradation; radar complements | [Sezgin et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.01336v1) |

The LiDAR-first argument is that heterogeneous sensors have different failure modes, so a single sensor’s weakness is not fatal. Waymo’s stack uses LiDAR for 3D geometry, cameras for 360° semantics, and radar for distance/speed and all-weather operation, explicitly noting that “radar is effective in rain, fog, and snow” ([Waymo Driver](https://waymo.com/waymo-driver/)). The limits are equally concrete. First, LiDAR’s angular resolution is below cameras for fine classification: PointPainting shows that projecting LiDAR points into an image semantic-segmentation network and appending class scores improves three LiDAR-only detectors on KITTI and nuScenes, with the painted PointPillars+ improving all 10 nuScenes classes for a 6.3 mAP gain ([PointPainting](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.10150)). Camera semantics add value to LiDAR. Second, LiDAR is not immune to weather: a controlled study testing radar, LiDAR, and camera under rain (16–98 mm/h) and fog found that LiDAR point intensity and variance decrease as weather intensifies, camera pixel-intensity variance increases, and radar is less attenuated ([Sezgin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.01336v1)). Radar, not LiDAR, is the all-weather backbone. Third, LiDAR shifts cost to the sensor BOM, power draw, and calibration burden, and raises mechanical-wear concerns; UL 4740 now exists specifically to evaluate LiDAR durability and contamination protection ([UL Standards & Engagement](https://ulse.org/focus-areas/travel-safety/autonomous-vehicles/)).

The deeper argument is epistemological: deterministic, auditable 3D occupancy is easier to verify than statistical perception. A point cloud either contains an obstacle at a metric location or it does not; a neural network’s confidence that a blob is a pedestrian is a statistical claim requiring a different validation methodology. This is why LiDAR-first stacks align naturally with the functional-safety framework. UL 4600, the Standard for Safety for Evaluation of Autonomous Products, explicitly “does not mandate the use of any specific technology... and also permits design process flexibility” ([UL Standards & Engagement](https://ulse.org/focus-areas/travel-safety/autonomous-vehicles/)). The standards framework evaluates the safety case, not the sensor modality: camera-only systems can in principle meet the same expectations, but must construct a statistical-perception safety argument rather than a deterministic-occupancy one. Mercedes-Benz DRIVE PILOT became the first SAE L3 system certified in the US, with Nevada the first state to confirm compliance; it is widely reported to use LiDAR in its sensor suite, though the fetched official page confirms the L3 milestone but does not enumerate the sensors ([Mercedes-Benz](https://group.mercedes-benz.com/technology/autonomous-driving/driving/drive-pilot-nevada.html)). The case nonetheless demonstrates that a LiDAR-equipped consumer system can clear a state L3 certification.

The March 18, 2018 Tempe, Arizona fatal crash is the canonical warning that LiDAR detection is not sufficient. The NTSB report on the Uber ATG test vehicle documents that the system detected the pedestrian but oscillated among classifications—“unknown,” “vehicle,” “bicycle”—and that braking was suppressed by software ([NTSB HAR-19/03](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/accidentreports/reports/har1903.pdf)). The ranging sensor worked; the classification and decision layers did not act. The redundancy argument must extend beyond sensors to the software that consumes them.

Waymo’s strategy illustrates the redundancy logic at scale. The fifth-generation Driver carries 29 cameras, 5 LiDARs, and 6 radars; the sixth-generation suite cuts this to 13 cameras, 4 LiDARs, and 6 radars—a ~42% sensor-count reduction that preserves redundancy while cutting cost ([Automotive Dive, 2024](https://www.automotivedive.com/news/waymo-6th-generation-driver-autonomous-driving-hardware-robotaxi-lidar-ai/725519/)). Waymo designs its lidar in-house, citing cost control, supply security, IP ownership, and co-design of the sensor with the calibration and fusion stack ([Waymo 5th-gen blog](https://waymo.com/blog/2020/03/introducing-5th-generation-waymo-driver)). The company manages validation through module- and system-level tests, hazard analysis, reliability and durability testing, and over 40,000 closed-course scenarios plus thousands of crash-avoidance tests ([Waymo Driver](https://waymo.com/waymo-driver/)). Waymo does not publish per-vehicle calibration time or recalibration intervals, so the specific calibration burden of a 40-sensor stack is not quantified in primary sources.

The rest of the LiDAR-first field converges on the same pattern. Aurora uses its FirstLight FMCW lidar as the long-range anchor, claiming >400 m range, per-point Doppler velocity, and interference immunity, with fleet equipage targeted for 2027 ([Aurora](https://aurora.tech/newsroom/firstlight-lidar-on-a-chip)). Baidu’s Apollo RT6 uses 38 sensors including 8 LiDARs and 12 cameras, with “full redundancy throughout both hardware and autonomous driving software” ([Baidu PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/baidu-unveils-next-gen-autonomous-vehicle-ready-to-provide-driverless-robotaxi-half-of-taxi-fares-301590644.html)). Pony.ai’s suite has been reported as 3 roof-mounted solid-state LiDARs, 3 blind-spot LiDARs, 11 long-range millimeter-wave radars plus cameras ([pony.ai](https://pony.ai/business?lang=en)). Zoox describes a 360-degree suite of cameras, lidar, radar, long-wave infrared, and microphones; Nuro uses solid-state LiDAR, automotive cameras, and imaging radars; Motional’s IONIQ 5 robotaxi carries “over 30 sensors” ([Zoox](https://zoox.com/know-your-ride); [Nuro](https://www.nuro.ai/nuro-driver); [Motional](https://motional.com/technology)). None publishes a simple component count comparable to Waymo’s or Baidu’s, so the census for these three is qualitative.

## Why vision+NN: fleet data and learned 3D

Tesla’s FSD architecture is a staged bet that learned inference from cameras can substitute for active 3D ranging, transferring more of the driving stack from hand-coded C++ into neural networks trained on fleet video.

| Stage | Year | What changed | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| HydraNet | 2021 | Shared-backbone multi-task perception; 8 cameras synchronized into 3D vector space; planning separate | [AI Day 2021 transcript](https://elonmuskinterviews.wordpress.com/2021/08/31/tesla-ai-day-the-presentation-i/) |
| + Occupancy Networks | 2022 | Volumetric 3D occupancy block predicting occupancy volume and flow; planning still Monte-Carlo tree search + rules | [thinkautonomous](https://www.thinkautonomous.ai/blog/tesla-end-to-end-deep-learning/) |
| End-to-end FSD v12 | 2023–2024 | City-street driving stack consolidated into a single neural network trained on millions of video clips; claimed to replace 300,000+ lines of C++ | [drivencarguide](https://www.drivencarguide.co.nz/news/tesla-rolls-out-fsd-v12-beta-will-ai-finally-nail-down-autonomous-driving/) |

HydraNet’s contribution was efficiency and shared representation: one RegNet/BiFPN backbone amortizes the forward pass across dozens of task heads, and the eight cameras are synchronized into a common 3D vector space. Occupancy Networks then added explicit geometry: instead of detecting only known object classes, the network marks each 3D voxel as free or occupied and predicts occupancy flow, which helps with occlusions, overhanging obstacles, and unclassified objects ([thinkautonomous](https://www.thinkautonomous.ai/blog/tesla-end-to-end-deep-learning/)). FSD v12’s claimed step is architectural: the network replaces the explicit C++ in the city-street stack, including behavioral responses, rather than merely feeding a separate rule-based planner ([drivencarguide](https://www.drivencarguide.co.nz/news/tesla-rolls-out-fsd-v12-beta-will-ai-finally-nail-down-autonomous-driving/)). The ~300,000-line figure originates in Tesla’s release notes and is a company claim, not an independently audited number; Tesla has not published a full architecture boundary, so how much hand-coded logic remains is not fully disclosed.

Vision-first autonomy is plausible because a layered stack converts 2D pixels into 3D scene representations. The foundational operation is lift-splat-shoot (LSS): each camera image is lifted into a frustum of features and splatted into a rasterized bird’s-eye-view (BEV) grid, trained end-to-end, robust to calibration error, and explicitly benchmarked against oracle LiDAR depth—which makes the depth-estimation step the acknowledged weak point ([Philion & Fidler, arXiv 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.05711)). BEVDet modularized this into image encoder → view transformer → BEV encoder → detection head, reporting 39.3% mAP / 47.2% NDS on nuScenes val ([Huang et al., arXiv 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2112.11790v3)). BEVFormer added spatiotemporal transformers: spatial cross-attention aggregates camera features into predefined grid-shaped BEV queries, while temporal self-attention recurrently fuses history BEV information; it reached 56.9% NDS on the nuScenes test set, described by the authors as “on par with the performance of LiDAR-based baselines,” with better velocity estimation and low-visibility recall ([Li et al., arXiv 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.17270)). PETR took a different route: position-embedding transformation encodes 3D coordinate information into image features so object queries detect directly in 3D ([Wang et al., arXiv 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.05625)).

Occupancy prediction goes beyond the BEV plane by estimating the occupancy state and semantics of every voxel. TPVFormer generalizes BEV with two perpendicular planes and a transformer encoder; trained only with sparse LiDAR semantic labels, it predicts semantic occupancy for all voxels and, per the authors, achieves “comparable performance with LiDAR-based methods on the LiDAR segmentation task on nuScenes” ([Zheng et al., arXiv 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.07817v2)). Occ3D established the standard benchmarks and explicitly labels out-of-vocabulary “General Objects” that predefined-category detectors miss ([Tian et al., arXiv 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.14365v3)). Two qualifications matter. First, occupancy networks handle unclassified obstacles by predicting free / occupied / unobserved per voxel, but they inherit the geometry uncertainty of camera inference. Second, many “vision-only” occupancy methods are camera-only at inference but LiDAR-supervised in training: TPVFormer uses sparse LiDAR semantic labels and SurroundOcc builds its dense labels from LiDAR scans ([Zheng et al., arXiv 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.07817v2); [Wei et al., arXiv 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.09551)). The “vision-only” claim is therefore about the deployed sensor suite, not the training pipeline.

End-to-end driving models have followed. UniAD unified perception, prediction, and planning into a single planning-oriented network on nuScenes ([Chen et al., arXiv 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10156)). VAD replaced dense rasterized representation with a vectorized one, cutting planning displacement error by 30.1% and collision rate by 29.0% versus UniAD while running 2.5× faster ([Jiang et al., arXiv 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.12077v3)). SparseDrive introduced a sparse-centric paradigm, reducing average L2 error by 19.4% and collision rate by 71.4% versus UniAD, while training 7.2× faster ([Sun et al., arXiv 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19620v2)). The depth-gap question is attacked directly by metric-depth foundation models:

| Model | Core mechanism | Headline claim | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Depth Anything V2 | Synthetic-image training, scaled teacher-student | >10× faster than Stable Diffusion-based models; 25M–1.3B params; metric variants via fine-tuning | [Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09414) |
| Metric3D v2 | Canonical camera-space transformation; joint depth-normal training | Zero-shot metric depth and normals; trained on 16M+ images | [Yin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15506) |

| Depth Pro | Multi-scale vision transformer; real + synthetic training; focal-length estimation | Absolute-scale depth without camera intrinsics; 2.25-MP map in 0.3 s | [Richter et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02073v2) |

These abstracts report speed, scale, and zero-shot generalization rather than a shared AbsRel / δ<1.25 figure, so cross-model accuracy ranking is not directly comparable from these sources. The strongest verified anchors are at the detection/segmentation level, not raw range error. BEVFormer’s 56.9% NDS on nuScenes test is described by its authors as on par with LiDAR baselines, while the LiDAR-only CenterPoint reports 65.5 NDS / 63.8 AMOTA on nuScenes—a residual gap of roughly 8–9 NDS points, though the two numbers come from different papers and configurations ([Li et al., arXiv 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.17270); [Yin et al., arXiv 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11275)). Against this, an LSS-lineage study states that “inaccurate depth estimation remains an important constraint” on camera-only 3D detection ([Hu et al., arXiv 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17895)). These are cross-paper, non-controlled comparisons, so they bound the gap rather than measure it precisely. The defensible reading is “vision can work,” not “vision is as accurate as LiDAR.”

The vision+NN camp’s core economic wager is that fleet scale can substitute for sensor capability. Cameras are cheap enough to embed in millions of customer vehicles, converting them into a passive data-collection network whose value compounds as the fleet grows. Tesla’s stated rationale, articulated by AI chief Andrej Karpathy, is that training on more than 1.5 petabytes of fleet video would let vision-only autonomy outpace competitors relying on a small number of sensor-laden cars driven by employees ([IEEE Spectrum, 2021](https://spectrum.ieee.org/tesla-places-big-bet-vision-only-self-driving)). The mechanism that converts this fleet into a data engine is Tesla’s “Shadow Mode”: FSD runs silently in the background even when not engaged, compares its hypothetical decisions against the driver’s actual actions, and flags disagreements as edge cases for training ([Not a Tesla App](https://www.notateslaapp.com/news/3108/teslas-fsd-shadow-mode-what-it-is-and-how-it-improves-fsd)). Tesla built Dojo—a custom supercomputer architecture based on its D1 chip—explicitly to process “millions of terabytes of video data captured from real-life driving situations from Tesla’s 4+ million cars” ([Wikipedia, Tesla Dojo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_Dojo)). Dojo entered production in July 2023 but was reported disbanded in August 2025 and restarted in January 2026 ([Wikipedia, Tesla Dojo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_Dojo)).

Empirical evidence linking data scale to performance is real but bounded. A peer-reviewed study evaluating performance across internal datasets ranging from 16 to 8,192 hours of driving data found that larger models achieve faster performance gains in the large-data regime, and that different driving scenarios require substantially different amounts of data to hit target performance thresholds—confirming that scaling helps, but not uniformly ([arXiv, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04338v1)). Critics argue the data-scaling logic faces a fundamental ceiling. ETH Zurich’s Marc Pollefeys contends that “trying to go from a system that mostly works to one that almost never makes mistakes by simply pushing ever more data through a machine learning pipeline is ‘doomed to fail’”—the long-tail problem, where “unique cases you can’t train for” eventually dominate ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/tesla-places-big-bet-vision-only-self-driving)). Weinberger adds that “the benefits of adding more and more data are diminishing at some point.” Pollefeys’s proposed mitigations are instructive: pre-mapping roads to narrow the uncertainty space, and using independent sensor modalities so that “the chance of three different sensors making the same mistake simultaneously [is] vanishingly small”—which is precisely the LiDAR-first redundancy argument.

Waymo counters not with fleet volume but with simulation at a scale no camera fleet can match in virtual mileage: more than 20 billion miles in simulation, used to replay and tweak real-world miles or build completely new virtual scenarios ([Waymo Driver](https://waymo.com/waymo-driver/)). Its latest Waymo World Model generates high-fidelity camera and LiDAR outputs for rare and safety-critical events and supports counterfactual “what-if” simulation of driving decisions ([Waymo, February 2026](https://waymo.com/blog/2026/02/the-waymo-world-model-a-new-frontier-for-autonomous-driving-simulation)). Simulation extends the fleet’s reach but does not eliminate the need for real-world data—particularly for the long-tail cases Pollefeys identifies. Waymo’s Open Dataset, with its 12 million LiDAR box annotations and deliberate geographic diversity, is a curated 3D data asset whose scale and diversity exceed what any camera-only fleet has released ([Sun et al., CVPR 2020](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2020/papers/Sun_Scalability_in_Perception_for_Autonomous_Driving_Waymo_Open_Dataset_CVPR_2020_paper.pdf)). But the denominator problem remains: Waymo’s rider-only fleet is orders of magnitude smaller than Tesla’s customer fleet, so its real-world 3D data accumulates far more slowly.

Mobileye’s Road Experience Management (REM) shows how a camera-first player can convert fleet data into a mapping moat without dedicated mapping vehicles. REM harvests anonymized, compact data (~10 kilobytes per kilometer) from consumer vehicles equipped with Mobileye’s EyeQ4 system, using on-board algorithms to identify lane markings, curbs, landmarks, and traffic signs. The system covered nearly 2.5 million kilometers of roads daily and roughly 500 million kilometers in a six-month period, with map creation, updating, and validation fully automated ([Intel/Mobileye](https://www.mobileye.com/blog/the-why-and-how-of-making-hd-maps-for-automated-vehicles/)). This is a data flywheel that supports camera-first scaling: the crowd of EyeQ-equipped vehicles generates the HD map that makes camera-first ADAS safer and more deployable, which in turn sells more EyeQ units.

## Deployment model and ODD decide the economics

The LiDAR-first vs vision+NN divide is not only a sensor-physics or software-architecture disagreement; it is a disagreement about where cost and risk should sit. Vision-first stacks push per-vehicle expense into AI compute, power draw, thermal management, and data infrastructure, betting that fleet-scale data and efficiency gains amortize it. LiDAR-first stacks keep onboard compute comparatively modest but add sensor BOM, calibration, cleaning, and fusion complexity. Which structure is economically rational is decided mainly by the deployment model: a low-utilization consumer ADAS product under OEM liability cannot absorb a large per-vehicle compute or sensor bill, whereas a geofenced robotaxi running 20+ hours a day under operator liability can amortize either one over high mileage.

The power draw materially affects EV range: the Motional Ioniq 5-based AV was EPA-rated at 168 miles, a 46% drop from the 303-mile consumer version ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/features/794319/autonomous-car-power-consumption/)). An MIT study cited in the same reporting found that at 840 W of autonomy power, one billion AVs driving one hour per day would consume as much energy as the world’s data centers did in 2023, and that in 90% of modeled scenarios each vehicle would need to stay below 1.2 kW to remain under that benchmark.

The camps also differ in less visible operating costs. A high-sensor-count stack imposes serious bandwidth and wiring demands: the Hyperion 7.1 reference specifies 90 Gb/s of camera I/O over GMSL ports and ~50 Gb/s for LiDAR/radar over Ethernet ([NVIDIA Hyperion 7.1](https://developer.nvidia.com/drive/hyperion-7.1)), and streaming multiple high-resolution camera feeds requires a data-center-class Ethernet backbone rather than legacy CAN bus ([Behind the Chip](https://behindthechip.substack.com/p/teslas-hw4)). Calibration is a recurring cost: robotaxi depot operations explicitly include sensor calibration alongside charging, cleaning, and tire changes ([Business Model Analyst](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/the-economics-of-robotaxis/)). Map upkeep is another: every new city means new HD maps for LiDAR-based operators. Waymo’s sixth-generation redesign cuts sensor count by about 42% explicitly to lower hardware complexity, energy use, and long-term maintenance costs ([Autonomous Vehicles Conference](https://www.autonomous-vehicles-conference.com/news/waymo-slims-sensors-to-drive-down-robotaxi-costs)). By contrast, vision-first shifts these burdens toward compute amortization and data infrastructure rather than per-vehicle sensor upkeep.

The decisive variable is utilization and liability. The robotaxi break-even test is cost per revenue mile below the fare per mile, at high enough utilization to pay back the hardware ([Business Model Analyst](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/the-economics-of-robotaxis/)). Waymo’s fifth-generation I-Pace retrofit was estimated at $150,000–$180,000 per vehicle; the sixth-generation Zeekr-based system is estimated at $80,000–$100,000 ([Business Model Analyst](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/the-economics-of-robotaxis/)). Goldman Sachs models depreciation falling from about $0.35/mile in 2025 to $0.14 by 2035, and remote-assistance ratios improving from one operator per ~3 cars today to 10 by 2030 and 35 by 2040 ([Business Model Analyst](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/the-economics-of-robotaxis/), citing Goldman Sachs). Because a robotaxi can be operated up to 23 hours a day with ~1 hour for charging, maintenance, and cleaning, the sensor and compute bill is spread over far more revenue miles than a consumer car averaging ~1 hour/day ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/features/794319/autonomous-car-power-consumption/)). A consumer ADAS product faces the opposite constraint: low daily utilization, OEM liability, and a fragmented regulatory patchwork that together punish any per-vehicle cost that cannot be amortized across a fleet.

The BOM comparison is stark. One 2024 estimate puts Tesla’s camera suite at ~$400 per vehicle versus ~$12,700 for the sensor suite on Waymo’s fifth-generation Jaguar SUVs, and Tesla is targeting a $30,000 all-in Cybercab ([Contrary Research, citing a 2024 Bloomberg report](https://research.contrary.com/report/cameras-versus-lidar-in-austin)). That estimate is not a primary teardown, but it frames the order-of-magnitude difference. Calibration is the most underappreciated recurring cost: initial calibration is routine in an automated factory environment, but maintaining calibration quality throughout the vehicle’s operating duration is the open challenge, because modern vehicles with an array of perception sensors require periodic recalibration as mechanical shifts occur during operation ([arXiv CaLiCaNet](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.12412v2)). ADAS calibration combines intrinsic parameters (camera focal length and lens distortion, LiDAR beam angle and FOV) with extrinsic spatial alignment across modalities; target-based calibration is accurate but requires controlled conditions, while targetless on-road calibration is more flexible but less precise ([Design World](https://www.designworldonline.com/how-does-multi-sensor-calibration-work-in-autonomous-vehicles/)). Calibration is a prerequisite for sensor fusion. Every added sensor is therefore a recurring calibration and fusion-integration cost over the vehicle lifetime, on top of its unit price.

## Regulation and safety evidence are technology-neutral but not comparable

No major regulator currently mandates or disfavors LiDAR, redundancy, or any specific sensor modality. UNECE R157, the EU General Safety Regulation, NHTSA’s 2025 framework, and China’s first mandatory L3/L4 standard are all formally technology-neutral and performance-based—they specify safety outcomes and validation processes, not sensor hardware. The one partial exception is UNECE R157, which explicitly requires “redundant systems” and “advanced sensors” for environmental awareness without naming LiDAR. This means the LiDAR-vs-vision divide is not a compliance question; it is an engineering, economics, and liability question.

Liability and public trust are the real de facto constraints. With no negligent human driver to target, AV crash victims are expected to bring strict product-liability claims against manufacturers and operators, making the defensibility of the perception stack a legal as well as engineering question. Public trust compounds this: a 2026 survey found only 13% of U.S. drivers would trust riding in a self-driving vehicle and 61% would be afraid to do so ([progressivepolicy.org, 2026](https://www.progressivepolicy.org/building-trust-through-transparency-a-new-federal-framework-for-autonomous-vehicle-safety/)). Under these conditions, a manufacturer operating without driver supervision has a strong incentive to adopt redundant, independently verifiable perception even where no rule requires it, because a single-sensor failure mode that cannot be explained or defended becomes a liability and reputational event. UNECE R157’s redundancy and validation expectations make a single-modality L3 system harder to certify in type-approval markets, while NHTSA’s technology-neutral, outcome-based framework leaves the decision to manufacturers and to state-level permitting, where public-trust and liability pressure can still favor redundant stacks.

The strongest public safety evidence favors Waymo’s LiDAR-first L4 robotaxi *on its own terms*, but it does not transfer to Tesla’s vision-first FSD, because the two camps operate at different SAE levels, in different ODDs, and with different data denominators. The IIHS found that Waymo’s driverless vehicles in San Francisco, Phoenix, Los Angeles, and Austin were involved in 68% fewer police-reportable crashes per VMT than human drivers, after cleaning NHTSA Standing General Order records from 2021–2024; Waymo vehicles traveled about 50 million driverless miles over the study period versus about 222 billion human-driver miles in the same locations, and Waymo also showed 85% fewer single-vehicle crashes and 81% fewer injury crashes per VMT ([IIHS, 2026](https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/waymos-driverless-cars-crash-less-often-than-people)). The peer-reviewed Kusano et al. (2024) study compared Waymo rider-only crash data over 7.14 million RO miles through October 2023 against human benchmarks from the literature: police-reported crashed-vehicle rates were 2.1 incidents per million miles (IPMM) for the ADS versus 4.68 IPMM for the human benchmark (a 55% reduction), and any-injury-reported rates were 0.6 IPMM versus 2.80 IPMM (an 80% reduction) ([Kusano et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39485678/)). Waymo’s own safety dashboard, updated through December 2025, claims 170.7 million RO miles and reductions against human benchmarks of 92% for serious-injury-or-worse crashes, 83% for airbag deployments, and 82% for injury-causing crashes; these are self-reported figures and are not independently verified in the same way as the IIHS or Kusano analyses ([Waymo Safety Impact](https://waymo.com/safety/impact/)).

Tesla does not publish rider-only VMT, so there is no denominator for a Waymo-style crash-rate comparison. Its safety record appears instead in regulatory data: NHTSA’s EA22-002 investigation reviewed 956 crashes where Autopilot was initially alleged to be in use and focused on 322 Autopilot-involved crashes; the resulting December 12, 2023 recall (campaign 23V-838) covered 2,031,220 vehicles because “the prominence and scope of the feature’s controls may not be sufficient to prevent driver misuse of the SAE Level 2 advanced driver-assistance feature,” remedied by an over-the-air software update ([NHTSA recall letter, 2023](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2023/RCAK-23V838-3395.pdf)). The May 7, 2016 Williston, Florida fatality illustrates the supervision problem: NHTSA’s ODI found the Model S was in Autopilot, the AEB gave no warning or automated braking, the driver took no braking or steering action, and the last recorded driver action was increasing the cruise-control set speed to 74 mph less than two minutes before impact; the tractor-trailer should have been visible for at least seven seconds on a clear, dry day ([NTSB ODI PE 16-007, 2017](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/inv/2016/INCLA-PE16007-7876.PDF)). Tesla’s FSD is supervised L2/L3, whereas Waymo operates unsupervised L4—a structural difference that makes raw crash counts non-equivalent.

The claims are not directly comparable for methodological reasons. Companies must report crashes but are not obligated to report miles driven, and only Waymo voluntarily provides driverless VMT, so other companies’ crash rates cannot be computed. Human drivers underreport roughly half of all crashes and a third of injury crashes, whereas AV companies report even minor incidents because of regulatory business risk; until the 2025 SGO amendment aligned property-damage thresholds, AV companies logged incidents that humans would not report, and expensive AV sensors make minor impacts more likely to exceed damage thresholds ([IIHS, 2026](https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/waymos-driverless-cars-crash-less-often-than-people)). A fair comparison would require common VMT denominators, common crash definitions and severity thresholds, common geography and ODD, and independent verification—none of which currently exists.

Incidents complicate both narratives. The Uber ATG fatal crash shows that LiDAR detection alone does not produce a safe outcome: the NTSB found the ADS detected the pedestrian 5.6 seconds before impact but never accurately classified her as a pedestrian or predicted her path, and the system design precluded emergency braking, relying instead on the operator—who was visually distracted by a cell phone ([NTSB HAR-19/03, 2019](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/HWY18MH010.aspx)). The Cruise pedestrian-dragging incident shows the reverse: a LiDAR-equipped L4 vehicle struck a pedestrian who had first been hit by a human-driven car and dragged her about 20 feet; Carnegie Mellon’s Philip Koopman concluded the robotaxi “had multiple possible ways available to avoid initial impact with the pedestrian,” but its computer driver failed to predict the Nissan-pedestrian collision, lost tracking of and “in essence forgot” the pedestrian, and did not detect the dragging ([Koopman, 2024](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~koopman/pubs/Koopman2024_CruiseMishap_IEEEReliabilityMagazine.pdf)). The California DMV suspended Cruise’s permits immediately, and GM ultimately stopped funding Cruise in December 2024 ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruise_(autonomous_vehicle))). Together these cases show that sensor modality is only one variable in safety outcomes; classification, system design, operator supervision, and safety culture dominate the incident record.

## Convergence and the hybrid middle

The LiDAR-first and vision+NN camps are converging at the component and research-method level, but not into a single architecture. 4D imaging radar is narrowing the density gap to LiDAR, FMCW LiDAR is extending range and adding per-point velocity, chip-scale solid-state LiDAR is compressing cost, and the flagship LiDAR-first company is publishing camera-centric end-to-end research while the flagship vision-only company has re-installed radar hardware. The result is a durable hybrid middle rather than a merger: at L4 the optimal remains “all sensors, well fused,” while at L2/L2+ the optimal is “cameras + radar + strong NN.”

4D imaging radar is the most direct threat to the dichotomy because it adds elevation and velocity to radar’s all-weather range. Mobileye’s imaging radar claims a rich point cloud with heightened elevational resolution, detecting road users at 315 m and hazards at 230 m ([Mobileye, 2024](https://www.mobileye.com/blog/mobileyes-imaging-radar-takes-the-wheel/)). But a survey of 4D mmWave radar stresses that 4D radar point clouds remain “markedly more severe” in sparsity and noise than LiDAR point clouds, so 4D radar approaches LiDAR-like density without matching it ([arXiv 2306.04242v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.04242v3)). FMCW LiDAR attacks the other side: Aurora’s FirstLight FMCW lidar claims over 400 m range, per-point Doppler velocity, and interference immunity, and the company has integrated discrete optical components into semiconductor chips to “dramatically reduce the cost,” with fleet fitment targeted for 2027 ([Aurora](https://aurora.tech/newsroom/firstlight-lidar-on-a-chip)). Aeva’s FMCW 4D lidar was selected as the reference LiDAR sensor for NVIDIA DRIVE Hyperion ([optics.org, 2026](https://optics.org/news/aeva-and-nvidia-to-integrate-4d-lidar-as-sensor-in-the-drive-hyperion-platform)).

Methodological convergence is equally important. Waymo’s EMMA is camera-centric—it does not use LiDAR or radar inputs, a limitation Waymo itself flags as requiring “more sophisticated 3D sensing encoders”—and it is a research model, not a production stack ([Waymo, 2024](https://waymo.com/blog/2024/10/introducing-emma)). EMMA therefore does not show LiDAR becoming unnecessary; it shows the LiDAR-first flagship borrowing camera-centric end-to-end methods while its deployed Driver remains a multi-sensor fusion stack. Vision-only players are also reconsidering radar: Tesla’s Hardware 4 vehicles ship with a front-mounted Tesla-branded radar module connected to the HW4 computer, and the board’s coding references a four-dimensional “Phoenix” radar; however, the unit was software-disabled during service appointments at the time of the teardown, and its activation remains unconfirmed ([InsideEVs, 2023](https://insideevs.com/news/671961/tesla-model-x-hardware-4-radar-confirmed/)). The “vision-only” label is softening at the hardware level even though Tesla’s production perception remains camera-primary. NVIDIA DRIVE Hyperion is the reference-architecture expression of this hybrid middle: an open, modular platform whose sensor suite includes lidar, multiple radars, cameras, ultrasonics, and external microphones, with Aeva’s FMCW 4D lidar as its reference sensor ([optics.org, 2026](https://optics.org/news/aeva-and-nvidia-to-integrate-4d-lidar-as-sensor-in-the-drive-hyperion-platform)).

China’s consumer ADAS market is collapsing the cost assumption that made vision-only economically rational in the first place. From January to July 2023, 202,000 passenger cars in China shipped with LiDAR as standard OEM equipment, up 523.3% year-over-year, with forecasts of over 350,000 for 2023 and over 600,000 in 2025 ([ResearchInChina, 2023](https://www.researchinchina.com/Htmls/Report/2023/72925.html)). NIO, XPeng, and Li Auto are the leading promoters, fitting LiDAR on models including the NIO ET5/ET7/ES7/ES8/ES6, Li L9/L8, and Xpeng P5/G9/G6. The top five passenger-car LiDAR suppliers in that window were all Chinese: Hesai ranked first with over 45% market share, and Innovusion followed with 25.0%, mainly serving NIO ([ResearchInChina, 2023](https://www.researchinchina.com/Htmls/Report/2023/72925.html)). Huawei’s ADS 2.0 reduced the count from three LiDARs in ADS 1.0 to one, illustrating cost-driven consolidation rather than abandonment. Yole Intelligence projects global automotive LiDAR revenue rising from $300 million in 2022 to nearly $4.5 billion by 2028 ([Inside Autonomous Vehicles, 2023](https://insideautonomousvehicles.com/nio-applications-push-innovusion-to-top-of-lidar-market/)).

Strategic exits show that economics and ODD, more than sensor physics, determine which programs survive. Argo AI shut down in October 2022 after Ford and Volkswagen declined further investment, with Ford recording a $2.7 billion non-cash, pretax impairment and explicitly redirecting resources from robotaxi AV to L2+/L3 ADAS ([TechCrunch, Oct 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/10/26/ford-vw-backed-argo-ai-is-shutting-down/)). Apple canceled Project Titan in February 2024 after roughly a decade and billions of dollars, shifting the team to generative AI ([The Guardian, Feb 2024](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2024/feb/27/apple-cancels-electric-car-layoffs)). Lyft’s Level 5 self-driving division was acquired by Toyota subsidiary Woven Planet for approximately $550 million, announced in April 2021 ([Lyft investor press release, Apr 2021](https://investor.lyft.com/news-events-presentations/press-releases/detail/153/woven-planet-a-subsidiary-of-toyota-to-acquire-lyfts-self-driving-car-division)). These exits reflect a judgment that owning full-stack L4 technology was not a rational capital allocation, independent of sensor choice.

Three fault lines remain genuinely unresolved by both camps. First, absolute metric depth: LiDAR measures it directly, cameras infer it, and 4D radar—despite its density gains—remains sparser and noisier than LiDAR ([arXiv 2306.04242v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.04242v3)). Second, adverse weather: camera performance degrades in rain, fog, and glare, LiDAR is affected by scatter depending on wavelength, and 4D radar is the most weather-robust but lowest-resolution modality. Third, the cost of redundancy: chip-scale FMCW lidar compresses the sensor BOM but does not eliminate calibration, fusion, and maintenance costs. Until these three fault lines move, convergence will remain at the component and research level rather than the architectural level.

## Decision framework and open questions

The LiDAR-versus-vision choice is better predicted by deployment economics and liability allocation than by sensor physics alone. LiDAR-first is rational when the operator—not the driver—bears responsibility for automated operation, the ODD is geofenced and pre-mapped, and high utilization amortizes a multi-thousand-dollar sensor suite; vision+NN-first is rational when the vehicle must hit a consumer price point, the fleet can supply edge-case data at scale, and a human remains the legal supervisor.

| Factor | LiDAR-first favored when… | Vision+NN-first favored when… |
|---|---|---|
| ODD and operating speed | Geofenced, pre-mapped, speed-limited ODD where deterministic 3D occupancy plus HD maps shrink the search space | Unconstrained consumer ODD where mapping every road is uneconomic and the network must generalize to unseen roads |
| Deployment model and liability | Operator/OEM bears liability during automated operation; high utilization amortizes the suite | Driver remains the legal supervisor at L2/L2+, so OEM exposure is limited and BOM dominates |
| BOM tolerance and volume | Robotaxi or low-volume flagship where a ~$12,700 suite is tolerable | High-volume consumer where a ~$400 camera suite matters; at a $25k price point even $200–$500 LiDAR is material |
| Data assets and fleet scale | Smaller curated fleet plus simulation-based validation | Millions of customer vehicles with shadow-mode mining; marginal sensor cost near zero once hardware is installed |
| Safety-case and regulatory exposure | Deterministic, auditable 3D occupancy aligns with ISO 26262/SOTIF/UL 4600 and supports L3 type approval | Statistical perception is harder to certify; at supervised L2 the safety case is the driver, and NHTSA’s stance is technology-neutral |
| Adverse-weather requirements | When weather is inside the ODD and cross-modality redundancy is needed—though LiDAR also scatters in fog/snow | When weather is excluded from the ODD or handled by 4D radar plus NN |
| Compute and energy budget | Cost shifts to sensor BOM, calibration, and fusion complexity | Cost shifts to AI accelerators, power, thermal load, and data infrastructure |
| Corporate philosophy | Majority of OEMs pursue sensor fusion with LiDAR as the redundancy answer | Tesla’s vision-only stance and fleet-scale bet; Musk has framed LiDAR as a “crutch” |

LiDAR-first is likely a mistake when it is applied to a high-volume consumer vehicle at a $25k price point, where the BOM cannot be amortized and the calibration burden compounds over the vehicle lifetime; or when the ODD is unconstrained, so the HD-map and geofence dependencies become liabilities rather than assets. Vision+NN-first is likely a mistake when the system is asked to operate unsupervised at L4 without the metric-depth and redundancy margin, when the fleet data does not cover the long tail, or when liability shifts to the OEM at L3 and the statistical safety case cannot be certified to the same expectations as deterministic 3D occupancy.

The vision-only thesis would be falsified if camera-inferred depth cannot match LiDAR metric depth at highway range, if adverse-weather incidents persist at scale, or if fleet data shows systematically higher crash rates in comparable ODDs. The redundancy thesis would be falsified if camera + 4D radar matches LiDAR-fusion safety at lower cost over large fleet data, or if LiDAR cost collapse makes redundancy cheap enough that the debate becomes moot. The Uber ATG crash is a falsification constraint on the redundancy thesis too: the system’s radar and LiDAR detected the pedestrian about six seconds before impact at 43 mph, but it classified her as “unknown,” then “vehicle,” then “bicycle,” and suppressed braking because the design did not consider jaywalking pedestrians ([NTSB HAR-19/03](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/accidentreports/reports/har1903.pdf)).

Open technical questions for the next 3–5 years:

- Can monocular/multi-view vision achieve LiDAR-grade absolute metric depth at highway range?
- Can camera + 4D radar match LiDAR+camera+radar performance in fog, snow, and heavy rain?
- At what LiDAR price does the vision-only economic argument weaken for consumer vehicles?
- Can statistical perception be certified to the same level as deterministic 3D occupancy under ISO 26262/SOTIF/UL 4600?
- Will foundation/world models obsolete modular perception-planning stacks?
