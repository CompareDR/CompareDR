# Technical Report: Why Some Autonomous Vehicle Systems Prioritize LiDAR While Others Rely on Neural Networks and Cameras

**Date:** October 10, 2026  
**Prepared in:** APA-style markdown format

---

## Executive Summary

Autonomous vehicle (AV) perception strategies have split into two dominant engineering philosophies. One prioritizes **LiDAR-centered multi-sensor redundancy**, usually combining LiDAR, radar, cameras, high-definition maps, and modular software. The other emphasizes **camera-heavy neural-network perception**, often arguing that scalable autonomy can be achieved through large data, end-to-end learning, and lower-cost passive sensors.

This report finds that the split is not mainly ideological. It reflects different optimization targets:

- **LiDAR-prioritized systems** are better suited to today’s **Level 4 robotaxi and fleet deployments**, where safety evidence, regulatory approval, redundancy, and bounded operational design domains are more important than consumer vehicle cost.
- **Camera/neural-network systems** are better suited to **mass-market Level 2 and Level 2+ ADAS**, where cost, manufacturability, fleet data collection, and broad consumer adoption dominate.
- For unsupervised Level 4 autonomy in complex urban environments, the evidence currently favors **multi-sensor stacks with LiDAR, radar, and cameras**, not camera-only autonomy. Cameras and neural networks are indispensable, but LiDAR remains a practical safety and validation tool because it provides direct 3D geometry, improves redundancy, and helps constrain perception uncertainty.

My concrete technical opinion is that **LiDAR should remain part of safety-critical Level 4 deployments in 2026**, especially in dense urban robotaxi operations. Camera-first neural systems can scale faster and cheaper, but without strong sensor redundancy and safety-case evidence, they are better viewed as advanced supervised ADAS rather than a sufficient basis for broad unsupervised autonomy.

## 1. Background: From DARPA-Style Pipelines to Modern Autonomy

Modern autonomous driving still follows a recognizable pipeline: localize the vehicle, perceive nearby objects and road geometry, predict other agents’ behavior, and plan/control the vehicle many times per second. This structure was already visible in DARPA Grand Challenge-era systems and remains embedded in many current robotaxi stacks, even where deep learning has replaced handcrafted components ([ABC7 News, 2025](https://abc7news.com/post/driverless-cars-waymo-history-secret-google-self-driving-car-project-robotaxi-company-darpa/16775642/)).

The DARPA Grand Challenge was important not because early vehicles were commercially ready, but because it concentrated robotics, perception, localization, and planning researchers around the end-to-end driving problem. The 2004 race had no finisher, yet later DARPA participants helped seed Google’s self-driving car project, Waymo, and other AV firms ([ABC7 News, 2025](https://abc7news.com/post/driverless-cars-waymo-history-secret-google-self-driving-car-project-robotaxi-company-darpa/16775642/)).

Early successful systems already used sensor fusion. Stanford’s *Stanley*, for example, combined laser scans of nearby terrain with camera vision of the horizon and adapted its software to changing road conditions in real time ([Thrun et al., 2006](https://spectrum.ieee.org/darpa-grand-challenge)). This matters because the LiDAR-versus-camera debate is not new; it is a continuation of a long-standing systems engineering question: should autonomy depend on redundant, physically different sensors, or can learning-based visual intelligence infer enough from cameras alone?

## 2. Sensor Modalities and Their Technical Tradeoffs

### 2.1 Cameras and Neural Networks

Cameras are attractive because they are inexpensive, compact, high-resolution, and semantically rich. They capture texture, color, signs, lane markings, gestures, traffic lights, and object appearance. Neural networks trained on large video datasets can learn strong classification, detection, segmentation, and prediction features from camera inputs.

However, cameras are passive sensors. They depend on ambient light and do not directly measure depth. Camera-based perception can degrade under poor illumination, shadows, fog, heavy rain, glare, and visual ambiguity ([Springer, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44443-025-00302-0)). NIST’s 2026 assessment similarly reports that fog-related diminished lighting and low sun altitude had especially large effects on YOLO, DETR, and RT-DETR perception performance, while occlusions and combined weather/adversarial road conditions increased uncertainty ([NIST, 2026](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/other/2026/01/30/on-the-assessment-of-sensitivity-of-autonomous-veh/final)).

Neural networks are also vulnerable to uncertainty, domain shift, and long-tail events. AVs operate in open-world environments where pedestrians, cyclists, construction zones, emergency vehicles, debris, glare, reflections, and unusual road layouts can appear in combinations not well represented in training data. Research on trustworthy AI for AVs highlights that AI introduces issues of unpredictability, opacity, overfitting, bias, and lack of causality, creating new testing burdens for ADAS and ADS systems ([Springer, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12544-025-00732-x)).

### 2.2 LiDAR

LiDAR provides direct 3D geometry and distance estimates by actively emitting light and measuring returns. It works independently of ambient illumination, making it useful at night and in lighting conditions that challenge cameras. It also provides geometric structure that is valuable for obstacle detection, free-space estimation, localization, mapping, and safety validation ([DriveX Workshop, 2026](https://drivex-workshop.github.io/cvpr2026/static/pdf/26_4D_Radar_Meets_LiDAR_and_Ca.pdf)).

LiDAR is not perfect. Returns can degrade in rain and fog due to scattering and attenuation, and reflective surfaces or long range can challenge detection quality ([DriveX Workshop, 2026](https://drivex-workshop.github.io/cvpr2026/static/pdf/26_4D_Radar_Meets_LiDAR_and_Ca.pdf)). Long-range classification also remains difficult in dense urban or adverse-weather conditions, and market research notes that heavy rain or reflective interference can reduce long-range object classification consistency ([Congruence Market Insights, 2026](https://www.congruencemarketinsights.com/report/automotive-lidar-market)).

Still, LiDAR’s major advantage is that it reduces reliance on learned monocular depth inference. In safety-critical autonomy, direct range measurement is valuable because braking, time-to-collision, and spatial clearance depend on knowing where objects are, not merely what they appear to be.

### 2.3 Radar and 4D Radar

Radar is often the third leg of the perception stack. It is robust in poor visibility and provides Doppler velocity cues, making it complementary to cameras and LiDAR. Its tradeoffs are lower spatial resolution, sparsity, noise, and weaker object classification features ([Nature Scientific Reports, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-32588-5)).

4D radar improves elevation and velocity awareness relative to older radar, but it does not eliminate the need for semantic perception. The most technically defensible architecture is therefore not “LiDAR only” or “camera only,” but **sensor fusion**, where cameras provide semantics, LiDAR provides geometry, and radar provides weather-resilient velocity cues.

## 3. Why LiDAR-Centered Systems Prioritize Redundancy and Validation

### 3.1 Level 4 Robotaxis Need Operational Assurance, Not Just Perception Accuracy

Level 4 robotaxis operate without a human driver inside defined operational design domains (ODDs). Their commercial viability depends on city permits, safety cases, incident reporting, remote assistance policies, and public trust. Waymo is widely positioned as the leading U.S. Level 4 robotaxi operator, but its commercialization remains constrained by city-level approvals, weather limits, software remedies, and defined ODD boundaries ([DataM Intelligence, 2026](https://www.datamintelligence.com/research-report/autonomous-self-driving-cars-market)).

For this type of deployment, LiDAR is useful not only as a sensor but as a **safety-case asset**. It helps engineers demonstrate that the vehicle can detect obstacles at known distances, validate spatial clearance, and cross-check camera outputs. Regulators increasingly expect safety evidence, scenario coverage, cybersecurity, software-update discipline, and failure documentation rather than broad autonomy claims ([Eno Center for Transportation, 2025](https://enotrans.org/article/2025-autonomous-vehicles-federal-policy-wrapped/)).

### 3.2 Traditional Perception Metrics Are Insufficient

A key deeper research finding is that the LiDAR-versus-camera debate cannot be answered by object-detection metrics alone. Metrics such as intersection over union (IoU) and mean average precision (mAP) evaluate perception accuracy in isolation, but they do not answer whether perception is good enough for downstream planning and decision-making ([Piazzoni et al., 2020](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2020/0483.pdf)).

In the IJCAI perception-error modeling study, safety outcomes depended strongly on temporal failure characteristics. No-collision rate improved with detection frequency only up to about 75%, while the maximum non-detection interval had a stronger effect on success rate ([Piazzoni et al., 2020](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2020/0483.pdf)). This finding is crucial: a camera-neural system may have high average accuracy but still fail dangerously if it loses an object for too long at the wrong moment.

Robust evaluation should connect perception errors to downstream safety outcomes: minimum spatial clearance, minimum temporal distance, no-collision rate, speed, acceleration, deceleration, and jerk limits ([Piazzoni et al., 2020](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2020/0483.pdf)). NIST similarly argues that robustness assessment should use uncertainty quantification across simulated and real adverse scenarios and tie perception quality to safety criteria such as stopping distance under different road surfaces and speeds ([NIST, 2026](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/other/2026/01/30/on-the-assessment-of-sensitivity-of-autonomous-veh/final)).

### 3.3 LiDAR Simplifies Some Safety Arguments

LiDAR does not guarantee safety, but it can make some safety claims easier to support. A direct 3D point cloud can verify obstacle existence and distance even when a camera classifier is uncertain. This is useful because gross perception failures—such as failing to detect an obstacle’s existence—are especially safety-critical. Perception Simplex research argues that fault-tolerant AV designs should include mechanisms to mitigate obstacle existence detection faults, using verifiable detection methods and functional redundancy ([Bansal et al., n.d.](https://ayooshbansal.com/assets/pdf/Perception_Simplex.pdf)).

This explains why robotaxi developers often accept LiDAR cost and complexity. In a commercial fleet, the cost of extra sensors may be justified by higher safety confidence, regulatory acceptance, and reduced risk of catastrophic failure.

## 4. Why Camera-Neural Systems Prioritize Scale, Cost, and Data

### 4.1 Cost and Manufacturability

Camera-heavy systems are attractive for mass-market vehicles because cameras are cheaper and already integrated into ADAS platforms. Level 2 vehicles dominate the ADAS market because they provide combined steering and speed control without the regulatory, liability, and validation burdens associated with Level 3 and higher autonomy. They also use scalable sensor setups, mainly cameras and radar, enabling deployment in mid-range vehicles ([MarketsandMarkets, 2026](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/driver-assistance-systems-market-1201.html)).

LiDAR cost is falling rapidly, but it remains a barrier for mass-market adoption. Advanced long-range LiDAR modules reportedly add about 19% to 24% to vehicle sensor-system costs in mid-volume programs, keeping adoption concentrated in premium vehicles and commercial autonomy use cases ([Congruence Market Insights, 2026](https://www.congruencemarketinsights.com/report/automotive-lidar-market)).

### 4.2 Neural Networks Benefit From Fleet Scale

Camera-first approaches also benefit from massive fleet data. If millions of consumer vehicles collect visual data, neural networks can be trained and improved across many scenarios. End-to-end and planning-oriented learning research has accelerated, with modern models attempting to combine perception, prediction, and planning into more unified architectures ([OpenDriveLab, 2026](https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/End-to-end-Autonomous-Driving/blob/main/papers.md)).

However, the most advanced research does not imply that camera-only autonomy is already sufficient. Long-tail events remain difficult. Motional’s 2026 nuReasoning dataset, for example, focuses specifically on rare and atypical scenarios involving vulnerable road users, aggressive cut-ins, hard braking, adverse weather, visual clutter, construction zones, occlusions, animals, debris, and out-of-distribution objects. Motional argues that generalized end-to-end architectures may struggle with complex edge cases without reasoning annotations and intermediate safety guardrails ([Motional, 2026](https://motional.com/news/cracking-long-tail-code-autonomous-driving-nureasoning)).

### 4.3 Camera-Neural Systems Are Strongest Where Humans Remain Responsible

The strongest near-term use case for camera-neural autonomy is supervised driving assistance. In Level 2 and Level 2+ systems, the driver remains responsible, which reduces the required autonomy safety burden. This is why the market is bifurcated: Level 2 supervised driving is the near-term revenue backbone for OEMs, while Level 4 robotaxis define larger strategic upside but are harder to scale ([DataM Intelligence, 2026](https://www.datamintelligence.com/research-report/autonomous-self-driving-cars-market)).

## 5. Comparative Technical Assessment

| Dimension | LiDAR-prioritized multi-sensor stack | Camera/neural-network stack |
|---|---:|---:|
| Primary strength | Direct 3D geometry, distance, redundancy | Low cost, rich semantics, scalable data |
| Typical deployment fit | Level 4 robotaxis, shuttles, autonomous fleets | Level 2/L2+ consumer ADAS |
| Weakness | Cost, calibration, integration, weather scattering | Poor lighting, glare, fog, lack of direct depth |
| Safety-case value | Strong for spatial clearance and obstacle distance | Strong only with extensive validation and uncertainty handling |
| Scaling advantage | Fleet-level, high-value deployments | Consumer-scale deployment |
| Regulatory fit in 2026 | Better aligned with evidence-heavy safety cases | More suitable when human supervision remains required |
| Long-tail handling | Improved through sensor redundancy, still hard | Depends heavily on data coverage and model generalization |

## 6. Market and Architecture Trends

The economics of LiDAR are changing. One market report projects automotive LiDAR growth from about USD 1.1 billion in 2025 to USD 25.7 billion by 2033, driven by Level 3 ADAS integration, premium EVs, solid-state LiDAR, autonomous fleet trials, and AI sensor fusion ([Congruence Market Insights, 2026](https://www.congruencemarketinsights.com/report/automotive-lidar-market)).

Solid-state LiDAR is especially important because it is more compact, lower power, more durable, and cheaper to calibrate than mechanical LiDAR. Congruence reports solid-state systems at nearly 49% of 2026 automotive LiDAR deployments and about 27% lower production-related calibration costs than mechanical systems ([Congruence Market Insights, 2026](https://www.congruencemarketinsights.com/report/automotive-lidar-market)).

Mordor Intelligence reports that solid-state average selling prices fell more than 30% from 2023 to 2025, with automotive-qualified flash sensors falling below USD 400, helped by silicon photonics, wafer-level optics, automated testing, and higher production volume. This supports consumer ADAS option packages below about USD 1,500 ([Mordor Intelligence, 2026](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/automotive-lidar-market)).

MEMS currently leads automotive solid-state LiDAR by share, with a cited 35.6% position in 2025, because compact beam steering can reduce package size and mechanical wear versus spinning LiDAR while fitting cost-sensitive ADAS needs ([Market.us, 2026](https://market.us/report/automotive-solid-state-lidar-market/)).

These trends weaken the old argument that LiDAR is inherently too expensive for vehicles. LiDAR remains costly relative to cameras, but the gap is narrowing, especially in premium EVs, L3 systems, and robotaxi fleets.

## 7. Safety Evidence and Public Benchmarking

Safety comparisons between AVs and human drivers are complicated by underreporting, ODD differences, crash definitions, and exposure differences. APM cites 2023 U.S. police-reported crash data at about 1.94 crashes per million miles, while Waymo has reported around 4.5 crashes per million miles; Waymo argues that human crashes are underreported, which makes direct comparison difficult ([APM Research Lab, n.d.](https://www.apmresearchlab.org/10x/robotaxi-safety)).

Injury-rate comparisons are more favorable to Waymo but still rough. APM reports Waymo had 3 injury-causing crashes over 7.3 million driverless miles, or 0.41 per million miles, while Arizona human drivers averaged 0.71 injury crashes per million miles in 2022 ([APM Research Lab, n.d.](https://www.apmresearchlab.org/10x/robotaxi-safety)). Waymo’s own Safety Impact data reports substantially lower incident rates than human benchmarks, including any-injury crashes at 0.67 incidents per million miles versus 3.77 for the benchmark, and airbag-deployment crashes at 0.29 versus 1.61 ([Waymo, 2026](https://waymo.com/safety/impact/)).

These data do not prove that LiDAR alone causes better safety outcomes. Waymo’s performance reflects an entire system: sensors, maps, simulation, operational design limits, fleet operations, software updates, and safety culture. But the evidence does show that LiDAR-centered, multi-sensor robotaxi systems can produce measurable safety claims under bounded ODDs.

## 8. Regulation: Why Sensor Strategy Is Becoming a Safety-Case Issue

AV regulation is moving from light reporting toward safety-case and failure-based oversight. Texas now requires permits for fully autonomous vehicles, while California is updating rules to require safety cases and shift from disengagement reports to documentation of dynamic-driving-task failures. NHTSA has also announced an AV Framework and streamlined ADS/ADAS crash reporting ([Eno Center for Transportation, 2025](https://enotrans.org/article/2025-autonomous-vehicles-federal-policy-wrapped/)).

The proposed AV Safety Data Act would require NHTSA reporting on vehicle miles traveled, injuries, law-enforcement interactions, and collisions. Such data are necessary because raw crash counts are not meaningful without exposure, ODD, road type, weather, and operating context ([Eno Center for Transportation, 2025](https://enotrans.org/article/2025-autonomous-vehicles-federal-policy-wrapped/)).

Safety certification expectations are also becoming more scenario-based and evidence-heavy. Urban SDK summarizes emerging expectations that Level 4 AVs demonstrate competence across 1,000+ scenarios, including emergency handling, system failures, and extreme weather, with redundancy, cybersecurity, over-the-air safety updates, and standardized simulation/real-world testing treated as mandatory elements ([Urban SDK, 2025](https://www.urbansdk.com/resources/safety-and-liability-key-challenges-in-regulating-self-driving-cars)).

This regulatory shift favors architectures that can produce auditable evidence. LiDAR helps because it supplies measurable 3D distance and obstacle information, but regulators are unlikely to mandate one sensor. Instead, they will increasingly require developers to prove that their chosen architecture meets safety goals across realistic ODDs.

## 9. Synthesis: The Real Difference Is Risk Allocation

The LiDAR-versus-camera divide is best understood as a difference in **risk allocation**.

LiDAR-first developers spend more on hardware to reduce perception uncertainty and simplify safety validation. Camera-first developers spend less on hardware but take on more software, data, and validation risk. Both use neural networks, but they use them differently:

- LiDAR-centered systems use neural networks inside a redundant sensor-fusion framework.
- Camera-centered systems rely more heavily on neural networks to infer depth, semantics, prediction, and sometimes planning from visual input.

This explains why the same industry can rationally support both strategies. A robotaxi fleet can amortize expensive sensors over commercial rides and maintenance depots. A consumer vehicle program must hit price points, styling requirements, manufacturability targets, and warranty constraints.

The deeper technical issue is not whether LiDAR or cameras are “better,” but whether the system can maintain safe behavior during **uncertainty, long-tail events, adverse weather, and temporal perception loss**. Current research suggests that downstream safety metrics, uncertainty-aware evaluation, and temporal failure analysis matter more than headline detection accuracy ([Piazzoni et al., 2020](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2020/0483.pdf); [NIST, 2026](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/other/2026/01/30/on-the-assessment-of-sensitivity-of-autonomous-veh/final)).

---

## 10. Conclusion and Technical Opinion

In 2026, LiDAR-prioritized systems and camera-neural systems are optimized for different deployment realities. LiDAR-centered multi-sensor stacks are better matched to unsupervised Level 4 robotaxis because they provide redundancy, direct geometry, and stronger evidence for safety cases. Camera-neural systems are better matched to scalable consumer ADAS because they reduce cost and leverage fleet learning.

My technical judgment is that **camera-only or camera-dominant unsupervised Level 4 autonomy is not yet the most defensible architecture for dense urban deployment**, unless restricted to narrow ODDs with exceptionally strong safety evidence. Neural networks are essential, but relying on them to infer all safety-critical geometry from cameras adds avoidable risk. Conversely, LiDAR is not a complete solution; it must be integrated with cameras, radar, prediction, planning, simulation, and post-deployment monitoring.

The most credible near-term architecture for driverless operation is therefore **multi-modal, uncertainty-aware, and safety-case driven**: cameras for semantics, LiDAR for geometry, radar for velocity and poor-visibility robustness, and neural networks for perception, prediction, and planning under explicit validation constraints. For mass-market supervised ADAS, camera-first neural systems remain economically rational. For unsupervised robotaxis, LiDAR remains technically justified.
