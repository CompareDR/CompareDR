# Should There Be Age Limits for Voting? A Principled Assessment

## The Core Tension

Voting-age limits sit at the intersection of two incompatible frameworks. On one view, voting is a universal political right grounded in democratic equality: every person subject to collective decisions has a claim to a say in them. On the other, voting is a competence-based qualification presupposing specific cognitive capacities — understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and the ability to express a choice. These frameworks generate opposing conclusions about both ends of the age spectrum, and the "bright line" of age operates as a fiercely contested proxy at the point of entry but a largely unexamined presumption at the point of exit.

The practical upshot is stark: roughly 90% of countries set the voting age at 18 or above, yet no modern democracy imposes a maximum voting age ([UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)). This asymmetry is not simply arbitrary, but neither is it fully principled. It reflects a genuine difference in how the law treats capacity acquisition (a fairly clear developmental trajectory) versus capacity decline (variable and poorly tracked by chronological age), combined with the historical expansion of the franchise and the equal-citizenship principle's resistance to removing existing rights.

The strongest defensible position combines a lower minimum voting age (16) with no maximum age, paired with civic education and future-generations institutions. The evidence supports adolescent deliberative capacity; it does not support either blanket disenfranchisement of older citizens or age-weighted voting. The remainder of this report develops the reasoning and evidence for that conclusion.

## Democratic Principles: Rights, Competence, and the Franchise

### The Universal-Right Framework

Normative democratic theory defines democracy as "a method of collective decision making characterized by a kind of equality among the participants" ([SEP, "Democracy"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/democracy/)). From this starting point, the franchise follows from equal citizenship: each person subject to collective decisions has a claim to a say. Democratic equality — the requirement that each citizen's interests receive equal consideration, elaborated in Ronald Dworkin's *Sovereign Virtue* — is foundational.

The "all-affected-interests" (or stakeholder) principle extends the claim further. Robert Goodin argues that those whose interests are affected by a decision should have a voice in it, implying a far broader franchise than conventional boundaries ([Goodin, "Enfranchising All Affected Interests, and Its Alternatives," *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 35(1), 2007](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1088-4963.2007.00098.x)). Human-rights law reflects this universalist impulse: major treaties guarantee "universal and equal" suffrage without setting age cut-offs, and UNICEF frames voting as a child's civil and political right under Article 12 of the Convention on the Rights of the Child ([UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)).

On this view, the standard minimum age of 18 is not a natural boundary but a convention — a proxy for capacities that voting presupposes. The all-affected principle supports lowering the minimum, but it does not by itself support a maximum age: the same logic that demands inclusion of the young also demands inclusion of the old, and the group it most clearly identifies as unrepresented is future generations, who cannot vote at all.

### The Competence Framework

The rival tradition treats voting as a task requiring knowledge, rationality, and judgment. John Stuart Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government* is the classic statement. Mill held that "no arrangement of the suffrage... can be permanently satisfactory in which any person or class is peremptorily excluded," but he also insisted on exclusions "required by positive reasons," including a literacy requirement — "I regard it as wholly inadmissible that any person should participate in the suffrage without being able to read, write, and... perform the common operations of arithmetic" ([Mill, ch. 8](https://www.ditext.com/mill/rg/chapter8.html)). His plural-voting proposal, which would give the educated extra votes, makes the competence logic explicit.

John Rawls's *Political Liberalism* supplies a contemporary foundation: citizens are expected to exercise the "two moral powers" — a capacity for a sense of justice and a capacity for a conception of the good — implying a threshold of civic competence ([SEP, "John Rawls"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rawls/)). Dana Kay Nelkin defends age as a "defensible criterion" for eligibility because it is "a proxy for having a broad set of cognitive and motivational capacities," suggesting that 16 is a good proxy under current developmental evidence ([Nelkin, *Journal of Practical Ethics* 8(2), 2020](https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/jpe/article/id/11970/)).

### The Asymmetry and Its Implications

The competence framework, taken seriously, would seem to require screening at both ends of the age spectrum. Yet no democracy imposes an individualized competence test on elderly voters. As Kordi observes, the U.S. Twenty-Sixth Amendment sets "a floor, not a ceiling, on the voting age," and "there is no uniform age of expiry for voting rights — i.e., adults do not age out of voter rolls at age seventy"; when the state does disenfranchise a voter for mental incompetency, "it does so on a case-by-case basis" ([Kordi, *University of Chicago Law Review Online*](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/online-archive/personalized-enfranchisement)).

This double standard — competence presumed acquired at 18 but never lost at any fixed age — suggests that the age criterion functions less as a pure competence test than as a "bright line": an administratively simple convention that is contested at the entry point (where the young claim inclusion) but accepted at the exit point (where the old retain the franchise).

## The Minimum Voting Age: Cognitive Development and Empirical Evidence

### The Developmental Science

The central finding of developmental research is that cognitive capacity for deliberative decision-making reaches adult-like levels around age 16, while psychosocial maturity — the capacity for self-restraint in emotionally arousing or risky contexts — continues to develop into the early twenties. This "maturity gap" is the key empirical foundation for debates about lowering the voting age.

Icenogle et al. (2019), assessing 5,227 individuals ages 10–30 from eleven countries, found that "cognitive capacity reached adult levels around age 16, [while] psychosocial maturity reached adult levels beyond age 18." They conclude that "juveniles may be capable of deliberative decision making by age 16, but even young adults may demonstrate 'immature' decision making in arousing situations," and that "it is therefore reasonable to have different age boundaries for different legal purposes" ([Icenogle et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6551607/)).

This replicates Steinberg et al. (2009), who found that "cognitive capacity — the basic cognitive processes supporting the ability to reason logically — matures by 16, whereas psychosocial maturity... does not fully mature until several years later." The implication for voting is significant: voting is typically a private, reflective act conducted in a low-arousal setting, without the immediate peer pressure or emotional stakes that characterize "hot" decisions like driving or criminal conduct. If the cognitive-control system reaches adult levels by 16, then 16-year-olds should possess the deliberative capacity that voting requires.

Oosterhoff et al. (2022) surveyed 397 adolescents (ages 16–17) and 788 adults, finding that "adolescents provided greater integrative and elaborative complexity in their reasoning to change the voting age than adults," consistent with "past research indicating that adolescents possess the cognitive capacity and political knowledge to vote" ([Oosterhoff et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10898223/)). The evidence on propositional political knowledge is mixed — some studies find parity with adults, while one U.K. study found lower knowledge — but Oosterhoff et al. caution that "propositional knowledge does not predict other indicators of political reasoning."

Researchers have cautioned against directly translating neuroscientific findings into fixed legal thresholds. Maslowsky et al. (2024) argue that developmental science should inform but not determinatively set legal boundaries ([Maslowsky et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1878929324000963)). The Icenogle study itself argues for different age boundaries for different purposes rather than a single threshold.

### Evidence from Jurisdictions That Lowered the Voting Age

The real-world evidence strongly supports lowering the minimum to 16, though with important qualifications about implementation and aggregate effects.

| Jurisdiction | Reform | Key findings |
|---|---|---|
| **Austria** (2007, first European country) | National and state elections | 16–17-year-olds turned out at higher rates than 18–20-year-olds; effects persisted into the 20s; no evidence of harm to vote quality or electoral outcomes ([Huebner & Eichhorn 2025](https://www.jrrt.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Votes-at-16-report-FINAL.pdf)) |
| **Scotland** (2015 Act permanent) | Scottish Parliament and local elections | >100,000 16–17-year-olds registered for 2014 referendum; cohorts eligible at 16/17 showed higher turnout in 2021, up to seven years later ([Eichhorn & Huebner 2025](https://www.cogitatiopress.com/politicsandgovernance/article/download/9283/4184)) |
| **Wales** (Senedd 2021; local 2022) | Senedd and local elections | First Senedd election under votes at 16 held in 2021; Wales Governance Centre evaluation characterises the first experience as a "false start" with low take-up ([Wales Governance Centre 2025](https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0011/2894096/Votes-at-16-A-False-Start-PDF-Feb-2025.pdf)) |
| **Germany (several Länder)** | State and local elections | States with voting age 16 saw larger increases in young adults' turnout in the 2021 federal election than states with a more restrictive voting age ([Huebner & Eichhorn 2025](https://www.jrrt.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Votes-at-16-report-FINAL.pdf)) |
| **Argentina, Brazil, Ecuador, Nicaragua** | Various | Included among countries where 16–17-year-olds vote at higher rates than first-time voters aged 18+; no negative outcomes reported ([Huebner & Eichhorn 2025](https://www.jrrt.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Votes-at-16-report-FINAL.pdf)) |
| **Norway** | Municipal voting-age trials from 2011 | Turnout similar to the population as a whole, higher than other young voters; no evidence of a long-term turnout effect; preferences not substantially different; more young candidates elected ([Norwegian Youth Wiki](https://national-policies.eacea.ec.europa.eu/youthwiki/chapters/norway/52-youth-participation-in-representative-democracy)) |
| **Japan** (2016, lowered from 20) | National elections | ~2.4 million new voters (~2% of electorate); 18-year-old turnout 51.17% in 2016 House of Councillors election, above the 40–44 bracket (50.30%); invalid-vote rate fell; no aggregate electoral impact ([East Asia Forum 2017](https://eastasiaforum.org/2017/10/21/what-happened-when-japan-lowered-the-minimum-voting-age/)) |
| **US municipalities** (Takoma Park MD 2013; Hyattsville MD 2015; Oakland/Berkeley CA 2024) | Local and school-board elections | Takoma Park: 44% turnout among registered 16–17-year-olds vs 10% overall in 2013, rising to 69% by 2020 vs 54% general; registration low ([SELC/Generation Citizen](https://selc.senate.ca.gov/sites/selc.senate.ca.gov/files/final-md-case-study_generation_citizen_vote16usa.pdf); [Vote16USA](https://vote16usa.org/after-election-1st-heres-how-oakland-and-berkeley-youth-turned-out/)) |

The pattern is consistent: 16- and 17-year-olds vote at rates comparable to or higher than older first-time voters, and there is no evidence of the predicted harms to vote quality or electoral outcomes. The JRRT synthesis concludes that 16- and 17-year-olds "are qualified to exercise their right to vote" and reports "repeatedly" that the reform produces no negative outcomes ([Huebner & Eichhorn 2025](https://www.jrrt.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Votes-at-16-report-FINAL.pdf)).

Two qualifications matter. First, preparation matters: Austria's schools' impact on political interest increased after the reform, and accompanying civic-education measures helped raise turnout and knowledge, implying that without such preparation the gains would not materialize ([Zeglovits, FEPS](https://feps-europe.eu/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Lowering-the-Voting-Age-16-%E2%80%93-what-other-countries-can-learn-from-Austria.pdf)). Second, the Welsh "false start" shows that implementation, timing, and salience shape outcomes: the first Senedd election under the new franchise was held in 2021 during the COVID-19 pandemic, and a Wales Governance Centre evaluation found low registration and turnout. The full report was not accessible for verification, so its specific figures could not be independently confirmed, but its central finding points to the role of delivery.

### Habit Formation and Long-Run Effects

The case for voting at 16 rests partly on habit formation: if the first opportunity to vote occurs during a period of stability — when young people are still in the parental home and in full-time education — the habit is more likely to take root than if the first election coincides with the residential, educational, and occupational instability of the early twenties. Mark Franklin's *Voter Turnout and the Dynamics of Electoral Competition* (Cambridge, 2004) develops this argument most fully, contending that the lowering of the voting age from 21 to 18 in the 1960s–70s inadvertently contributed to long-term turnout decline because 18-year-olds typically experience their first election during a transitory life stage ([Franklin 2004, discussed in Eichhorn & Huebner 2025](https://www.cogitatiopress.com/politicsandgovernance/article/download/9283/4184)).

Alexander Coppock and Donald Green's 2016 study estimates that the average effect of voting in one election on the probability of voting in a future election is approximately 10 percentage points, using both randomized GOTV experiments and regression discontinuity designs exploiting the voting-age eligibility cutoff at 18 ([Coppock & Green 2016](https://ajps.org/2015/09/28/measuring-the-habit-forming-effects-of-voting/)). The experimental evidence is striking in its persistence: in a 2006 Michigan social-pressure experiment, a 5-percentage-point treatment effect persisted for at least six years, with Coppock and Green estimating the effect of voting itself on future turnout among "compliers" at approximately 16 percentage points for that experiment.

Dinas et al. (2024) challenge the view that only salient first elections produce lasting habit effects, showing instead that all elections should affect subsequent turnout in elections of the same type. Individuals are more likely to vote in, and be interested in, elections of the same type as their first voting experience ([Dinas et al. 2024, *Political Science Research and Methods*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/early-voting-experiences-and-habit-formation/4E381E8D8092788B0A267EAFBE9039D8)). This suggests the mechanism is more contextual than a simple "first vote" hypothesis would predict.

The most direct test comes from Scotland. Eichhorn and Huebner (2025), using original survey data from 863 young people aged 16–31 collected seven years after the initial reform, found that young people who were eligible to vote at 16/17 exhibited significantly greater turnout in the 2021 Scottish Parliament election than those who got to vote for the first time at 18 or older. Crucially, they found no similar pattern for non-electoral political engagement: the effect appears to be specific to voting turnout ([Eichhorn & Huebner 2025](https://www.cogitatiopress.com/politicsandgovernance/article/download/9283/4184)).

This specificity matters. It suggests that voting at 16 may create habitual voters without necessarily creating more broadly engaged citizens — a finding that complicates claims about the reform's wider civic benefits. The durability of the effect beyond the first several elections also remains contested, though the seven-year follow-up in Scotland is substantial.

### Counterarguments: Knowledge, Influence, and the "Raise to 25" Proposal

The strongest empirical counterarguments — that 16- and 17-year-olds lack sufficient political knowledge, are mere conduits for parental or peer preferences, and vote irresponsibly — are not supported by the best available evidence. In Brazil, levels of political knowledge and media consumption are indistinguishable above and below the age of 18 ([UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)). After Austria lowered the voting age, Eva Zeglovits's review concludes that "16- and 17-year olds are not less politically mature than older first time voters" and that, in terms of political interest and political knowledge, "they do not differ from other young voters" ([Zeglovits, FEPS](https://feps-europe.eu/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Lowering-the-Voting-Age-16-%E2%80%93-what-other-countries-can-learn-from-Austria.pdf)).

On susceptibility to influence, UNICEF Innocenti cautions that adolescents may be susceptible to external influence but emphasizes that "voters of all groups, ages and education levels often base their political decisions on loyalties, identities, and peer influences," and "in voting, adolescents are influenced by a range of factors, but so are adults." Zeglovits reports that "there is no evidence that political parties have shifted their agenda towards young people's interests" in Austria and that "young people in Austria have proven to handle their voting rights responsibly."

The mirror-image proposal — raising the voting age to 25 on the ground that cognitive maturation continues into the mid-20s — is a normative argument whose empirical premise remains contested. UNICEF notes that "a significant proportion of scientists in the neurodevelopmental field have argued that lowering the voting age is in line with current evidence around adolescent brain development." The proposal also faces a consistency problem: no democracy applies individualized competence tests to 18- to 24-year-olds, so the argument implies either that the current minimum is also too low or that age is being used as a proxy rather than as a competence measure.

## The Maximum Voting Age: Cognitive Decline and Gerontocracy

### Cognitive Aging and Voting Capacity

Voting capacity is tied to specific cognitive functions — understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and expressing a choice — not to chronological age. The seminal study by Appelbaum, Bonnie, and Karlawish (2005) found that patients with very mild to mild Alzheimer's disease generally retained adequate ability to vote, while performance was highly variable among those with moderate disease and most persons with severe Alzheimer's did not. Critically, "the desire to vote was a poor predictor of voting capacity" ([Appelbaum et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263849/)).

Poptsi et al. (2022) examined 391 older adults using the Competence Assessment Tool for Voting (CAT-V) and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), finding that "only people who score more than 28 points on the MMSE have voting capacity." The CAT-V's discriminatory power varied by age group: accuracy was excellent for ages 51–65, good for 66–75 and 76–85, and perfect for 86–95 at a cutoff of 9.5. Notably, the 76–85 group showed only 64.7% specificity, meaning nearly a third of non-demented individuals in that band would be misclassified as lacking voting capacity if the CAT-V were used as a screening instrument ([Poptsi et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9776084/)).

Dementia prevalence rises steeply with age. The BrightFocus Foundation reports that nearly 19% of Black and 14% of Hispanic Americans aged 65 and older have Alzheimer's disease, compared with 10% of older white adults ([BrightFocus](https://www.brightfocus.org/alzheimers/facts-figures/)). The Alzheimer's Association estimates that about 1 in 9 Americans age 65 and older (11%) has Alzheimer's, a number projected to grow from 7.2 million today to 13.8 million by 2060 ([Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/alzheimers-dementia/facts-figures)). These figures mean that a substantial minority of older voters live with some degree of cognitive impairment — though the majority do not, and most retain voting-relevant capacity.

Normal cognitive aging differs fundamentally from dementia. Salthouse's processing-speed theory holds that age-related declines in "fluid" cognition — particularly processing speed and working memory — account for much of the observed age difference, while "crystallized" abilities such as vocabulary and general knowledge are relatively preserved ([Salthouse 1996](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1996-01780-001)). If voting draws primarily on crystallized knowledge and reasoned judgment, normal aging may not substantially impair it.

The asymmetry problem is acute: the minimum-age threshold presumes that competence must be demonstrated before voting rights are granted; the absence of a maximum age presumes competence until death. Yet no democracy applies individualized cognitive assessments to elderly voters. The American Bar Association Commission on Law and Aging states that "evaluating a person's capacity to vote outside the voting process itself is inappropriate and should not be done except by a court authorized to do so," and that "a medical diagnosis does not disqualify a person from voting" ([ABA, via Aging Wisdom](https://agingwisdom.com/the-right-to-vote-and-cognitive-impairment-updated-4/)). The functional test is deliberately modest: the capacity to vote is "much like the capacity to ride a bicycle, which can be determined accurately only by allowing the individual to mount a bike and start pedaling."

### Legal Frameworks and Capacity Assessments

No modern democracy imposes a universal cognitive screen on voters. In the United States, a dense federal framework — the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), the Voting Rights Act (VRA), the Help America Vote Act (HAVA), the National Voter Registration Act (NVRA), and the Voting Accessibility for the Elderly and Handicapped Act (VAEHA) — establishes a strong presumption that a diagnosis of cognitive impairment or dementia does not, by itself, disqualify a person from voting.

| Statute / instrument | Key protection for voters with cognitive impairments |
|---|---|
| ADA (Title II) | State and local governments "cannot categorically disqualify people with disabilities, including those with intellectual or mental health disabilities, from voting just because of their disability" ([ADA](https://www.ada.gov/topics/voting/)) |
| VRA § 208 | A voter who requires assistance may be given assistance by a person of the voter's choice, other than the voter's employer or union officer ([52 U.S.C. § 10508](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/52/10508)) |
| HAVA (2002) | Jurisdictions must provide at least one accessible voting system for voters with disabilities at each polling location ([Elville Associates](https://www.elvilleassociates.com/blog/voting-rights-cognitive-impairment-disabilities)) |
| NVRA (1993) | Offices providing public assistance or state-funded programs primarily serving people with disabilities must also offer voter registration ([ADA](https://www.ada.gov/topics/voting/)) |
| CRPD Article 29 | States must ensure voting procedures are "appropriate, accessible and easy to understand and use"; protect the right to vote by secret ballot "without intimidation"; and allow assistance by a person of the voter's own choice ([UN CRPD Article 29](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-29-participation-in-political-and-public-life.html)) |

The principal disenfranchisement mechanism is not a medical diagnosis but a legal status: state guardianship and conservatorship laws, which strip voting rights from some or all people under guardianship without an individualized inquiry into voting capacity. The National Disability Rights Network argues that laws barring people from voting based on guardianship or disability status, without an individualized inquiry, are "ripe for legal challenge" on Equal Protection, due process, VRA, and ADA grounds ([NDRN](https://www.ndrn.org/resource/voting-guardianship/)).

The leading judicial treatment is *Doe v. Rowe*, 156 F. Supp. 2d 35 (D. Me. 2001), in which a federal district court held that Maine's constitutional bar on voting by persons under guardianship for reasons of mental illness violated the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses. The provision remained in the constitution, however: a legislatively referred amendment to remove it was defeated at the November 7, 2023, election, after similar measures failed in 1997 and 2000 ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Maine_Question_8,_Repeal_Constitutional_Provision_Prohibiting_Voting_for_Individuals_Under_Guardianship_Due_to_Mental_Illness_Amendment_(2023))). The case illustrates both the constitutional vulnerability of automatic guardianship disenfranchisement and the political difficulty of curing it.

The expert consensus rejects diagnosis-based exclusion. The ABA Commission on Law and Aging and the University of Pennsylvania's Penn Memory Center state that a diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease, intellectual disability, stroke, or head injury "does not disqualify a person from voting," and that a person who "can indicate a desire to participate in the voting process and... can indicate a choice among available ballot selections... should be given the opportunity to vote and, if desired, appropriate assistance in voting" ([ABA/Penn Memory Center](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_aging/publications/bifocal/vol-42/vol-42--issue-1--september-october-2020-/assisting-cognitively-impaired-individuals-with-voting---a-quick/)).

Assistance is the operational bridge between inclusion and autonomy. Under VRA § 208 and CRPD Article 29(iii), assistance must come from a person of the voter's own choice. The decisive question is whether the voter can indicate a choice, not who helps. A caregiver who is "confident" that a spouse with dementia would vote Republican may not select that candidate; but if the voter, given the option, indicates the Republican candidate, the helper may assist in marking the ballot ([Mental Health and Aging](https://www.mentalhealthandaging.com/dementia-and-voting-faqs-about-dementia-voting-rights/)). This is the line between assistance and proxy voting: assistance effectuates the voter's expressed will; proxy voting substitutes the assistant's will.

### The Gerontocracy Concern

The gerontocracy argument holds that older voters are a growing share of the electorate, turn out at far higher rates than the young, and therefore command a rising share of electoral power — skewing policy toward the old at the expense of the young and future generations. The descriptive premise is strongly supported.

In the United States, the Census Bureau's 2020 Voting and Registration Supplement found overall turnout of 66.8% of citizens 18+, with the highest rate among those 65–74 (76.0%) and the lowest among those 18–24 (51.4%) — a gap of approximately 25 percentage points ([U.S. Census Bureau, 2021](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2021/2020-presidential-election-voting-and-registration-tables-now-available.html)). The UK shows a comparable gap: in 2019, estimated turnout was 47% among 18–24-year-olds versus 74% among over-65s — yet in 1964 the two groups voted at essentially identical rates (76.4% and 76.7%), showing that the gap is a modern phenomenon, not a constant of democratic politics ([Economics Observatory](https://www.economicsobservatory.com/what-do-we-know-about-voter-turnout-in-parliamentary-elections)).

| Election | Youngest cohort | Oldest cohort | Turnout gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| US 2020 (CPS) | 18–24: 51.4% | 65–74: 76.0% | ≈25 points |
| UK 2019 (Ipsos MORI est.) | 18–24: 47% | 65+: 74% | ≈27 points |
| UK 1964 (historical baseline) | 18–24: 76.4% | 65+: 76.7% | ≈0 points |

Because older cohorts both vote more and are larger than the youngest cohorts, their share of the electorate exceeds their share of the voting-age population. In the US, the 65+ group made up 23% of the citizen voting-age population in 2020 but 26% of all voters ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2021/04/record-high-turnout-in-2020-general-election.html)). Dr. Craig Berry's Intergenerational Foundation report calculates the "potential" and "actual" voting power of each single-year age cohort, projecting that in 2051 the average cohort aged 58–62 would number 937,000, against only 825,000 18-year-olds ([Berry, IF report](https://if.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IF_Democratic_Deficit_final.pdf)).

But the inference from "older voters are numerous and active" to "rule by the old" is contested. Older voters are not a cohesive generational bloc; they are divided by class, race, and ideology. The British Election Study finds that "the relationship between age and vote choice in 2019 is partly one of continuity... and partly one of common trends — where the level of support changed... it did so in similar ways across age groups," even though age was one of the most important predictors of the vote ([British Election Study](https://www.britishelectionstudy.com/bes-findings/age-and-voting-behaviour-at-the-2019-general-election/)). The *Conversation*'s analysis of British Social Attitudes data shows that while younger people shifted sharply left on inequality between 2016 and 2022, older people barely moved — yet older people are *more* supportive of increased taxation and spending (67% versus 43% among the young) ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/age-not-class-is-now-the-biggest-divide-in-british-politics-new-research-confirms-213699)).

James Chappel's review-essay ["The Myth of Gerontocracy"](https://bostonreview.substack.com/p/the-myth-of-gerontocracy) argues that "we don't live in a gerontocracy but an oligarchy, and a focus on age can only distract from that more existential threat." The accessible excerpt does not develop the full empirical case, but the thesis is a direct challenge to Samuel Moyn's framing. The strongest defensible version of the concern is therefore not that a monolithic elder bloc dictates policy, but that the *electoral weight* of older cohorts — compounded by their greater wealth and organization — tilts the agenda toward short-term preservation and away from long-horizon investment.

### Arguments For and Against a Maximum Voting Age

No modern democracy has established a maximum voting age, and the normative case remains analytically thin. The strongest scholarly treatment concludes that political equality cannot robustly justify either blanket disenfranchisement of older citizens or partial age-weighting of their ballots ([Poama 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14748851211062604)).

The case for a maximum voting age runs along four lines:

1. **Symmetry with minimum-age logic.** If societies may exclude citizens below a threshold age on the ground that they lack the cognitive capacities voting requires, consistency might seem to require excluding citizens above a threshold where those capacities decline.

2. **Gerontocracy and intergenerational power imbalance.** Older voters are an increasing share of the electorate and vote at higher rates. Astra Taylor's 2019 *New York Times* argument noted that people 65 and older are more than three times as likely as those under 30 to make political donations.

3. **Declining capacity.** Normal cognitive aging and dementia prevalence rise steeply with age. The Brennan Center notes that "about half of people over 80 suffer from dementia or other cognitive impairment" ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/age-limits-would-strengthen-congress)).

4. **Precedent of age limits on office.** Many democracies impose age limits on officeholders — minimum ages for candidacy and mandatory retirement ages for judges. Thirty-two states and the District of Columbia impose maximum age limits for judges ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/age-limits-would-strengthen-congress)).

Yet these arguments face decisive objections:

**(a) Age is a poor proxy for cognitive capacity.** Capacity is highly variable across individuals, and a fixed upper age would exclude many voters who retain full capacity while failing to catch those whose capacity has declined. A maximum voting age would produce both over- and under-inclusion.

**(b) Equal citizenship and autonomy.** Voting is a fundamental political right of equal citizens. Revoking it at a threshold age treats older citizens as less than full members of the political community. The young are excluded *before* acquiring the franchise; the old would be stripped of it after a lifetime of exercising it. This distinguishes age-based disenfranchisement from competence-based exclusion of the young.

**(c) Administrative and due-process problems.** Determining which citizens have "declined" enough to lose the vote would require individualized capacity assessments — raising profound discrimination and due-process concerns. No jurisdiction applies cognitive screening to elderly voters, and doing so would invite abuse, inconsistent standards, and stigmatization.

**(d) The asymmetry may be principled.** Minimum-age thresholds exclude those who have not yet acquired civic capacities; maximum-age thresholds would revoke a right based on a presumed loss. The presumption of competence at the age of majority is a convention, but it has no upper bound because capacity is not reliably predictable from chronological age.

Poama and Volacu press the symmetry challenge on the most charitable ground — "equal opportunity of political influence" — and conclude that it cannot justify a blanket disenfranchisement of older citizens ([Poama & Volacu, *European Journal of Political Theory*, 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14748851211062604)). Two problems drive the result: first, *epistemic limitations* — there are "significant epistemic limitations to deciding the size of age-relative electoral weights"; second, *robustness* — equal influence might, "under some realistic circumstances, require increasing older citizens' voting weight." They distinguish a synchronic argument (disproportionate present influence) from a diachronic one (disproportionate influence over the future) and find both "hardly applicable and unrobust."

Public opinion data show strong support for maximum age limits, but the questions asked concern *officeholders*, not voters. A Change Research/TouchGrass national survey found majority support for maximum ages for Congress (83%), the presidency (79%), state governors (80%), and Supreme Court justices (78%) ([Change Research/TouchGrass](https://changeresearch.substack.com/p/voters-back-age-limits-for-government)). A 2023 Pew poll cited by the Brennan Center found 82% of Republicans and 76% of Democrats support maximum age limits for federal elected officials and Supreme Court justices ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/age-limits-would-strengthen-congress)). These findings indicate support for age limits on officeholders — a different question.

## Intergenerational Equity and the Representation of Future Generations

### The Theoretical Problem

Intergenerational equity — the principle that each generation holds the political community and natural environment in trust for those who follow — generates a distinctive problem for democratic design: future generations cannot vote, yet decisions made today profoundly shape their lives. The UN's Committee of Experts on Public Administration defines intergenerational equity as the principle that "institutions should construct administrative acts that balance the short-term needs of today's generation with the longer-term needs of future generations" ([UN/CEPA](https://publicadministration.desa.un.org/intergovernmental-support/cepa/intergenerational-equity)).

Edith Brown Weiss's foundational *In Fairness to Future Generations* (1989) developed intergenerational equity as a theory of international law, grounded in a trusteeship model: each generation holds the natural and cultural heritage in trust for future generations and must pass it on in no worse condition than it was received. John Rawls provided "the first systematic discussion of obligations to future people within a theory of justice," formulating the "just savings principle" that requires each generation to preserve just institutions and capital for its successors ([Meyer, "Intergenerational Justice," SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2019/entries/justice-intergenerational/)).

The core philosophical problem is a "permanent asymmetry in power-relations between living people and those who will live in the future": present generations can affect not only the conditions but "the very existence of future people (whether or not future people will exist), the number of future people (how many future people will exist), and the identity of future people (who will exist)" ([Meyer, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2019/entries/justice-intergenerational/)). Derek Parfit's non-identity problem poses a foundational challenge: because current policies affect who will be born, it is difficult to claim that future people are "worse off" under those policies — without the policies, they would not have existed at all. Most believe the problem can be overcome, for example by distinguishing between persons as types and as tokens and assigning rights to future persons as types.

### Policy Consequences of Age Skew

The age skew in contemporary electorates does appear to produce measurable policy biases on climate and fiscal policy, though the relationship is mediated by party systems and institutions.

The most robust evidence of generational policy-preference gaps comes from climate policy. A Pew Research Center survey of 13,749 U.S. adults (April 2021) found that Gen Z and Millennial respondents were substantially more supportive than older generations of phasing out gasoline-powered vehicles (56% and 57%) and of phasing out fossil fuel use entirely. Roughly two-thirds of Gen Z (66%) and Millennial (64%) adults opposed expanding offshore oil and gas drilling, compared with 46% of Baby Boomer and older adults. The gap was especially pronounced among Republicans: Gen Z Republicans were 30 percentage points less likely than Baby Boomer and older Republicans to favor hydraulic fracturing (44% vs. 74%), and about three times as likely to favor phasing out fossil fuels entirely (20% vs. 6%) ([Pew Research Center, 2021](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/05/26/key-findings-how-americans-attitudes-about-climate-change-differ-by-generation-party-and-other-factors/)).

On fiscal and social-policy preferences, the systematic review by Vlandas, McArthur, and Ganslmeier (2021) — covering nearly 150 articles — found that older people are distinctive in their "low levels of support for education spending" and in their higher electoral turnout. Population ageing is associated with reduced education spending and increased healthcare spending, both within and between countries. The effects on pension spending are "less clear": while ageing is associated with greater aggregate pension spending, individual pension entitlements appear less generous when demand is greater ([Vlandas et al., 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/187536/1/Ageing_and_the_economy_a_literature_review_of_political_and_policy_mechanisms.pdf)).

Mulkeen (2025) illustrates the fiscal-externalization mechanism with public-debt examples: the United States borrowed over $8 trillion to fund military operations in Iraq and Afghanistan, choosing debt over raising revenue from those who directly profited, while the financial burden was deferred to younger taxpayers. Similar patterns followed the 2007–08 bank bailouts and the COVID-19 pandemic borrowing ([Mulkeen, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)).

The case for a systematic pro-old policy bias should not be overstated. Three caveats emerge: age is a weak to moderate predictor of policy preference once party identification and ideology are controlled; the causal chain from demographic ageing to policy outcomes is understudied, with party systems and institutions as critical intervening variables; and chronological age is "a blunt instrument that does not reflect the diversity of people's experiences and needs" ([Population Europe/Age-It](https://population-europe.eu/research/policy-insights/what-intergenerational-fairness-should-look)).

### Institutional Mechanisms for Representing Future Interests

The representation of future generations may be better addressed through institutional mechanisms distinct from age-based electoral rules. A range of institutions have been proposed and, in some cases, implemented: parliamentary committees for future generations, intergenerational trust funds, deliberative mini-publics, climate banks, and ombudspersons for future generations. Finland, Wales, Singapore, and Uruguay have attempted such mechanisms in recent decades ([APA Blog](https://blog.apaonline.org/2025/02/12/democracy-and-future-generations-a-philosophical-minefield/)).

Wales enacted the Well-being of Future Generations Act 2015, requiring all 44 public bodies to work towards sustainable development goals and creating a Future Generations Commissioner with "name and shame" powers ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/how-to-create-a-government-that-considers-future-generations-148947)). However, a UK high court judge described the Act in 2019 as "vague, general and aspirational," and its enforcement mechanisms remain weaker than those of the Israeli model it drew from. Hungary's Commissioner for Future Generations (established 2008) saw powers later reduced; Israel's Commission for Future Generations held an "effective veto" on legislation but was disestablished in 2016.

The fundamental challenge is that traditional democratic representation "requires that representatives be authorized by and accountable to those they represent," but "future generations can neither authorize nor hold to account those who claim to represent them today" ([APA Blog](https://blog.apaonline.org/2025/02/12/democracy-and-future-generations-a-philosophical-minefield/)). Anja Karnein worries that without direct accountability mechanisms, representatives of future generations may "find speaking on behalf of such generations a convenient way to further their own agenda, rather than the interests of future people, who can't contradict them."

Climate litigation has begun to give legal force to intergenerational-equity concerns. In *Verein KlimaSeniorinnen Schweiz and Others v. Switzerland* (2024), the European Court of Human Rights found that Switzerland had violated Article 8 (right to respect for private and family life) by failing to take sufficient action against climate change, interpreting Article 8 to encompass a "right to effective protection by the State authorities from the serious adverse effects of climate change on lives, health, well-being and quality of life" ([Climate Case Chart](https://www.climatecasechart.com/document/verein-klimaseniorinnen-schweiz-and-others-v-switzerland_6852)). Germany's Federal Constitutional Court reached a parallel conclusion in *Neubauer v. Germany* (2021), though primary sources could not be verified in this review.

## Comparing Minimum and Maximum Age Limits: Consistency and Asymmetry

### The Symmetry Challenge

The charge of inconsistency is direct: if society may exclude 16-year-olds for lack of competence, why does it not screen 70-year-olds for the same competence? Alexandru Volacu's "Justice, Symmetry, and Voting Rights Ceilings" offers the first systematic critical assessment of arguments for restricting senior citizens' voting rights, reconstructing the case for a voting "ceiling" and finding it wanting ([Volacu, *Theoria*, 2021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/theo.12306)).

Poama and Volacu press the challenge on the most charitable ground — "equal opportunity of political influence over electoral outcomes" — and conclude that it cannot justify a blanket disenfranchisement of older citizens. Their argument is internal: they decline to appeal to dignity or status (which would beg the question against proponents) and instead show that the equal-influence rationale is "quasi-inapplicable and practically unrobust" across a relevant range of political contexts. First, *epistemic*: there are "significant epistemic limitations to deciding the size of age-relative electoral weights." Second, *robustness*: "equal influence might, under some realistic circumstances, require increasing older citizens' voting weight, and so cannot robustly vindicate disenfranchisement proposals."

Ethan Jerzak's "Age-Weighted Democracy" argues that the symmetry can be saved by reinterpreting political equality *diachronically* — across a person's life — rather than synchronically at each election ([Jerzak, *Philosophical Quarterly*](https://academic.oup.com/pq/advance-article/doi/10.1093/pq/pqag044/8760801)). On this view, equal political influence does not require equal vote weight at every election; it requires that each person have equal influence over the political decisions of their life as a whole. Because people live longer than the policies they help choose, rational agents behind a veil of ignorance would give more weight to their younger selves, and so vote power should decline with age. This is a genuinely egalitarian argument, and it engages the symmetry challenge more seriously than a simple "too old to vote" complaint. But it remains a philosophical proposal: no democracy has implemented it, and its weight schedule is unspecified in practice.

### Why Minimum Ages Are Universal and Maximum Ages Are Not

Four features explain the asymmetry:

1. **Historical path**: The franchise has been repeatedly expanded — to women, to the illiterate, and to 18-year-olds by the U.S. Twenty-Sixth Amendment (1971), which set a floor, not a ceiling. Before World War II, the voting age in almost all countries was 21 or higher; the shift to 18 occurred over three decades and was driven by military conscription, not by a scientific consensus about adolescent development ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age); [CRS Report LSB11286](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11286.html)).

2. **Presumed adult competence**: In the United States there is "no uniform age of expiry for voting rights — i.e., adults do not age out of voter rolls at age seventy. As a default, a voter may vote until death," and disenfranchisement for mental incompetency is imposed case-by-case, not by age ([Kordi](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/online-archive/personalized-enfranchisement)).

3. **Human-rights framing**: Instruments such as the ICCPR guarantee "universal and equal" suffrage without setting age cut-offs, and differential treatment must have a "reasonable and objective" basis; around 90 per cent of countries set the voting age at 18 or above, while adults vote without maximum age limits ([UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)).

4. **Bright-line convenience**: Age is an administrable, ex-ante rule, but a crude one — "a crude and imperfect surrogate for voter competency" ([Kordi](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/online-archive/personalized-enfranchisement)), and "as good a proxy for electoral competence as any" only because no better uniform rule exists ([Hamilton, *William & Mary Law Review*, 2012](https://scholarship.law.wm.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2505&context=facpubs)). The fact that no one proposes competence-testing 25-year-olds shows that the minimum is not an individualized competence test but a convention.

### The Bright-Line Problem and the Threshold-vs.-Proxy Distinction

Both a minimum and a maximum age are proxies for competence; the difference is that the minimum proxies for *acquisition*, which has a fairly clear developmental trajectory, while the maximum would proxy for *decline*, which is variable and poorly tracked by age. David Archard's "threshold vs. proxy" distinction captures the underlying issue: a threshold view treats a fixed age as marking the acquisition of capacity, while a proxy view treats age as a stand-in for the underlying competence — and the proxy view is vulnerable to evidence that the stand-in is inaccurate ([SEP, "Children's Rights"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rights-children/)).

A consistent competence-based framework would therefore replace the age proxy with individualized assessment at both ends — Kordi's "personalized enfranchisement," which would allow minors under 18 to be deemed competent through "a highly individualized process," treating age as "a minor input in a broader assessment." But this carries heavy costs: the American South "relied on the concept of voter competency to systematically disenfranchise southern blacks and poor whites via literacy tests and civics exams," and a personalized regime "would struggle to escape the gravitational pull of such a racist past." There are distributive concerns: "allocating rights based on individual's competency will lead to problematic distributive effects," potentially disadvantaging poor and minority voters ([Kordi](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/online-archive/personalized-enfranchisement)).

A consistent affected-interests framework, by contrast, would enfranchise everyone affected — supporting a lower minimum and no maximum. The asymmetry between the two ends is thus best defended not by a symmetry of rules but by a relevant moral difference: the minimum excludes people who are on a trajectory of acquiring the capacities voting presupposes and who will live longest under current decisions, while the maximum would exclude people whose capacity is individual and variable and whose status as equal citizens is unquestioned.

## Reform Options: Competence Thresholds, Age Quotas, Civic Juries, and Weighted Voting

The institutional reform menu sorts into two families with sharply different democratic profiles. Inclusion-expanding reforms — lowering the voting age, age quotas, citizens' assemblies, civic education, voting advice applications, and future-generations institutions — widen or enrich participation and are generally consistent with equal citizenship. Competence-restricting reforms — civic-knowledge tests, plural or weighted voting, epistocratic veto chambers, and individualized competence screening — seek to improve the epistemic quality of the electorate but carry a heavy historical burden of abuse and face serious equality and feasibility objections.

| Reform | Mechanism | Competence rationale | Inclusion / equal citizenship | Intergenerational fairness | Feasibility / abuse risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Civic-knowledge tests | Test voters; restrict franchise to passers | Direct competence screening | Low — literacy-test history; class/racial bias | Neutral | Low — easily manipulated |
| Plural / weighted voting | Extra votes by knowledge or age | Epistemic weighting | Low — violates equal rights | Positive if age-weighted | Low — equality objection |
| Age quotas / reserved seats | Reserved legislative seats for youth | Descriptive representation | Moderate — presence without franchise | Positive — youth voice | Moderate — tokenism risk |
| Citizens' assemblies / civic juries | Randomly selected mini-publics | Improves citizen input quality | Moderate — consultative | Positive — long-term issues | Moderate — capture risk |
| Lowering the voting age | Extend franchise to 16–17 | Habit formation; affected interests | High — expands inclusion | Positive — youth voice | High — implemented widely |
| Future-generations institutions | Commissioners / ombudsmen | Represents unborn interests | Neutral — no franchise effect | High — closes unborn gap | Moderate — accountability problem |
| Civic education | School-based civics instruction | Improves voter knowledge | High — inclusion-preserving | Positive — long-term engagement | High — low abuse risk; modest effects |
| Voting Advice Applications | Online voter–party matching | Reduces information costs | High — no restriction | Neutral | High — scalable; mixed evidence |
| Individualized competence screening | Case-by-case assessment for minors | Precise competence screening | Low — stigmatization; bias risk | Neutral | Low — due-process and abuse risks |

### The Literacy-Test Shadow

The historical record of competence-based exclusion is damning. In the post-Reconstruction American South, literacy tests and poll taxes were used systematically to disenfranchise African American voters: Mississippi's 1890 constitution combined a literacy test with a poll tax, and the Supreme Court upheld the scheme in *Williams v. Mississippi* (1898) ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/jim-crow-laws-black-vote)). Any competence-testing regime must contend with this legacy: the same instrument can be facially neutral and discriminatory in administration. Political-knowledge tests also correlate with education and socioeconomic status, so a "neutral" test risks replicating class and racial hierarchies.

Jason Brennan formulates the competence principle directly: "anyone or any deliberative body that exercises power over anyone else has an obligation to use that power in good faith, and has the obligation to use that power competently," and if they do not, "that's a claim against them having any kind of authority or any kind of legitimacy" ([Illing, *Vox*, 2018](https://www.vox.com/2018/7/23/17581394/against-democracy-book-epistocracy-jason-brennan)). In the *Vox* interview he proposed that everyone — "even children" — may vote, but that voters also complete "a quiz of very basic political knowledge," allowing officials to "statistically estimate what the public would have wanted if it was fully informed."

The most systematic objection is David Estlund's "qualified acceptability requirement": political authority is legitimate only if its coercive enforcement of law can be justified to all "qualified" points of view, and epistocracy is ruled out because it cannot be justified to those it excludes. Estlund's "qualified epistemic claim" holds that democracy is the best epistemic strategy *among those defensible in terms that are generally acceptable* — epistemically superior methods that are too controversial to ground legitimately imposed law are excluded ([Gerald Gaus, discussion](https://www.gaus.biz/Estlund.pdf); see also Estlund, *Democratic Authority*, Princeton, 2008). Pierpaolo Moraro's "Against Epistocracy" (*Social Theory and Practice* 44(2), 2018) argues that Brennan's inference from voter ignorance to the intrinsic injustice of democracy is too quick. Deliberative democrats extend this line: Cristina Lafont (*Democracy Without Shortcuts*, Oxford, 2020) argues against epistocratic "shortcuts" that bypass the deliberative participation of citizens, and Hélène Landemore (*Open Democracy*, Princeton, 2020) defends inclusive, sortition-based institutions on epistemic and egalitarian grounds.

Claudio López-Guerra's *Democracy and Disenfranchisement* presses the competence principle from the other direction. He argues that outcome-based (instrumental) claims fail to justify disenfranchising minors or the mentally impaired, and rejects the "Conventional Suffrage Doctrine" — the set of claims that minors, the mentally impaired, non-citizen residents, convicted criminals, and the ignorant should be excluded ([López-Guerra, *Social Theory and Practice* 38(1), 2012](https://www.jstor.org/stable/23562157)). On the mentally impaired, he contends that we lack empirical evidence that they would be influenced by guardians, lack interest, or make random or poor choices, and that because many have the minimum necessary moral and cognitive capacity to vote, fairness requires their inclusion. The parallel is pointed: if the competence principle is taken seriously, it should apply to the mentally impaired as well as to the young — yet no democracy applies a routine individualized competence test to either group at the ballot box.

### Age Quotas and Reserved Seats

A less restrictive way to increase youth presence in representative institutions is age quotas or reserved seats. Rwanda reserves two Chamber of Deputies seats for members aged 35 or under, elected by the National Youth Council (Constitution, Art. 76, 2003) ([IPU Parline data](https://data.ipu.org/parliament/RW/RW-LC01/data-on-youth)). Uganda reserves five seats for youth representatives selected by the National Youth Delegates Conference. The theoretical basis is descriptive representation: Anne Phillips's *The Politics of Presence* (1995) argues that a "politics of ideas" is an inadequate vehicle for dealing with political exclusion and that the presence of disadvantaged groups in elected assemblies can improve representation ([Phillips](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Politics_of_Presence.html?id=KFuw2VZMlW0C)).

Age quotas are a partial remedy: they increase presence without expanding the franchise, and they risk tokenism if quota seats carry less power. They also do not answer the underlying question of whether 16- and 17-year-olds should vote.

### Civic Juries and Citizens' Assemblies

Deliberative mini-publics — citizens' assemblies, civic juries, deliberative polls — are designed to improve the quality of citizen input without restricting the franchise. James Fishkin's deliberative polling method brings a random sample of citizens together for informed deliberation. The Irish Constitutional Convention (2013) and subsequent Irish citizens' assemblies illustrate the model. Hélène Landemore's study of the French Convention citoyenne pour le climat (2019–2020) — 150 randomly selected citizens — found that participants acted as "de facto legislators," producing reasoned proposals and resisting capture by experts and organized interests; she characterizes the relationship as "citizens on top, experts on tap" ([Landemore](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/citizen-legislators-citizens-on-top-and-experts-on-tap-in-the-french-convention-for-climate-201920/4212574ABBFE12C5CC2ACE6193B23CA0)).

The distinction from voting is important: mini-publics do not aggregate preferences across the whole electorate; they produce recommendations or, at most, proposals for referendum. Cristina Lafont's *Democracy Without Shortcuts* (2020) critiques the delegation of decisions to mini-publics as a "shortcut" that bypasses broader democratic legitimation.

### Civic Education and Voting Advice Applications

If the concern is that voters lack knowledge, the most direct remedy is to improve knowledge rather than restrict the franchise. CIRCLE's analysis of a 2012 national survey of 4,483 young Americans (ages 18–24) found that 60.2% of those who recalled studying voting in high school turned out to vote, compared with 43% of those who recalled no civic education course; the relationship is correlational, not causal, but the association is strong ([CIRCLE](https://circle.tufts.edu/latest-research/high-school-civics-linked-voting-political-knowledge)). Civic education is thus a plausible, inclusion-preserving way to improve voting quality, though its effects on turnout are modest and confounded by socioeconomic advantage.

Voting Advice Applications (VAAs) are online tools that match voters to parties or candidates based on issue positions. Tromborg et al.'s study of a Danish candidate VAA (used ~2.5 million times in the 2019 election) found that users who received party-incongruent advice became 16 percentage points more likely to change their vote intention than users who received party-congruent advice; 63% of those who changed switched to the recommended party ([Tromborg et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-political-science-review/article/candidates-voters-and-voting-advice-applications/B4BBA53D4C023855DCA885A5B6F9E135)). VAAs are thus a promising information tool but not a proven remedy for voter ignorance at scale; their reach is concentrated in Northwestern Europe.

## Conclusion: Inclusivity, Informed Decision-Making, and Intergenerational Fairness

The debate over voting-age limits turns on four criteria: whether a limit is a reasonable proxy for the capacities voting requires; whether it respects equal citizenship and affected interests; whether it is administratively feasible and resistant to abuse; and whether it advances intergenerational fairness. Applied consistently, these criteria support lowering the minimum voting age to 16 — where real-world evidence from Austria and Scotland shows that 16- and 17-year-olds vote at rates comparable to or higher than older first-time voters — while counting against a maximum voting age or age-weighted voting, which fail on proxy validity, equal citizenship, and feasibility grounds.

**Minimum age limits.** The status quo of 18 fails the competence-proxy criterion at the margin. Developmental science finds that 16- and 17-year-olds possess the political maturity to vote, and that concerns about their ability are "generally not supported by developmental science" ([Oosterhoff, Wray-Lake & Hart 2022](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691621994221/)). The Austrian experience confirms that the "first-time voting boost" is stronger among 16- and 17-year-olds than among 18- to 20-year-old first-time voters, and that their turnout is not substantially lower than the average ([Zeglovits & Aichholzer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4864896/)). The Scottish longitudinal evidence shows that young people enfranchised at 16 or 17 exhibited greater turnout in the 2021 Scottish Parliament elections — up to seven years after the reform — than peers who first voted at 18 or older, though the effect did not extend to non-electoral political engagement ([Eichhorn & Huebner 2025](https://www.cogitatiopress.com/politicsandgovernance/article/download/9283/4184)).

Lowering the minimum to 16 satisfies the competence-proxy and equal-citizenship criteria: 16-year-olds are affected by political decisions and possess the requisite capacities. It advances intergenerational fairness modestly by bringing younger citizens into the electorate. It is administratively feasible, as the experience of Austria, Scotland, and about a dozen countries shows. The main caveat is implementation: the school and family context at 16 matters, and the Welsh "false start" is an important counterpoint. "Voting age zero" and proxy-voting proposals (such as Demeny voting, which would allow parents to cast proxy votes on behalf of their children) fail the administrative-feasibility and equal-citizenship criteria: proxy voting is paternalistic and would effectively give parents extra votes.

**Maximum age limits.** No modern democracy has established a maximum voting age; the idea circulates as a normative proposal rather than a governing practice. The case for one rests on symmetry with the minimum-age logic and on gerontocracy concerns. But the symmetry fails. Age is a poor proxy for cognitive decline: capacity is highly variable across individuals, and a fixed upper age would exclude many voters who retain full capacity while including many younger voters who do not. The competence-proxy criterion therefore counts against a maximum age, not for it.

The equal-citizenship criterion also counts against it. A maximum voting age would deny equal political status to a group defined by age. Poama and Volacu argue that the "equal opportunity of political influence" principle cannot ground a blanket disenfranchisement of older citizens, and that partial disenfranchisement via age-weighted voting is "quasi-inapplicable and practically unrobust" across political contexts; there are significant epistemic limitations to deciding the size of age-relative weights, and equal influence might, under some realistic circumstances, require *increasing* older citizens' voting weight ([Poama & Volacu 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14748851211062604)).

Individualized competence assessment — testing voters for decisional capacity rather than using age — is the most defensible competence-based alternative, but it raises serious concerns about discrimination, due process, and administrative burden, and no democracy has adopted it for general elections. The ABA Commission on Law and Aging states that "a medical diagnosis does not disqualify a person from voting," reflecting a deliberate policy choice: the risks of disenfranchising competent voters outweigh the risks of allowing some incompetent voters to cast ballots.

**Balanced assessment.** The strongest case is for lowering the minimum to 16, paired with civic education and future-generations institutions. This combination satisfies the competence-proxy criterion (the evidence supports adolescent capacity), the equal-citizenship criterion (16-year-olds are affected by decisions), the administrative-feasibility criterion (it has been implemented in a dozen countries), and the intergenerational-fairness criterion (it brings younger citizens into the electorate). The case for a maximum age or age-weighted voting is weak: it fails the competence-proxy criterion (age is a poor proxy for decline), the equal-citizenship criterion (it denies equal status), and the administrative-feasibility criterion (it is quasi-inapplicable and unrobust).

The criteria are not applied consistently across the age spectrum, and part of the asymmetry is arbitrary — the minimum is a proxy and the maximum would be too, and the burden of justification has shifted over time. But the strongest consistency arguments for a maximum age fail on their own terms (epistemic, robustness), and the principles that support lowering the minimum do not support a maximum. A consistent framework would either individualize competence assessment at both ends — at significant cost to equal citizenship and administrative feasibility — or treat the franchise as a right of affected citizenship with no upper limit and address intergenerational equity through representation mechanisms. The latter course is preferable: it respects equal political membership while acknowledging that the unborn cannot be represented through the franchise and require separate institutional guardians.

**Evidence gaps.** Several uncertainties remain. Long-run habit effects beyond the seven-year Scottish window are unknown. Cross-national replication of the Austrian and Scottish findings is limited. The effects of misinformation on adolescent voters are poorly understood. The Cardiff "False Start" findings need independent assessment. And no empirical study has tested the effects of a maximum voting age or age-weighted voting in any real electorate, so all claims about their consequences remain normative or speculative.
