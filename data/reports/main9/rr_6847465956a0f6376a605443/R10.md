# The Case for and Against Age Limits on Voting

## I. The Question in Plain Terms

The question is whether age should function as a suffrage criterion at all, and if so at which end of the lifespan. A minimum age excludes those deemed not yet ready to vote; a maximum age would exclude those deemed no longer competent. The two limits look like mirror images, but in practice they are handled very differently by every existing democracy, and the asymmetry between them is itself part of the answer.

Three things are often conflated and need to be separated upfront. First, *voting* (casting a ballot) is not the same as *candidacy* (running for office); the question here is about voters. Second, a *compulsory-voting exemption* is not a *suffrage restriction* — Argentina, Brazil and Peru release citizens above 70 from the legal duty to vote, but the right remains untouched. Third, age is being used in the question as a *proxy for cognitive capacity*; that is the load-bearing assumption, and it deserves scrutiny at both ends of the lifespan.

The empirical record for evaluating both ends of the age axis is uneven. For the minimum, we have twenty years of natural experiments: Austria lowered to 16 in 2007, Scotland enfranchised 16- and 17-year-olds for the 2014 independence referendum, Wales followed in 2021, and the UK government announced a plan to extend the franchise to 16 for all UK elections in July 2025 [1]. For the maximum, we have essentially no natural experiments at all: no modern democracy has ever imposed a hard upper voting age, and the *compulsory-voting* exemption cases are the closest approximations, with their critical legal-and-ethical distinction. The debate on the upper end therefore lives mainly in political theory, satire, and serious academic proposals that have never been enacted.

## II. The Historical Settlement

For most of the 20th century, the democratic world's voting age was 21. The 21-to-18 shift happened in a wave between roughly 1969 and 1975, driven in most countries by the Vietnam-era argument that if 18-year-olds were old enough to be drafted, they were old enough to vote. Czechoslovakia had led earlier, lowering to 18 in 1946, but the global move was concentrated in the early 1970s: the UK Representation of the People Act 1969, the US 26th Amendment in 1971, France in 1974, Germany in 1970, Australia in 1973, Canada in 1970 [2]. By 1980, 18 was effectively the global standard.

The 21-to-18 transition was not based on new evidence that 18-year-olds had suddenly become more cognitively mature. It was based on a consistency-of-rights argument and a moral argument about the burdens of citizenship. That historical context matters because it shows the lower limit is not a scientific threshold but a *political settlement*, and political settlements can move.

The next move is now underway. Austria became the first EU member to set 16 as the voting age for all elections in 2007 [3]. Brazil, Cuba, Ecuador, Nicaragua and Argentina allow voting at 16 (in Argentina, Brazil and Ecuador the 16-17 vote is *optional*, not compulsory, and the elderly are exempted from the duty to vote as well) [4]. Wales enfranchised 16- and 17-year-olds for Senedd elections in 2021, and on 17 July 2025 the UK Labour government published a strategy paper that would extend the franchise to 16 for all UK elections; the Representation of the People Bill implementing that change was introduced in the House of Commons on 12 February 2026 [1][5]. The direction of travel at the lower end is downward; the direction of travel at the upper end is, in practice, nowhere.

## III. The Minimum-Age Debate

The arguments for keeping or raising a minimum voting age cluster around three ideas. The first is cognitive: the human brain, especially the prefrontal cortex, continues to develop into the mid-20s, with the most demanding executive functions — impulse regulation under emotional pressure, weighing of long-term risks — stabilising around age 25 [6]. The second is life-experience: at 16 most citizens are still in full-time education, have not yet held jobs, paid significant tax, or signed contracts. The third is informedness: surveys of political knowledge show that the youngest voters consistently score lowest. The 2007 Pew Research survey of US public-affairs knowledge found that only 15% of 18-29-year-olds fell in the highest-knowledge third of the sample, compared with 47% of those aged 50-64 and 43% of those 65 and older [7].

The arguments for lowering or abolishing the minimum are equally serious. The developmental science cuts differently when the *specific* cognitive demands of voting are isolated. The developmental psychologist Laurence Steinberg distinguishes between "hot" cognition (reasoning under emotional arousal or peer pressure) and "cold" cognition (reasoning in calm, deliberative conditions). The "hot" system continues to mature into the early 20s; the "cold" system is well developed by 16. Voting is overwhelmingly a cold-cognitive act: it is done in private, often days after media exposure to arguments, without time pressure [8]. The consistency argument also bites back: in most democracies, 16-year-olds can work, pay income tax, consent to medical treatment, leave school and (in some jurisdictions) marry or join the armed forces. Taxing people without representation is the canonical democratic offence.

The empirical record from the jurisdictions that have actually lowered the age is, on balance, reassuring. Scotland's Electoral Commission reported that in the 2014 independence referendum, 75% of 16- and 17-year-olds claimed to have voted, compared with 54% of 18-24-year-olds; 109,593 16- and 17-year-olds registered, around 89% of those eligible; and 97% of those who voted said they would vote again [9]. Researchers at Edinburgh and Vienna studying the Scottish and Austrian cases found that 16- and 17-year-olds displayed political preferences just as diverse as older voters, that the enfranchisement did not change election outcomes, and that first-time voters at 16 turned out at higher rates than first-time voters at 18, suggesting the voting habit forms earlier when it forms at all [10][11]. In Austria the 2008 national election saw an 88% turnout among newly enfranchised 16-year-olds; that figure fell to 63% in 2013, but rebounded in 2017 to above the 18-19 turnout, and the longitudinal evaluations by Zeglovits and Aichholzer found that political knowledge among 16- and 17-year-olds in Austria was comparable to that of slightly older Austrians and that interest *increased* after enfranchisement [10][12].

The "brain matures at 25" claim that anchors the cognitive case for a higher minimum is real but overstated. The relevant evidence is that the prefrontal cortex continues to *refine* through the early 20s, not that it is *off* at 17 and *on* at 25. Jay Giedd, whose NIMH imaging studies are usually cited for the 25 figure, has himself said that the number is a "nice-sounding" heuristic and that brain development is a gradient, not a switch [6]. Steinberg, the leading proponent of the hot/cold distinction, has used the hot/cold split to argue *for* enfranchising 16-year-olds, on the ground that voting is cold-cognition work [8]. The strongest empirical conclusion is that 16-year-olds perform as well as slightly older voters on voting-relevant cognition, and that the most demanding emotional-regulation tasks (which justify keeping 18 as the age for things like alcohol, military service, and certain credit contracts) are not the central demands of voting.

## IV. The Maximum-Age Debate

The mirror-image case for an upper limit begins with cognitive decline. Fluid intelligence — the ability to solve novel problems, identify patterns, process complexity — peaks in the 20s and 30s and declines thereafter. Working memory and processing speed fall off measurably from the 60s, and the prevalence of dementia rises sharply with age. Depending on the source, dementia affects roughly 5% of those aged 70-74, around 16% at 80-84, and 30% or more above 90; Alzheimer's disease alone is present in roughly 10% of those over 65 and in more than a third of those over 85 [13]. Because voting requires memory, comprehension of complex policy trade-offs, and resistance to misinformation, the cognitive case for an upper limit is not a frivolous one.

The case is sharpened by the empirical finding that older voters are markedly more vulnerable to misinformation. Grinberg and colleagues' analysis of Twitter during the 2016 US election found that users over 50 accounted for the overwhelming majority of fake-news sharing, and that a tiny number of "supersharers" (the top 0.1% of users) generated 80% of the false-news cascade [14]. Guess, Nagler and Tucker found that Facebook users over 65 shared links to fake-news domains roughly seven times as often as the youngest users [15]. In the 2016 US election, the 65+ cohort turned out at 70.9%, against 46.1% for 18-29-year-olds [14]. The combination of higher turnout and higher susceptibility means that if misinformation is a problem for democratic quality, the upper end of the age distribution is where it is most concentrated.

A second, distinct argument is intergenerational. The political theorist Philippe Van Parijs, in a 1998 article in *Philosophy and Public Affairs*, proposed giving the under-60s one extra vote, or assigning an 18-year-old's vote a weight of 2 reduced by 1% per year of age, on the ground that those who will live longer with the consequences of today's decisions should have proportionally more influence over them [16]. The proposal has affinities with John G. Stewart's earlier, blunt suggestion in 1970 to disenfranchise citizens at age 70 or retirement, and with Christopher Buckley's 2007 satirical novel *Boomsday*, in which incentives for voluntary exit at 70 are floated as a solution to the fiscal burden of an aging Boomer population [17]. The Van Parijs version is more defensible because it does not strip the elderly of the vote outright; it just reduces its weight in proportion to the *time horizon* over which the voter will live with the consequences.

## V. The Case Against a Maximum

The case against any upper limit is, on inspection, the same as the case against any blanket exclusion. The strongest version rests on the all-affected-interests principle: those whose interests are affected by political decisions ought to have a say in those decisions. Most 80-year-olds have interests — pensions, healthcare, property, taxation, family — that are deeply affected by political decisions; the cognitive-decline statistics describe a *trend*, not a threshold, and most 80-year-olds are not demented. Blanket exclusion by age would disenfranchise a great many competent citizens alongside the smaller number whose capacity has genuinely failed.

The historical analogy is uncomfortable for supporters of age limits of either kind. Every previously accepted blanket exclusion from the franchise — by property, by sex, by race, by literacy — was eventually recognised as unjustified, and the burden of proof came to rest on those who would still defend the exclusion. The default in modern democratic theory is universal suffrage; an age-based exclusion is the only such exclusion that remains widespread and uncontested. The fact that the older group is also the group that is most reliably politically engaged (turnout in the 65+ band exceeds turnout in the 18-29 band in essentially every established democracy) makes the move to disenfranchise them look less like a competence adjustment and more like the votes of people whose interests we don't like being excluded from the count.

There are also practical and principled counter-arguments specific to the cognitive case. First, age is a poor proxy for capacity; a 70-year-old with intact cognition is indistinguishable in voting-relevant performance from a 50-year-old, and a 90-year-old with mild cognitive impairment may still grasp a ballot question better than a 16-year-old. Second, the obvious alternative — individual capacity testing — is itself arbitrary, discriminatory, and politically dangerous; in practice it would be used against the poor, the badly educated, and minorities. Third, dementia is an *individual* diagnosis, not an age-bracket condition, and the medical and legal infrastructure for managing it (guardianship, capacity assessments, proxy voting in narrow cases) already exists without changing the universal franchise.

The closest empirical analogues are not hard disenfranchisement but the *compulsory-voting exemption* cases. Argentina, Brazil, Peru and Ecuador make voting optional for those above 70 (Ecuador uses 65; Paraguay 75); Greece makes it optional above 70 [4]. In Brazil's 2024 municipal elections, around 15.2 million voters were in the voluntary bracket — overwhelmingly the over-70s, with 16-17-year-olds and illiterate voters as the smaller categories. The exemption is a *release of legal duty*, not a removal of the right to vote; citizens in those brackets can still vote, and many do. The exemption also carries a substantive democratic meaning different from disenfranchisement: it acknowledges that older citizens have, in many cases, already discharged the civic duty of a lifetime of voting, and that *forcing* them to the polls in their 80s and 90s serves no democratic purpose. This is a *narrow*, defensible adjustment that does not challenge universal suffrage. It is not a precedent for a hard age ceiling.

## VI. The Symmetry Problem

The most analytically interesting feature of the debate is that the case for a minimum and the case for a maximum are structurally identical. Both rest on the same logical move: *age is a proxy for cognitive capacity to vote*. Both invoke the same body of evidence — developmental science at the lower end, age-related cognitive decline at the upper end. Both propose to *exclude* from the franchise a group of people who, in the empirical aggregate, perform somewhat worse on some voting-relevant cognitive measure than the median voter. If the minimum is defensible, the maximum should be too; if the maximum is not, the minimum is on shakier ground than it appears.

The asymmetry in political acceptability — virtually every democracy accepts a minimum, none accepts a maximum — therefore requires an explanation that is *not* about cognitive science. Three explanations are available. The first is *temporal asymmetry*: we can watch children become adults, and the slope is gradual and predictable; we cannot watch adults become impaired, and the slope is variable and individual. The second is *direction of cognitive error*: the case against the very young is that they will not adequately weigh long-term consequences, but the same critique is made of the very old — *drill, baby, drill* voting is the canonical example. The third is *interest-group politics*: the elderly vote, organise, and lobby; the very young do not, and the costs of excluding them are paid by people with no political voice.

The first explanation is the most honest. Age-based capacity proxies work best where the developmental trajectory is steep and predictable, and worst where it is gradual and individual. The minimum exploits the steep part; the maximum would be imposed on the gradual part. That is a real distinction, but it is a *prudential* distinction about which end of the lifespan a crude age proxy works least badly on — not a principled distinction about whether age is a legitimate suffrage criterion at all.

## VII. Intergenerational Equity as a Distinct Problem

Even if the maximum-age debate is rejected, the *intergenerational-equity* problem that motivates much of it is real. The demographer Anne Goujon's 2026 analysis in *Science* projects that in Italy — the starkest case, but a leading indicator for other aging democracies — voters aged 50 and over will outnumber voters aged 18-49 by 2050, having been a clear minority in 1950 and a roughly even split in 2000 [18]. The shift is driven by sustained sub-replacement fertility (Italian fertility was estimated at 1.14 children per woman in 2025) and rising longevity. Many younger Italian residents hold foreign citizenship and are ineligible to vote, which makes the under-50 political weight even smaller than the demographic share suggests.

Goujon's analysis names the resulting problem "dejuvenation": the progressive reduction of the demographic, economic, cultural and political weight of younger generations in aging societies. The political effects are not subtle. Politicians facing aging electorates over-allocate to pensions, healthcare for the elderly, and short-term consumption, and under-allocate to education, climate adaptation, debt reduction and the long-term investments that disproportionately benefit the young. The decisions being made today about AI governance, climate targets, public debt trajectories, and infrastructure will shape the lives of people who will live the longest with them, but those people have the smallest voice in making them.

The temptation is to read this as an argument for a voting-age *adjustment* — lower the minimum, raise the maximum, weight the votes — and so bring the electorate's age profile into better balance with the population's. But that move conflates two distinct problems. The real problem is not that the median voter is too old; it is that the *structure* of democratic incentives rewards short-term electoral gain and penalises long-horizon investment, regardless of which age cohort is voting. Lowering the minimum to 16 would, on the best available projections, add at most a few percentage points of under-30 share to the electorate — nowhere near enough to outweigh the gerontocratic tilt of turnout rates. Capping at 70 would shrink the elderly share of the vote by a much larger fraction, but only by removing a great many competent voters and without addressing the deeper institutional problem.

The mechanisms that scholars have proposed for the deeper problem are different in kind. Age-weighted voting (Van Parijs), age-targeted referendums on irreversible decisions (Campos 2024), parliamentary bodies for future generations, constitutional mandates for intergenerational equity, and bodies like the Welsh Well-being of Future Generations Commissioner [18][19]. These proposals have a common shape: they accept that the *franchise* cannot easily be re-engineered without violating the universal-suffrage norm, and they try to build long-horizon representation *outside* the franchise. The most defensible answer to the intergenerational-equity problem is therefore not to tinker with the voting age but to build new institutions around it.

## VIII. Inclusivity, Informedness, and What Age Actually Predicts

The trade-off between inclusivity and informed decision-making looks, on a quick reading, like a strong case for excluding the least-informed cohorts. But the empirical relationship between age and political knowledge is not what the trade-off assumes. The youngest cohort is the *least* informed, as Pew's 2007 data show; the most-informed cohort is *not* the elderly, but the 50-64 band; the 65+ band slips back slightly below the 50-64 band, possibly because retirement, health, and lifestyle changes reduce news exposure [7]. The relationship is curvilinear, with a peak in late middle age and decline on both ends. Excluding the bottom of the curve at the upper end is no more empirically defensible than excluding the bottom of the curve at the lower end.

The more telling finding is that the strongest predictor of political knowledge is not age but *education* and *engagement*. Pew's 2007 data showed that 63% of college graduates fell in the high-knowledge third of the public, against 20% of those with high school education or less — a far larger gap than any age gap [7]. The Pew-Knight Initiative's more recent engagement research finds that "Mobilizers" (the most politically active) score 72% correct on political-knowledge questions, against 41% for "Outsiders" (the least engaged), and that within each engagement tier the older cohort does better than the younger, but the engagement gap dwarfs the age gap [20]. A similar pattern appears in the misinformation literature: the relevant moderators are digital literacy, analytic-thinking disposition, and accuracy motivation — not age, except through its correlation with digital literacy [15][21].

The implication is that the inclusivity-vs-informedness trade-off is more accurately framed as a *political-engagement* trade-off. A democracy can either expand the franchise and accept that some less-engaged citizens will vote, or restrict the franchise to the most-engaged and accept that many citizens will be excluded from a decision that affects them. The historical trajectory of every successful democracy has been the first option, on the lesson that blanket exclusions are eventually recognised as unjustified. The same lesson applies, mutatis mutandis, at the upper end: cognitive impairment, where it exists, is a medical and legal question handled by guardianship and capacity law, not a question for the franchise.

## IX. Synthesis

The minimum voting age, on the available evidence, should sit at 16 rather than 18. The cold-cognitive demands of voting are well within the capacity of 16-year-olds, the natural experiments in Scotland, Austria and Wales have not produced the harms the opponents predicted, and first-time voters at 16 develop stronger lifelong voting habits than first-time voters at 18. The "old enough to fight, old enough to vote" logic that drove the 21-to-18 shift has a 21st-century analogue in the consistency of taxation, employment, medical consent, and (in some jurisdictions) military service at 16; the UK government's 2025 announcement and the Representation of the People Bill introduced in February 2026 are a plausible next step, and the Austrian data suggest the long-term democratic effect is likely to be modestly positive.

A maximum voting age is not defensible. It is unprecedented in the modern democratic world, it would disenfranchise a great many competent citizens to address a problem (severe cognitive impairment) that is both rarer and more individual than the 21st-century cognitive-decline statistics suggest, and it would do so using a proxy (age) that the same body of evidence condemns at the lower end. The only upper-age adjustment that has empirical purchase is the *compulsory-voting exemption* in countries like Argentina, Brazil, Peru, Ecuador, Greece and Paraguay — and that is a release of legal duty, not a removal of the right. The exemption is a sensible narrow accommodation; the disenfranchisement is not.

The harder question, intergenerational equity, is real and worsening, and age limits — of either kind — are not the right answer. The Italian 2050 projection is a warning, not a solution template. The most promising direction is institutional: parliamentary bodies for future generations, age-weighted referendums on irreversible decisions, constitutional mandates for intergenerational equity, and structural reforms (chamber reform, campaign-finance reform, lower thresholds for third-party access) that weaken the connection between age and political voice without touching the franchise. A democracy that lowers its minimum to 16 while building serious future-generations institutions has a better answer to the aging-electorate problem than one that leaves the franchise at 18 and waits for the median voter to drift into their 60s.

The deepest finding of the debate is therefore negative: age is a crude, status-based proxy for the capacity to vote, and it should be used as sparingly as possible. It has a defensible role at the lower end, where developmental science and the empirical record point to 16 as adequate. It has no defensible role at the upper end, where the proxy is weakest and the principle of universal suffrage strongest. And it has no role at all in the problem it is most often invoked to solve, which is a structural gerontocratic bias in democratic incentives that only institutional reform can address.
