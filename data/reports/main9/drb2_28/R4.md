# Energy Recovery Technologies in Urban Water Supply Systems: Conventional Micro-hydroturbines versus Pump-as-Turbine (PAT)

*Scope and key terms.* This report compares purpose-made micro-hydroturbines with Pump-as-Turbine (PAT) systems for energy recovery in urban water supply and distribution networks, using publicly available information up to early 2024. The **best efficiency point** (BEP) is the head/flow condition at which a machine reaches maximum hydraulic efficiency. **Specific speed** (Ns) is a dimensionless index that classifies a machine by its head–flow geometry and shapes the steepness of its efficiency curve; a **hill chart** is the envelope of efficiency contours in the head–flow plane. A **pressure-reducing valve** (PRV) is the conventional throttle that in-pipe hydropower replaces or assists. **Hydraulic** efficiencies refer to the runner; **wire-to-water** efficiency is lower once generator, drive and power-electronics losses are included. **CAPEX** is capital expenditure; **O&M** is operation and maintenance; **LCOE** is levelized cost of energy.

## 1. Detailed comparison of conventional micro-hydroturbines and PATs

### 1.1 Operational efficiency: best efficiency point and partial-load behavior

**Finding.** The efficiency gap between a purpose-made micro-hydroturbine and a PAT is smallest at the BEP and widest at part load. A well-matched conventional turbine holds a high, flat efficiency curve across a broad flow range, whereas a PAT has a narrower high-efficiency island and derates more steeply as flow falls below design.

Purpose-made micro-turbines generally reach higher and flatter peak efficiencies than reversed pumps. The Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook reports that a Pelton turbine has a “potential efficiency of 90 percent, even in small hydro applications,” and that multi-jet Pelton machines “can operate very efficiently at flows as low as 10 percent of the design flow” ([Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)). The same handbook describes the Francis as having a “higher peak efficiency than other turbines” but a “narrow operating range,” the Crossflow as maintaining high efficiency over a very wide flow range, and the Turgo as less efficient than the Pelton but with a broad flow range ([Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)). The US Department of Energy confirms the impulse/reaction split and head-driven selection logic, noting that Francis turbines are commonly used at 130–2,000 ft (≈39.6–610 m) and Pelton turbines at very high head and low flow ([US DOE, “Types of Hydropower Turbines”](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines)).

PATs, by contrast, are standard pumps reversed. JICA’s design guide states that the maximum efficiency of a reversed pump “varies according to the type and size of pump, but is typically 40% to 80%” ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)). KSB, a pump manufacturer, reports that a double-entry volute-casing pump running in turbine mode reaches “approx. 85 %,” and that “in general, turbine efficiency corresponds to pump efficiency” ([KSB, “Turbine mode”](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)). Thus a pump whose pump-mode BEP efficiency is ~85% will give roughly the same turbine-mode BEP efficiency. Vendor pages often quote ~95% for a purpose-built turbine versus ~85% for a reversed pump; these are seller statements and are consistent with the general pattern that a custom runner outperforms a reversed pump.

A reversed pump does not reproduce its pump-mode BEP exactly. KSB states that “the flow rate and turbine head are always greater in turbine mode at BEP … than the flow rate and head are in pump mode at the same rotational speed,” while “turbine efficiency corresponds to pump efficiency” ([KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)). Fontanella et al. (2020), using a 34-pump experimental database assembled under the REDAWN project, found that “the BEP pump and turbine mode conditions are mainly correlated by the rotational speeds in pump and turbine mode,” and used this to derive new pump-to-turbine relationships ([Fontanella et al. 2020, *Water* 12(4):1175](https://www.redawn.eu/sites/default/files/Fontanella%202020%20Performance%20Prediction%20Model%20for%20PATs%20water-12-01175.pdf)). Liu et al. (2022) likewise note that “PAT efficiency is always slightly lower than that of pump,” which is why geometry optimization is needed ([Liu et al. 2022, *Frontiers in Energy Research* 9:818118](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2021.818118/full)).

Specific speed is the master variable in this translation. Fontanella et al. describe methods by Grover and by Hergt et al. that express turbine-mode performance as a function of PAT specific speed, and cite Barbarelli et al., whose selection procedure was tested on six pumps with specific speeds between 9 and 65 ([Fontanella et al. 2020](https://www.redawn.eu/sites/default/files/Fontanella%202020%20Performance%20Prediction%20Model%20for%20PATs%20water-12-01175.pdf)). In broad terms, low-specific-speed centrifugal PATs suit higher head and lower flow, mixed-flow PATs occupy the middle, and axial-flow PATs suit low head and high flow; the specific-speed band determines both the pump type and the shape of the efficiency hill.

The decisive difference is part-load behavior. KSB explains that a constant-speed PAT “is only designed for a defined volume flow rate and a defined pressure gradient. All other operating conditions must be controlled by the throttling elements. The energy potential is not fully exploited,” and that “efficiency drops very sharply with increasing speed,” with an operating limit of “approx. 1.5 times the volume flow rate at BEP” ([KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)). JICA likewise warns that “the range of flow rates over which a particular unit can operate is much less than for a conventional turbine” and that a PAT “requires a fixed flow rate” ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)).

Conventional turbines are explicitly designed to flatten the hill chart. Multi-jet Pelton machines remain efficient down to ~10% of design flow; Crossflow turbines use two or more inlet guide vanes to maintain efficiency over a wide flow range; and double-regulated (full) Kaplan turbines adjust both runner-blade pitch and inlet guide vanes to widen the high-efficiency region. Semi-Kaplan variants, with only one regulation mechanism, are “compromised when operating across a wide flow range” and are best where flow does not vary greatly ([Wyoming Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)). The practical consequence in a water distribution network is that a PAT sized for average demand will spend much of its life at part load, where its efficiency and pressure-control authority are weakest; Lydon’s case studies, cited in Liu et al. (2022), found that “up to 40% gross power potential can be recovered by using PAT,” but that recovery depends strongly on layout and operating strategy ([Liu et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2021.818118/full)). Giosio et al. (2015) describe a PAT micro-hydro test facility with incorporated inlet flow control, aimed at improving the operating range and hill-chart efficiency of a pump-as-turbine ([Giosio et al. 2015, *Renewable Energy*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148114008544)).

Because manufacturers publish only pump-mode curves, PAT selection relies on prediction methods. Fontanella et al. (2020) survey the main approaches: Stepanoff related pump- and turbine-mode flow, head and hydraulic efficiency as a function of pump efficiency; Childs related pump- and turbine-mode powers; Hancock modified Childs by assuming similar BEP efficiency in the two modes; Grover and Hergt et al. used PAT specific speed; Alatorre-Frenk and Thomas correlated BEP flow and head with pump efficiency; Derakhshan and Nourbakhsh used pump specific speed; Nautiyal et al. correlated horizontal-axis single-stage PATs with pump specific speed and BEP efficiency; Yang et al. calibrated velocity triangles and corrected the Derakhshan–Nourbakhsh relation; Tan and Engeda used specific diameter; and Barbarelli et al. proposed a four-phase selection procedure ([Fontanella et al. 2020](https://www.redawn.eu/sites/default/files/Fontanella%202020%20Performance%20Prediction%20Model%20for%20PATs%20water-12-01175.pdf)). Liu et al. (2022) group these into four categories—using BEP, using specific speed, loss modeling, and polynomial fitting—and report that “the prediction errors of published methods are within ±10% mostly” ([Liu et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2021.818118/full)). Williams (1994) provided an early comparison of eight prediction methods and analyzed the consequences of poor turbine prediction for PAT operation ([Williams 1994, *Proc. IMechE*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1243/PIME_PROC_1994_208_009_02)).

Blade modification can narrow the BEP gap. Nasir et al. (2024) report that “blade grooving and blade tip rounding are recognized as the most promising techniques for improving efficiency, with gains of 4.91% and 4.00%, respectively,” and that, except for impeller-diameter trimming, blade modifications “have a significant impact on the efficiency of PATs” ([Nasir et al. 2024, *Heliyon*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10881369/)). Liu et al. (2022) independently conclude that “the blade rounding and forward-curved impellers are the most effective and feasible techniques” among geometry-optimization options ([Liu et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2021.818118/full)). The economic case is weaker than the hydraulic case: Nasir et al. state that “further research is needed to investigate the economic advantages of impeller modification” ([Nasir et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10881369/)). This is consistent with the broader finding that the reduction in PAT efficiency relative to classic turbines “is well compensated by the modest installation and maintenance cost” ([Fontanella et al. 2020](https://www.redawn.eu/sites/default/files/Fontanella%202020%20Performance%20Prediction%20Model%20for%20PATs%20water-12-01175.pdf)).

Constant-speed PAT operation is simple but inefficient at off-design flow. Fontanella et al. (2020) list the standard flexibility measures—bypassing part of the flow, activating a PRV in parallel with the PAT, and varying the PAT’s rotation speed with an inverter drive ([Fontanella et al. 2020](https://www.redawn.eu/sites/default/files/Fontanella%202020%20Performance%20Prediction%20Model%20for%20PATs%20water-12-01175.pdf)). Carravetta et al. (2013) compared hydraulic regulation (HR) and electric regulation (ER) for PATs in a water distribution network and found that “the hydraulic regulation mode of the plant is better than the electric regulation mode for American Petroleum Institute (API) manufacturing standards of pumps” ([Carravetta et al. 2013, *Water* 5(3):1211](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211)). The exact efficiency recovery obtainable from variable-speed operation is site-specific and was not quantified in the accessible sources; it should be established by hydraulic modeling rather than assumed.

PAT instabilities and geometric parameters are reviewed by Graciano-Uribe et al. (2021), covering loss mechanisms and flow phenomena that degrade part-load performance ([Graciano-Uribe et al. 2021, *JSDEWES*](https://hrcak.srce.hr/270144)). The accessible text confirms the review’s scope—instabilities and geometric-parameter effects in micro-hydro PATs—but the specific quantitative loss ranges and instability thresholds could not be independently verified, so they are not restated here.

*Caveats.* The JICA 40–80% range is broad and explicitly conditioned by pump type and size; it should not be read as a typical value for a well-matched PAT, which KSB puts at ~85%. Vendor efficiency claims (including ~95% for purpose-built turbines) are seller statements. Exact part-load efficiency values at 50%, 30% and 20% of design flow for PATs and for each conventional turbine were not confirmed from accessible pre-2024 sources; the qualitative hill-chart comparisons above are the defensible evidence. Per-method accuracy for Al-Ttofiq, Schuldhenried, Dali, Whitmore, Knapp and Singh remains unverified; the ±10% error band reported by Liu et al. (2022) applies to published methods collectively.

### 1.2 Costs: procurement, installation, and operation & maintenance

**Finding.** In sub-100 kW urban water-network energy recovery, the electromechanical package is usually the smaller share of total project cost—civil works and pipework frequently dominate—so the PAT cost advantage is real but narrower than equipment-only comparisons suggest. PATs benefit from mass-produced standard pumps, which lowers procurement cost, shortens delivery and simplifies spare parts; conventional micro-turbines are typically custom-designed and carry higher electromechanical cost and longer lead times. O&M is a small percentage of CAPEX for both, yet it accumulates over a 20+ year life and can materially change the comparison. Payback is site-specific and cannot be inferred from machine cost alone.

Total project CAPEX comprises electromechanical equipment (turbine, generator, controller, valves), installation labour and civil works (intake, penstock, powerhouse, tailrace). Electromechanical components account for only 35–40% of total micro-hydropower plant cost, so civil works, piping and balance of plant typically dominate (Motwani et al., 2013, as cited in [Nasir et al. 2023, *International Journal of Sustainable Energy*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606)). The ORNL Small Hydropower Cost Reference Model reports installed capital cost (ICC) for small hydro of roughly $2,000–$7,500/kW, based on IEA-ETSAP 2010 data ([ORNL, 2012](https://info.ornl.gov/sites/publications/files/pub39663.pdf)). A harmonized review of 13 small-hydropower studies across four continents found a median installed cost of $2,655/kW and a mean of $3,609/kW, with a very wide spread (up to $11,502/kW) and total project net present value (NPV) from −$1.8 million to $30 million ([UNH EPSCoR small-hydropower cost review, 2022](https://scholars.unh.edu/nh_epscor/31/)). The spread reflects differences in capacity factor, discount rate, project lifetime and site conditions; the authors explicitly caution that inconsistent reporting limits meaningful cross-study comparison and that LCOE falls as plant capacity rises.

The principal PAT cost advantage is mass production. Standard centrifugal pumps are manufactured in large volumes in standard sizes, so a PAT can be bought off-the-shelf rather than custom-designed for the site. NCAT’s design guide estimates that “the cost of a PAT is 50% or less than that of a” conventional turbine ([NCAT, 2011](https://www.ncat.org/publication/micro-hydro-power-a-beginners-guide-to-design-and-installation/)). SPP Pumps, a pump manufacturer, states that PATs offer a “substantial economic advantage,” with return on investment “in as little as three years,” and that pumps and spare parts are more readily accessible than turbines ([SPP Pumps, 2022](https://www.spppumps.com/embracing-hydroelectric-power-the-concept-reality-of-pumps-as-turbines-pats/); vendor statement). The same source identifies the historical problem with conventional micro-hydro: non-availability of standardized equipment (normally Francis or Kaplan), site-specific design, and costly electromechanical packages. JICA’s PAT design guidance likewise emphasizes the mass-production advantage, short delivery and easy installation with standard fittings ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)).

Installation cost is driven less by turbine type than by site conditions—access, geology, penstock length and powerhouse construction. For in-pipe WDN installations, civil works can be smaller because the device sits in an existing pipe and may need only a chamber, bypass and connection; however, a bypass and isolation valves are still required for maintenance and pressure control. This means the PAT equipment saving may be diluted at the whole-project level, and the lowest-cost machine is not necessarily the lowest-cost installation.

O&M for small hydro is commonly reported as 1–6% of installed capital cost per year, and can be higher ([IRENA, 2012](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2012/RE_Technologies_Cost_Analysis-HYDROPOWER.pdf); [ORNL, 2012](https://info.ornl.gov/sites/publications/files/pub39663.pdf)). The US DOE Hydropower Market Report series documents substantial plant-to-plant variability in O&M costs within the same size category and age cohort, with small plants facing higher per-kW O&M than very large plants ([US DOE, 2023 edition](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/U.S.%20Hydropower%20Market%20Report%202023%20edition_20230928.pdf)). For PATs, O&M is generally comparable to or lower than for custom turbines because pump bearings, seals and spare parts are standard and widely available ([SPP Pumps, 2022](https://www.spppumps.com/embracing-hydroelectric-power-the-concept-reality-of-pumps-as-turbines-pats/)). Direct-drive PATs avoid belt losses and belt maintenance; JICA notes that a cross-flow installation, by contrast, may require a belt drive because the turbine runs slower than an equivalent PAT ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)).

LCOE and equivalent cost of power express total life-cycle cost per unit of electricity. In a rural-electrification comparison, Nasir (2023) reports an equivalent cost of power of 0.065 $/kWh for a PAT versus 0.100 $/kWh for a cross-flow turbine, based on projected system lifetime, and payback of 3.42 versus 6.04 years ([Nasir et al. 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606)). This is a rural case, not a WDN case, so the absolute values should not be transferred directly; the direction of the advantage (PAT cheaper per kWh) is consistent with the mass-production argument. In a real WDN application, Viana et al. (2023) replaced two PRVs with PATs in a Brazilian network and report a payback period of 2.1 years; the most efficient pump recovered 4,331 kWh/year ([Viana et al., 2023](https://www.rbciamb.com.br/Publicacoes_RBCIAMB/article/view/1551)). The payback is short because the PAT cost is low relative to the recovered energy value—but the recovered energy itself is small, illustrating that low machine cost does not automatically imply short payback if available power is small. Trade press and vendor sources report PAT payback “as short as three years” ([World Pumps, 2009](https://www.worldpumps.com/content/features/pumps-as-turbines-in-the-water-industry); [SPP Pumps, 2022](https://www.spppumps.com/embracing-hydroelectric-power-the-concept-reality-of-pumps-as-turbines-pats/)); these are vendor/trade claims and should be corroborated against site-specific modelling.

Novara et al. (2019) developed a cost model for PATs in run-of-river and in-pipe microhydropower that predicts PAT and generator cost from the nominal flow rate and available hydraulic head ([Novara et al., 2019](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/abs/10.1061/(ASCE)WR.1943-5452.0001063)). The model identifies flow rate and head as the primary cost drivers because they determine pump size and generator rating. It is useful for early-stage screening, but like all parametric cost models it should be calibrated to local equipment prices and does not capture site-specific civil works.

Adding a variable-speed drive (VFD) and power electronics increases capital cost and control complexity but can extend the operating range and improve part-load efficiency. Viana et al. (2023) found that constant-speed PATs could not maintain effective pressure control under variable demand and recommended variable-speed operation ([Viana et al., 2023](https://www.rbciamb.com.br/Publicacoes_RBCIAMB/article/view/1551)). None of the sources reviewed quantify the incremental CAPEX of a VFD for a sub-100 kW in-pipe installation, so this remains an evidence gap.

*Caveats.* The ORNL, IRENA, JICA, Nasir and Novara documents could not be opened in full in the underlying research; the figures attributed to them are as indexed in the public record and should be re-derived from the primary documents before design use. The DOE ~$0.05/kWh small-plant O&M figure and the ~5% direct-drive output saving could not be verified from fetched page content and are flagged as evidence gaps.

### 1.3 Pressure regulation capability

**Finding.** In urban water distribution networks, pressure control is the primary function and energy recovery is secondary: a turbine installed in place of a PRV must first preserve the downstream pressure the PRV was providing, and only then harvest energy. A PAT can match PRV pressure control, but because a standard centrifugal pump has no adjustable guide vanes or blade pitch, it needs external control hardware—a bypass and throttling valves, or a variable-speed drive—to regulate pressure across the highly variable flows typical of WDNs. Across the pre-2024 literature, hydraulic regulation (HR), in which a fixed-speed PAT sits in series with a control valve and a parallel bypass, is repeatedly found to be the best trade-off between energy produced and installation cost, whereas purely electric regulation (ER, variable speed) offers wider operating flexibility at higher cost and with efficiency penalties.

Carravetta et al. (2013) frame PRVs as “the cheapest and a simpler solution to reduce pressure,” and argue that “in the framework of a virtuous energy strategy, any PRV regulation should be converted in order to produce energy” ([Carravetta et al. 2013](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211)). The binding constraint is that the network must still be supplied: Lydon et al. (2017) state that “pressure within the water network must be maintained within a prescribed range downstream,” and note that PRV sites exhibit “highly variable flow … regularly in excess of ±50% of the mean flow rate,” which complicates both PAT sizing and pressure control ([Lydon et al. 2017, *Renewable Energy* 114B:1038–1050](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148117307498)).

Two principal PAT regulation modes are described in the literature. **Hydraulic regulation (HR)** uses a PAT rotating at fixed speed, installed in series with a control valve and a bypass line equipped with a second control valve; **electric regulation (ER)** uses a single generation line plus an inverter that varies the PAT rotational speed so that its head-loss curve shifts to follow the operating conditions; a **coupled hydraulic and electric regulation (HER)** scheme superimposes the two ([Mitrovic et al. 2022, *Environ. Sci. Proc.* 21(1):80](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-4931/21/1/80)).

The comparative evidence consistently favours HR on cost-effectiveness. Carravetta et al. (2013) conclude from a real-system application that “the hydraulic regulation mode of the plant is better than the electric regulation mode for American Petroleum Industry (API) manufacturing standards of pumps” ([Carravetta et al. 2013](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211)). Mitrovic et al. (2022) report that “the HR scheme is the optimal tradeoff between the effectiveness of the energy recovery and the installation costs” ([Mitrovic et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-4931/21/1/80)). The ASCE layout study by Fontana, Marini and Creaco (2021) compared HR, ER and HER across four case studies and found that “the hydraulic regulation-based installation layout represents the best trade-off between energy produced and installation costs,” while “the solely electrical regulation option exhibits poor flexibility for accommodating yearly demand variations” ([Fontana et al. 2021, *J. Water Resour. Plan. Manag.* 147(12)](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0001480)). The practical reason is that a centrifugal pump lacks adjustable or variable-pitch diffuser vanes, so external control devices are unavoidable in turbine mode; KSB notes that “the integration of a PaT … into a pressure control system is ideally performed via a bypass line” and that “external control devices (control valve I and II) are required in turbine mode” ([KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998); vendor statement).

Variable-speed (VFD/inverter) operation is the main route to widening the PAT operating range, because it shifts the head-loss curve rather than dissipating excess head across a throttle. KSB describes the mechanism: “if the system’s operating mode changes, i.e. if a lower flow rate is required, the rotational speed is increased; if, however, the volume flow demand increases, the speed is reduced” ([KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998); vendor statement). The trade-off is explicit: “this fact has a negative effect on the overall efficiency of the system. While efficiency drops very sharply with increasing speed, constant pressure reduction and thus a decreasing volume flow rate, the PaT’s operating limit is very quickly reached with increasing volume flow rate and thus a decreasing speed (approx. 1.5 times the volume flow rate at BEP)” ([KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998); vendor statement).

Souza, Mesquita and Blanco (2023) quantify the benefit in a real municipal network. At constant speed, “operation as a PCV occurs only in the flow close to the best efficiency point (BEP), impairing the supply in the network.” At variable speed, “the PAT maintains the best efficiencies (0.62 to 0.64) and power (3.44 kW) when flows are high and speeds are low (2,400 rpm at 11 am and 3,000 rpm at 6 pm),” so that “the pump outlet pressure and network throughput are maintained according to the values required by the PCVs” ([Souza et al. 2023, *Water Resour. Manag.* 37(3)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-022-03421-9)). The same study reports 270,192.19 kWh/year recovered, a 27-month payback, NPV of US$64,476.18 and IRR of 63% for the analysed PAT—but these are single-case results, not general performance guarantees ([Souza et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-022-03421-9)).

Purpose-made turbines regulate pressure through intrinsic hydraulic controls that a reversed pump lacks ([Wyoming Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)):

- **Pelton (impulse):** flow is metered by needle valves/spear valves on each jet, with a jet deflector for emergency diversion; multiple jets (up to six) maintain efficiency under variable flow. The deflector lets the needle valve close slowly, keeping overpressure surge “to an acceptable level.”
- **Crossflow/Banki (impulse):** two or more inlet guide vanes direct flow onto part of the runner at low inflow or the whole runner at high flow, sustaining efficiency over a wide flow range.
- **Francis (reaction):** adjustable guide vanes (wicket gates) with fixed runner blades; guide vanes can shut off flow in an emergency, and a butterfly valve on the inlet provides a second emergency shutoff.
- **Kaplan (reaction):** full/double-regulated machines adjust both runner-blade pitch and inlet guide vanes, giving the widest flow range; semi-Kaplan variants adjust only one of the two, compromising wide-range performance but costing less where flow is stable.

The key point for network integration is that these machines regulate flow and pressure inside the turbine, whereas a PAT must add external valves or a drive to achieve the same effect.

Reaction turbines can be used in-line and hold downstream pressure; impulse turbines generally cannot. The Wyoming handbook states that “the Francis turbine can be used in-line with an existing pipeline and has the ability to maintain downstream pressure requirements … useful in municipal installations where pipeline distribution pressures are required,” whereas “since Pelton turbines discharge to atmosphere they are not well suited if downstream pipeline pressures are required, such as a municipal system installation” ([Wyoming Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)). Crossflow and Turgo are likewise impulse machines and are not described as downstream-pressure devices in that source. This is a decisive selection criterion: where a WDN requires a pressurized downstream main, a Francis (or Kaplan/Semi-Kaplan) or a PAT with external control is indicated, while a Pelton is effectively excluded.

Three constraints govern pressure regulation. **Minimum residual pressure** is set by the network’s supply requirement; Lydon et al. (2017) treat the prescribed downstream pressure range as a hard constraint on PAT operation ([Lydon et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148117307498)). **Water hammer / transient surge** arises when flow is shut quickly. The Wyoming handbook warns that on a Francis, “the sudden reduction of flow and thus velocity will create a transient surge and cause water hammer in the pipeline,” so “an extensive transient surge analysis is required to determine the pressure increase and design of surge protection tanks or other design measures”; the Pelton’s jet deflector exists precisely to let the needle valve close slowly and limit surge ([Wyoming Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)). **Water quality, sediment and debris** are additional constraints on in-line devices in potable networks, but their treatment is consolidated in Section 4.

On whether PAT control matches PRV performance: the evidence is affirmative when the PAT is correctly configured. Souza et al. (2023) show that variable-speed PAT operation holds “the pump outlet pressure and network throughput … according to the values required by the PCVs” ([Souza et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-022-03421-9)). Viana et al. (2023), replacing two PRVs in a real DMAs, found that “optimal efficiencies are achieved at flow rates corresponding to the pump’s best efficiency point or near it, mimicking pressure control as if they were the valves themselves,” but also that one of two candidate PATs “exhibited negative outlet pressure, affecting the pressure in DMA2”—a reminder that a PAT can fail the pressure duty if poorly selected, and that “for effective pressure control in WDNs, other operational strategies, such as variable speed operation, should be explored” ([Viana et al. 2023](https://www.rbciamb.com.br/Publicacoes_RBCIAMB/article/view/1551)).

On multi-unit layouts, Lydon et al. (2017) report that “up to 40% of the gross power potential of an existing PRV could be converted to electrical energy using a PAT while also controlling pressure,” but that “the use of 2 PATs in parallel to increase the efficiency of the overall system achieved marginal improvements in performance” ([Lydon et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148117307498)). The same study flags that existing PAT selection methodologies “did not concur well with the experimental results,” so layout and unit selection should be validated against measured site data rather than generic charts.

### 1.4 Design and operational complexity

**Finding.** The complexity/longevity trade-off is asymmetric. PATs are standard, mass-produced rotodynamic machines with a shallow supply chain, a short electromechanical component chain and widely available spare parts; purpose-made micro-turbines are custom-built and carry more regulation hardware. In urban WDNs this makes the PAT the lower-procurement-risk option, but it shifts risk into hydraulic control and off-design operation. Some conventional types compensate with self-cleaning, long-life construction (Crossflow) or downstream-pressure capability (Francis), while Kaplan buys regulation range at the cost of complexity and price.

JICA’s design annex for reverse pump turbines lists the manufacturing advantages of reversing a standard pump: pumps are mass-produced; an integral pump and motor can be bought as a turbine-generator set; a wide range of heads and flows is covered by a large number of standard sizes; cost is low; delivery is short; seals and bearings are readily available as spare parts; and installation uses standard pipe fittings ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)). A direct-drive arrangement removes the belt drive: JICA reports very low friction loss (saving up to 5% of output power), a smaller base plate, no pulleys and, in a monobloc design (pump and motor in one integral unit), simpler construction with fewer bearings; maintenance is reduced because there is no belt tensioning or belt replacement ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)). The trade-off is hydraulic rather than mechanical: the flow range of a given PAT is much narrower than that of a conventional turbine, and the unit is best suited to a roughly fixed flow rate. Direct drive also fixes turbine speed to generator speed, reducing the range of flows over which the PAT can be matched to site conditions and limiting generator choice.

Conversely, JICA notes that small Crossflow turbines are more expensive to manufacture than larger ones because of the difficulty of fabricating the runner; a Pelton for the same low-flow duty would need three or four jets, giving a complicated casing and nozzle arrangement; and a small Francis would be even more expensive than a Crossflow ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)). This is the manufacturing-complexity penalty for custom, site-specific machines at sub-100 kW scale. The electromechanical component count differs sharply: the PAT chain is pump/motor, coupling or direct drive, generator and controls, whereas conventional machines add regulation hardware—Francis has a spiral scroll case, adjustable guide vanes/wicket gates and a draft tube (the downstream diffuser that recovers pressure); Kaplan adds adjustable runner-blade pitch on top of guide vanes; Pelton adds needle valves, jet deflectors and up to six jets ([Wyoming Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)). The balance of plant is similar for both technologies—NCAT’s guide lists trash rack, weir, forebay, penstock, powerhouse, turbine, tailrace and transmission lines—so the discriminator is the electromechanical package, not the civil scope ([NCAT/ATTRA beginner’s guide, Kindberg 2011](https://attra.ncat.org/publication/micro-hydro-power-a-beginners-guide-to-design-and-installation/)). A hybrid example is Giosio et al. (2015), who report a low-cost micro-hydro turbine unit using a pump impeller as runner with incorporated inlet flow control and an improved operating range; the full text was not accessible, so the detail remains abstract-level ([Giosio et al. 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148114008544)).

### 1.5 Expected service life

**Finding.** Service life is governed by operating point, water quality and maintenance rather than by technology family alone. PATs benefit from standard components and a deep supply-chain; some conventional types compensate with self-cleaning, long-life construction (Crossflow) or downstream-pressure capability (Francis), while Kaplan buys regulation range at the cost of complexity and price.

The strongest service-life statement among the conventional types is for Crossflow: the Wyoming handbook attributes “very little maintenance and prolonged life” to its self-cleaning design and standardized componentry ([Wyoming Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)). Francis is described as having “reliable, simple construction” with adjustable guide vanes and fixed runner blades; its maintenance is driven by the guide-vane linkages, bearings and draft tube, and the handbook warns that runaway throttling (overspeed when the electrical load is lost) creates transient surge and water hammer, requiring surge analysis and protection design ([Wyoming Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)). Kaplan is “highly adjustable in both the pitch and the runner blades as well as the inlet guide vanes,” which increases efficiency and widens the flow range but makes it “relatively expensive compared to other turbine types”; double-regulated (full) Kaplan has both adjustable guide vanes and adjustable blades, while semi-Kaplan has only one and is more cost-effective where flow does not vary greatly. More regulation hardware implies more maintenance and higher cost. Pelton’s multiple jets (up to six) retain efficiency under variable flow, but the nozzle/needle-valve and jet-deflector hardware is more complex; the deflector exists specifically to prevent runaway speed and overpressure surge during emergency stop ([Wyoming Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)).

For PATs, the wear items are bearings and seals, and JICA states that such spare parts are easily available because pumps are mass-produced ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)). Nasir’s 2024 review lists lower maintenance costs and easy availability among the PAT advantages, and identifies the absence of reverse-mode characteristics in manufacturer catalogs as a primary limitation ([Nasir et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10881369/)). A pump lifespan exceeding 20 years is reported in the PAT literature (Le Marre et al. 2022), but the full text was not accessible; the figure should be treated as literature-level rather than verified here ([Le Marre et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148122007790)). Nasir’s 2023 economic analysis uses a projected system lifetime without stating the number in the retrieved portion ([Nasir et al. 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606)). No numeric major-overhaul interval for sub-100 kW turbines or PATs was confirmed from pre-2024 public sources; generic hydroelectric overhaul guidance addresses large units and is not directly transferable to this scale. This is an evidence gap.

### 1.6 Market availability

**Finding.** The availability gap between the two families is structural rather than incidental. PATs are selected from mass-produced centrifugal-pump ranges sold through a deep global pump-manufacturing base, giving short delivery, many standard sizes and easy spare parts; conventional micro-turbines are lower-volume, more site-specific products from a thinner specialist tier. Standards and potable-water compliance add procurement friction to both, but PATs can partly ride on existing pump type-testing and drinking-water material certifications, whereas purpose-made turbines face a model-test framework written for multi-MW machines that is rarely economic at sub-100 kW scale.

JICA’s design annex states plainly that “standard pump units when operated in reverse have a number of advantages over conventional turbines for micro-hydro,” because pumps are mass-produced; the listed benefits are an integral pump-and-motor set, availability over a wide range of heads and flows, a large number of standard sizes, low cost, short delivery time, easily available seals and bearings, and easy installation using standard pipe fittings. The same annex tabulates standard centrifugal pumps (Southern Cross 50x32-160 through 80x50-200 series) with both pump-mode and reverse-operation ratings, illustrating that off-the-shelf pumps are documented for turbine duty ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)).

The pump-industry side confirms this. SPP Pumps, a centrifugal-pump manufacturer of over 140 years, has offered PAT solutions since 2009, rates them up to 500 kW (and “up spec”), and states that pumps and spare parts are more readily accessible than turbines; it also reports a return on investment “in as little as three years” and lists municipal drinking-water supply, PRV replacement and closed-loop pressure control as target applications ([SPP Pumps, 22 July 2022](https://www.spppumps.com/embracing-hydroelectric-power-the-concept-reality-of-pumps-as-turbines-pats/)). SPP identifies the historical micro-hydro problem as the non-availability of standardized equipment—Francis and Kaplan machines were designed to site specification, making each site unique and the electromechanical package costly ([SPP Pumps, 2022](https://www.spppumps.com/embracing-hydroelectric-power-the-concept-reality-of-pumps-as-turbines-pats/)). SAER Elettropompe similarly markets a “complete range of PATs” with low required investment and low maintenance costs for civil and industrial water recovery ([SAER Elettropompe, “Pump as Turbine” product page, archived 2023](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://www.saerelettropompe.com/en/products/special-solutions/pat.html)).

The conventional-turbine tier is large but oriented to bigger units. ANDRITZ Hydro reports more than 180 years of turbine design, over 31,900 turbines and more than 471,000 MW installed globally, with Pelton units up to 1,800 m head/400 MW, Francis up to 700 m/800 MW, Kaplan up to 90 m/230 MW and bulb units from 0.5–30 m head/76.5 MW; it also supplies pump turbines for storage, but these are custom-engineered products ([ANDRITZ Hydro, “Turbines for hydropower plants”](https://www.andritz.com/products-en/group/products/turbines-hydropower)). NCAT’s micro-hydro guide reinforces the contrast: micro-hydro (defined as <100 kW) is highly site-specific, and each site faces different regulations, ecology and topography, so detailed design cannot be reduced to a standard recipe ([NCAT/ATTRA](https://attra.ncat.org/publication/micro-hydro-power-a-beginners-guide-to-design-and-installation/)).

Four IEC standards frame the acceptance-testing landscape, and their scale limits matter for sub-100 kW urban deployments:

- **IEC 60193:2019** — model acceptance tests for hydraulic turbines, storage pumps and pump-turbines (laboratory model tests) ([IEC Webstore](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/60951)).
- **IEC 60041:1991** — field acceptance tests to determine the hydraulic performance of hydraulic turbines, storage pumps and pump-turbines ([IS/IEC 41:1991, archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gov.in.is.iec.41.1991)).
- **IEC 62006:2010** — acceptance tests of small hydroelectric installations; catalogue records describe it as defining the tests, measuring methods and contractual guarantee conditions for field acceptance tests of generating machinery in small hydroelectric installations up to 15 MW ([SCC Standards Database](https://scc-ccn.ca/standardsdb/standards/2017675)).

The practical consequence is that model acceptance testing per IEC 60193 is scaled to MW-class machines and is rarely economic for sub-100 kW units; micro-hydro procurement therefore leans on manufacturer performance data, pump curves and field acceptance testing under IEC 60041/62006. JICA explicitly warns that PAT selection is difficult and recommends that the client confirm performance with the designer or pump manufacturer in advance, because pump characteristics differ by manufacturer ([JICA Annex 6.2](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737046_11.pdf)). That confirmation step is a real procurement risk: a PAT chosen from a catalogue curve may not deliver the predicted turbine-mode BEP without manufacturer data.

Equipment installed in potable networks must satisfy drinking-water materials and product requirements. In the United States the key standard is NSF/ANSI 61, *Drinking Water System Components — Health Effects*, which covers materials and products that contact drinking water ([NSF knowledge library, NSF/ANSI 61](https://www.nsf.org/knowledge-library/nsf-ansi-standard-61-drinking-water-system-components-health-effects)); comparable national approval schemes exist elsewhere (e.g., WRAS in the UK). The NSF page itself was blocked in the underlying research, so the standard is identified at catalogue level rather than from a browsed full text; utilities should verify current certification with the manufacturer and local regulator.

Documented deployments indicate market activity and utility acceptance rather than independently verified energy yields. InPipe Energy (Oregon, USA) installs a mini turbine beside an existing pressure valve, diverting flow through a bypass and returning it to the main pipe; the company had implemented three such systems, including one lighting a baseball stadium outside Portland and one serving the East Bay Municipal Utility District in California expected to reach about 130,000 kWh/year, and was collaborating with New York City on its water supply ([Inspenet, January 2024](https://inspenet.com/en/news/system-that-generates-electricity-water-pipe/)). SOAR Hydropower, part of the Canyon Hydro family, has over 40 years of integrating generation into water systems; its energy-recovery turbines are typically placed in parallel with existing PRVs, in drinking-water, irrigation and wastewater lines ([SOAR Hydropower](https://soarhydro.com/)). The same trade-press review cites a 2022 Oak Ridge National Laboratory estimate that US conduit hydropower potential is about 1.41 GW, with more than 350 pipeline hydropower projects approved or built (about 530 MW); it also reports that NLine Energy and Canyon Hydro had installed about 100 miniturbines across the United States and Rentricity more than 30 systems ([Inspenet, January 2024](https://inspenet.com/en/news/system-that-generates-electricity-water-pipe/)).

*Caveats.* No pre-2024 source located gave a definitive count of PAT suppliers or a numeric lead-time range in weeks/months; JICA describes delivery only as “short,” and the “three years” payback is a vendor claim. Deployment counts come from trade-press/project sources. The claimed SAER potable-water compliance option could not be verified from the archived product page fetched here, so it remains an unverified vendor statement.

**Synthesis.** The two families trade performance for cost and availability. Purpose-made micro-hydroturbines generally lead on design-point efficiency, part-load behaviour and pressure-control flexibility, whereas PAT installations lead on procurement cost, market availability, lead time and installation simplicity. In PRV-replacement applications with steady flow and modest budgets, the PAT usually wins; in variable-demand networks where pressure control and wide-flow efficiency are paramount, the conventional turbine—or a variable-speed PAT with a PRV hybrid—is usually the better long-term choice.

The cost and availability comparisons are the best supported. The efficiency and pressure-regulation comparisons rest on a mix of academic, agency and vendor sources; vendor figures (≈85% PAT efficiency, ≈1.5× BEP operating limit, ≈95% purpose-built turbine efficiency) are plausible but are seller statements. **Notes and reading guidance.**

- **Classification footnote.** Archimedean screw is neither strictly impulse nor reaction (it is a gravity/positive-displacement machine); GWVHT is a low-head reaction/hydrokinetic device; the spherical turbine classification remains tentative; PAT is a reaction machine (a standardized centrifugal pump run in reverse). Kinetic/free-flow turbines are classed by US DOE as a type of reaction turbine but are not included here.
- **Unit conversions.** Imperial values are converted at 1 ft = 0.3048 m and 1 cfs ≈ 0.02832 m³/s; converted figures are rounded. Flow-rate ranges are site-dependent: they scale with runner diameter, specific speed, number of jets or nozzles, and regulation arrangement, so the values shown are typical published envelopes rather than universal limits.
- **Efficiency footnote.** Efficiency figures are design-point (BEP) hydraulic/turbine efficiencies where stated; they are not wire-to-water efficiencies. Where a source gives only a qualitative statement, no percentage is shown. Vendor statements (KSB, GWVHT vendor sheet, GE Vernova, Voith, Ossberger, Stellba) are labeled as such and are not independently verified.
- **Where sources disagree.** (i) Pelton: the Wyoming handbook attributes the wide flow range to multiple jets (up to six), whereas a single-jet machine would be narrower; the ~90% figure is a “potential efficiency” for well-designed jets and buckets, not a guaranteed minimum. (ii) Kaplan: the same source’s efficiency curves show a full/double-regulated Kaplan maintaining efficiency over a wider flow range than semi-Kaplans and a fixed-blade propeller; this explains why full-Kaplan is “relatively expensive” and semi-Kaplan is chosen where flow varies little. (iii) Francis: higher peak efficiency but a narrow operating range—a peak-versus-range trade-off. (iv) GWVHT: independent studies report 30–40% efficiency while the vendor sheet claims up to 80%; the discrepancy is typical of commercial versus measured performance. (v) PAT: KSB’s ~85% is a vendor figure for a double-entry volute-casing pump at BEP, while Nasir et al. (2024) report that PAT overall efficiency is lower than purpose-made turbines without giving a universal number; the value is conditioned by specific speed and pump size.
- **Remaining gaps.** Numeric head/flow/efficiency ranges for Pelton, Turgo, propeller, bulb/Straflo/tube, PAT, spherical and VLH were not confirmed from accessible pre-2024 sources. The IIT Roorkee turbine-classification chapter and the ESHA guide’s selection and efficiency tables were identified but not retrievable in full, so their numeric tables are not used here. The spherical turbine parameters of Muratoglu & Demir (2022) could not be retrieved. These gaps should be closed before the table is used for procurement or design.

For urban WDN energy recovery, where heads are typically low and flows vary diurnally, the decisive parameters are the width of the efficient flow range and the ability to maintain downstream pressure. Crossflow and Kaplan/Semi-Kaplan offer the widest verified flow-regulation ranges; Francis maintains downstream pressure; Pelton is effectively excluded by its atmospheric discharge. PATs offer the simplest pressure-control package (bypass and control valves) but with a narrow operating limit of about 1.5× BEP flow. Archimedean screws, GWVHT and VLH require open-channel or very-low-head conditions uncommon in pressurized WDNs, and the spherical turbine remains experimental. In-line hydrokinetic turbines (ILTs) have been demonstrated at PRV stations—most notably the Halifax Water Orchard Control Chamber, where a fixed-geometry PAT was selected at about 39.6 m head (130 ft) and 0.11 m³/s (3.9 cfs) ([Knapp & Reekie 2017](https://www.wateronline.com/doc/in-line-turbines-harness-energy-for-water-utilities-0001))—but they are not yet a broadly standardized product category.

## 4. Main economic and technical challenges

### 4.1 Economic and financial challenges

**Finding.** The binding constraint on urban water-network energy recovery is financial, not hydraulic. Projects are squeezed by high upfront capital cost (much of it civil works and balance-of-plant rather than the machine), low and volatile capacity factors driven by diurnal and seasonal demand, tariff and policy dependence, and institutional budgeting that often separates water and energy accounts. A site can be technically viable—positive head, adequate flow, a PRV worth replacing—and still fail on payback, financing or revenue risk.

Electromechanical equipment is only part of the bill: Motwani et al. (2013), as cited by Nasir et al. (2023), put electro-mechanical components at 35–40% of total micro-hydropower plant cost, so civil works, piping and balance of plant typically dominate ([Nasir et al. 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606)). This matters for WDNs because an in-pipe turbine may be inexpensive, yet the connection, bypass, underground chamber and grid interface can exceed the machine cost. PAT capital-cost estimates in the literature are order-of-magnitude and context-dependent: Ramos et al. (2013) cited 200–400 €/kW for nominal power below 40 kW; Carravetta et al. (2009) planned 230 €/kW for the PAT plus 115 €/kW for the generator; De Marchis et al. (2013) used 2,000 €/kW for PAT plus generator (all cited in [Nasir et al. 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606)). The spread is large enough that a low machine price does not guarantee a short payback when available power is small.

Capacity factor—actual annual energy output divided by nameplate capacity × 8,760 h—is the main revenue driver, and in WDNs it is structurally low and uncertain. Demand varies diurnally and seasonally, and PRVs/energy-recovery devices operate at part load for much of the year; this is the same variable-flow problem that depresses turbine efficiency at part load. Klein and co-workers (2022) explicitly identify capacity factor, discount rate and project lifetime as key input parameters whose high variability limits meaningful comparison across studies ([UNH EPSCoR 2022](https://scholars.unh.edu/nh_epscor/31/)). Alawadhi et al. (2023) make the sensitivity explicit: for a 218 kW in-pipe installation in the UAE, they analyzed payback and return on investment across scenarios of capital investment, O&M cost and plant capacity factor, finding a payback of 1–6 years and ROI up to 85% (Alawadhi et al. 2023, *Sustainability*). A Spanish study cited in the same paper reported payback of 6–14 years depending on water-velocity scenarios. The spread shows that capacity factor and flow regime, not equipment choice alone, determine economics.

Payback period—capital cost divided by annual net revenue—is highly sensitive to electricity price, equipment cost and utilization hours. Nasir et al. (2023), in an Ethiopian rural-electrification case, found a PAT payback of 3.42 years versus 6.04 years for a cross-flow turbine, with equivalent cost of power of 0.065 $/kWh (PAT) versus 0.100 $/kWh (cross-flow) ([Nasir et al. 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606)). The result is peer-reviewed but comes from a rural micro-hydro context, not an urban WDN, so its absolute values should not be transferred directly; the transferable lesson is the relative result—PATs shorten payback where a suitable pump exists. The same study found that impeller blade modification had no significant economic advantage unless the modification cost stayed at or below 25% of the initial PAT cost. Klein and co-workers (2022) show that LCOE falls and benefit-cost ratio rises with increasing power capacity—that is, sub-100 kW WDN projects sit at the disadvantageous end of the curve ([UNH EPSCoR 2022](https://scholars.unh.edu/nh_epscor/31/)). This is why a technically excellent small turbine can still fail: annual energy is small relative to fixed CAPEX, so payback stretches and the project becomes vulnerable to any change in electricity price or utilization. Competition with low-cost grid power compounds the problem, because the avoided cost or export price of recovered energy is set by the prevailing tariff rather than by the project’s own cost.

Revenue from WDN energy recovery depends on the electricity tariff or feed-in arrangement, which is often outside the water utility’s control. The UNEP/C2E2 urban water-energy review notes that water prices and energy prices are sometimes beyond the control of local municipalities, and that water and energy are administered by different government agencies, with policy making, planning and investment often taking place in isolation (Zhu/UNEP-C2E2, 2021). This is a structural market-design obstacle: even when recovered energy is physically available, the utility may not be able to monetize it at a predictable price. At the national level, the U.S. DOE/ORNL *Hydropower Market Report 2023* reports that incentives in the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and Inflation Reduction Act are expected to stimulate investment, but also that U.S. import tariffs on hydraulic turbines and parts contributed to a decline in trade activity starting in 2019, alongside COVID-era supply-chain delays and cost increases ([U.S. DOE/ORNL 2023](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/U.S.%20Hydropower%20Market%20Report%202023%20edition_20230928.pdf)). Tariff and trade policy therefore act on both the supply side (equipment cost and lead time) and the revenue side (market design).

A further institutional barrier is the **split incentive** between water and energy budgets: the water utility typically owns the pipe, funds the installation, and is evaluated on leakage and pressure compliance, while the recovered electricity value accrues to the energy budget or a third party. The pre-2024 sources reviewed here do not quantify this effect, but it structurally discourages investment in a device whose primary function (pressure control) is a water-side obligation and whose primary revenue (energy) is an energy-side return. This is an analytical inference from the institutional setting rather than a measured finding, and it should be treated as a hypothesis to be tested in utility-specific business cases.

Specific financing mechanisms and business models—ESCO contracts, third-party ownership, feed-in tariffs, net metering, green tariffs—are recognized renewable-support instruments, and the California in-conduit guidebook documents a decade of incentives and a commercial vendor/third-party ecosystem ([California Energy Commission 2020, CEC-500-2020-030](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)). However, the retrieved pre-2024 sources do not provide detailed, verified case-level evidence on which of these models have actually been used for urban WDN energy recovery; this remains an evidence gap. The California Energy Commission’s guidebook states the aggregate outcome plainly: despite a decade of incentives and a large untapped market, actual development of in-conduit hydropower projects is below its potential ([California Energy Commission 2020](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)). No specific named project abandoned for purely economic reasons was verified; project-level abandonment data should be treated as an evidence gap.

### 4.2 Technical and operational challenges

**Finding.** The binding technical constraint on in-pipe energy recovery is not the turbine but the network. Urban WDNs impose continuous diurnal and seasonal swings in flow and pressure, while a rotodynamic machine—whether a purpose-made micro-turbine or a PAT—is optimized for a single BEP. The device must therefore reproduce, continuously and across a wide operating range, the pressure-control function of the PRV it replaces. Purpose-made turbines carry adjustable hydraulic control elements (guide vanes, spear valves, blade pitch) that PATs lack, so PATs must rely on bypasses, external control valves or variable-speed drives to fill the gap—each with efficiency, complexity or reliability penalties.

Carravetta et al. (2013) state the problem directly: “the difficulty of energy recovery in water networks consists in the variability of the available power, which is dependent on the user demand pattern.” Their response is the Variable Operating Strategy (VOS), a design procedure for time-varying flow discharge and pressure drop, and a “system effectiveness” objective function that weights device efficiency, mechanical reliability and plant flexibility. They position PATs explicitly as a compromise—“having lower efficiencies and lower costs” than a traditional turbine—and note that the limited power available in a WDN, combined with the high cost of a miniaturized conventional turbine, “could excessively extend the payback period of the machine” ([Carravetta et al. 2013](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211)). The consequences appear as off-design derating and instability. Graciano-Uribe et al. (2021) report that inverse-mode pump operation produces “instabilities … which generates S-shape curves and cavitation,” and they survey selection criteria and performance-prediction methodologies as the main challenges for PATs in micro-hydro ([Graciano-Uribe et al. 2021](https://hrcak.srce.hr/270144)). KSB’s turbine-mode guidance quantifies the asymmetric limits: with a constant-speed machine, efficiency drops sharply as speed is raised to hold pressure at reduced flow, and the operating limit is reached at roughly 1.5 times the BEP flow ([KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)).

Regulation is where PATs and conventional turbines diverge most sharply. Carravetta et al. (2013) compare two PAT regulation modes—hydraulic regulation (HR) and electrical regulation (ER)—to achieve “a suitable equivalence between PRV and PAT for pressure control management,” and report that, for API-standard pumps, hydraulic regulation outperformed electrical regulation in their real-system application ([Carravetta et al. 2013](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211)). KSB explains the underlying reason: a standard centrifugal pump has no adjustable or variable-pitch diffuser vanes, so external control valves are required in turbine mode, and the unit is ideally integrated through a bypass line. A constant-speed PAT “is only designed for a defined volume flow rate and a defined pressure gradient”; all other operating conditions must be handled by throttling, so “the energy potential is not fully exploited.” A variable-speed generator can vary the flow, but at the cost of the efficiency penalties and the ~1.5× BEP operating limit noted above ([KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)).

Three risk families are routinely cited but were not quantified in the accessible pre-2024 sources reviewed here:

- **Transient/water-hammer.** No fetched source provided a quantified transient or water-hammer analysis for PAT or micro-turbine insertion in a WDN. Transient risk should therefore be treated as a site-specific design requirement—surge modelling, valve-closure sequencing, surge protection—rather than a parameter that can be generalized from the reviewed literature. The Wyoming handbook warns that on a Francis, sudden flow reduction creates transient surge and water hammer, requiring extensive surge analysis ([Wyoming Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)). *Evidence gap.*
- **Water quality, sediment, debris and corrosion.** The fetched sources did not provide specific guidance on sediment, debris or corrosion management for in-conduit turbines in potable networks. Runner longevity and potable-water compliance make this a practical concern, but it is not quantified in the accessible pre-2024 literature reviewed here. *Evidence gap.*
- **Grid connection and power quality.** KSB reports that feeding recovered energy into the grid via an asynchronous motor used as a generator is “both an economical and technically straightforward procedure,” with speed held constant apart from a load-dependent slip; however, isolated operation “requires elaborate control” to maintain constant frequency under fluctuating loads ([KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)). Islanding protection and power-quality limits for grid-connected distributed generation were not verified from a PAT-specific pre-2024 source. *Evidence gap.*

Space and access constraints in dense urban areas, and integration of the device with utility SCADA and pressure-management systems, were not quantified in the fetched sources; these are deployment-specific constraints that depend on the chosen layout and on the utility’s control architecture. *Evidence gap.*

Selection is difficult because pump manufacturers publish performance data for pump mode, not turbine mode, and the turbine-mode BEP must be inferred. Carravetta et al. (2013) address this with a system-effectiveness-based selection methodology; Graciano-Uribe et al. (2021) review selection criteria and performance-prediction methodologies and conclude that, compared with Kaplan, Francis and Pelton turbines, PATs are a good alternative for micro-hydro “thanks to their low cost and easy availability” ([Carravetta et al. 2013](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211); [Graciano-Uribe et al. 2021](https://hrcak.srce.hr/270144)). Lydon et al. (2017) state that “PATs are not widely used and the process of their selection is generally not well defined, due to difficulty in anticipating their performance,” and that existing PAT selection methodologies “did not concur well” with their experimental results ([Lydon et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148117307498)). This is the core institutional-technical gap: without a codified selection and acceptance procedure, each utility must re-derive performance predictions and negotiate approvals on a case-by-case basis.

Model-acceptance and field-test standards for hydraulic turbines exist (IEC 60193, IEC 62006, IEC 60041) and are scaled to MW-class machines; they are rarely economic for sub-100 kW units. What is missing is an equally mature installation and design code for in-line PATs in potable water networks. The Energy Booster study references ISO/ASME 14414, which “sets the requirements for performing the energy assessment on pumping systems and reporting the results accordingly” ([Energy Booster study, *Water* 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)); this is a pump-system energy-assessment standard, not a PAT-in-pipe installation code, but it is the closest referenced normative anchor. In practice, packaged vendors therefore self-certify against potable-water contact and pressure-equipment requirements rather than against a PAT-specific design standard, which increases procurement risk for utilities.

### 4.3 Mitigation and remaining gaps

The pre-2024 literature documents a consistent set of mitigation strategies, all aimed at reducing civil works, widening the operating range or de-risking selection. Packaged/modular PAT units—such as the Energy Booster concept, designed to overcome “most of the limitations of the present technologies when low energy is available and high discharge variation occurs” using a series-parallel regulation system based on an electronic or hydraulic principle ([Energy Booster study, *Water* 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526))—avoid large dedicated civil works, as do commercial packaged systems such as InPipe Energy’s In-PRV. Hybrid PAT + PRV layouts place the PAT in parallel with a bypass/PRV so that pressure control is retained during low-flow, maintenance or transient conditions; SOAR’s generating pressure-reducing valve is described as designed to run in parallel with the existing water system ([*Water Power* magazine, SOAR case](https://www.waterpowermagazine.com/analysis/energy-recovery-from-public-water-systems/)). An important caution comes from Lydon et al. (2017), who tested two PATs in parallel at a site and found only marginal improvement in overall performance ([Lydon et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148117307498)), so multi-unit layouts do not automatically overcome part-load derating.

Variable-speed (VFD) control is the principal lever for extending the PAT operating range and maintaining efficiency while regulating pressure; Souza et al. (2023) examine pressure regulation using PATs at variable speed for energy recovery ([Souza et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-022-03421-9)), and Merciera et al. address control of variable-speed PATs ([Merciera et al., Université Laval](https://corpus.ulaval.ca/bitstreams/73254a57-ecfb-4eeb-b212-cbc23e219bf4/download)). Software-based selection and hydraulic modelling reduce selection risk: the HYPER tool identifies the PAT parameters that maximize energy production while ensuring pressure regulation ([Marini et al. 2023, HYPER tool](https://github.com/gustavomarini/HYPER)); Balacco et al. (2023) describe PaT-ID as a tool for selecting the optimal pump-as-turbine for a WDN ([Balacco et al. 2023, *Energy* 282](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/energy/v282y2023ics0360544223017607.html)); and Le Marre et al. (2022) propose a decision-support algorithm for PAT regulation and partial-load limits ([Le Marre et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148122007790)). Digital twins—live hydraulic models of the surrounding network synchronized with SCADA data—are a promising but still largely research-stage mitigation; Sinagra et al. (2023) describe a digital twin incorporating the hydraulic model of the surrounding network to relate configuration parameters to energy recovery ([Sinagra et al. 2023, *Water Resources Management* 37(12)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/waterr/v37y2023i12d10.1007_s11269-023-03575-0.html)).

The evidence base remains dominated by laboratory, numerical and single-case studies rather than codified standards. Several sources required for a fuller assessment could not be retrieved in full: the SOAR case, the Almazrouei 2023 TRANSCO thesis, Stefanizzi et al. (2023), Le Marre et al. (2022), Souza et al. (2023) and Merciera et al. are accessible only at abstract, indexed-record or partial level, and their detailed numerical outputs should be verified against the original papers before being relied upon. No pre-2024 source was verified that quantifies the split-incentive effect or documents a specific policy mechanism enabling an urban WDN energy-recovery project; these remain open research and business-case questions. The technical concept is proven at laboratory and single-site scale, and commercial packaged offerings exist, but multi-site standardized deployments with independently audited performance remain rare in the public pre-2024 record.
