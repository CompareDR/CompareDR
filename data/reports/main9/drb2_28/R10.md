# Energy Recovery in Urban Water Supply Networks: A Technical Comparison of Micro-Hydroturbines and Pump-as-Turbine (PAT) Technologies

## 1. Comparison of Conventional Micro-Hydroturbines and Pump-as-Turbine (PAT) Technologies

Conventional micro-hydroturbines and Pumps-as-Turbines (PATs) are the two mainstream energy-recovery options for excess pressure in urban water distribution networks (WDNs). The two technologies differ fundamentally in design philosophy: a micro-hydroturbine is engineered and manufactured specifically to extract hydraulic energy, whereas a PAT is an off-the-shelf centrifugal pump operated in reverse, exploiting the well-known reversibility of turbomachines. This distinction propagates into every comparison axis below.

### 1.1 Operational Efficiency (Best Efficiency Point and Partial-Load Behavior)

Micro-hydroturbines are designed to maximise efficiency at their target operating point. Commercial runners of well-matched types reach the following peak (BEP) efficiencies: Pelton 85–92 % [1, 2], Francis 90–95 % [1, 3], Turgo 80–88 % [1, 2], Crossflow 70–85 % [1, 2], Kaplan/Propeller 85–94 % [1, 4]. Because dedicated micro-turbines are precisely matched to a site's head and flow, they retain a relatively flat efficiency curve in the vicinity of BEP. Reaction-type runners (Francis, Kaplan) with adjustable guide vanes or adjustable blades can keep efficiency high across roughly 30–100 % of design flow [5]. Impulse runners (Pelton, Turgo) tolerate partial loads well — Pelton runners are reported to deliver acceptable performance down to ~10 % of design flow when needle/nozzle regulation is used [6].

PATs are not optimised for turbine operation. Most published BEP efficiencies of single-stage centrifugal pumps operated in reverse fall in the 60–80 % range, with the turbine-mode BEP at higher flow and head than the pump-mode BEP [7, 8, 9]. Beyond ±20–30 % of the design point, efficiency drops sharply because a centrifugal pump has no flow-control element analogous to guide vanes: off-design operation triggers flow separation on the impeller blades and a pronounced swirling flow at the outlet [8, 10, 11]. In real WDN applications, where daily flow can vary by ±50 % or more [12, 13], the average annual PAT efficiency is typically reported between 50 and 65 % [11, 14, 15], noticeably below the efficiency of a well-matched micro-hydroturbine running at a similar installed capacity. Variable-speed (VSD) operation can recover a substantial portion of the off-design losses and stabilise efficiency near BEP across a wider flow band, but adds an inverter, electronic control and ongoing complexity [8, 10, 11].

### 1.2 Costs (Procurement, Installation and O&M)

Micro-hydroturbines are site-specific engineered products. The turbine itself represents 35–50 % of total project cost in micro/small hydro; the remainder is intake works, penstock, civil structures, generator, control, grid connection, and engineering [16, 17, 18]. Published total installed costs for sub-100 kW micro-hydro cluster around USD 4,000–8,000 per kW in developed markets and USD 1,500–4,000 per kW for self-built schemes [16, 19, 20]. Maintenance is straightforward but requires periodic nozzle/bucket inspection, bearing service and, every 10–15 years, a runner/generator overhaul [21].

PATs, by contrast, are procured from the global centrifugal-pump market, which is large, mature and price-competitive. Empirical studies and industry sources routinely report that a PAT costs roughly one-tenth of an equivalent purpose-built turbine, and in many comparisons the gap is reported as a five- to ten-fold capital-cost advantage [7, 22, 23, 24]. Spare parts (seals, bearings, impellers) and service expertise are inherited from the pump industry, which lowers O&M cost and shortens the lead time for replacement components [7, 22]. A payback period of 53 days was reported for the full-scale Antalya (Turkey) WDN PAT installation [25], 2.1 years for a Brazilian WDN [26], and 3.42 years (vs 6.04 years for a crossflow turbine) in an Ethiopian micro-hydro feasibility study [27]. PAT-specific installation is generally simpler than for a micro-turbine because the pump-flange geometry, mechanical seals and pipework are already standardised [7, 22, 23].

### 1.3 Pressure Regulation Capability

In a WDN, an energy-recovery device must do more than generate electricity; it must also regulate downstream pressure to keep the network within its operating envelope. Conventional micro-hydroturbines handle this through their inherent hydraulic characteristics:

- Impulse turbines (Pelton, Turgo, Crossflow) operate at atmospheric pressure on the runner side and are normally followed by a tailrace. They cannot, by themselves, deliver a pressurised discharge. When installed in a pressurised network, they must be coupled to a downstream pressure-control loop (typically a control valve or a by-pass), and any flow not passing through the runner loses the energy it would otherwise have generated [6, 28].
- Reaction turbines (Francis, Kaplan) are fully enclosed and inherently operate with a pressurised inlet and outlet. They therefore accept an upstream pressure, dissipate a controlled portion as head across the runner, and deliver water at the required downstream pressure. Adjustable guide vanes and (for Kaplan) adjustable runner blades provide continuous, fast pressure regulation [3, 4, 5, 29].

A PAT, being a centrifugal pump in reverse, is also a reaction machine and is therefore inherently compatible with a pressurised pipeline. However, a PAT on its own has no internal flow-control element; its operating head/flow curve is fixed at a given rotational speed, and the pressure it produces is a function of flow. A single, fixed-speed PAT therefore cannot maintain a constant downstream pressure across the wide flow range seen in a WDN [11, 12, 13]. In practice, PATs are combined with one of three regulation strategies: (i) hydraulic regulation — a series PRV throttles excess head and a parallel by-pass routes excess flow [10, 11]; (ii) electrical regulation — a variable-speed drive shifts the PAT characteristic curve to follow the WDN operating point [8, 10, 11]; (iii) hybrid (HER), combining both [10, 11]. With any of these schemes, a PAT can in principle deliver downstream-pressure control comparable to a Francis or Kaplan runner, but the regulating hardware and control logic are external to the machine, not built-in.

### 1.4 Design and Operational Complexity

Micro-hydroturbines require site-specific hydraulic design (runner diameter, speed, number of nozzles, guide-vane geometry), bespoke civil works, and typically engineering support from specialised manufacturers [16, 17, 18]. Commissioning, governor tuning and synchronisation with the grid need skilled labour. Once running, they are robust but not portable: a runner is matched to one site.

PATs are radically simpler from a design standpoint. The "selection" exercise consists of (i) identifying the required BEP in turbine mode from the WDN's available head/flow, (ii) choosing a standard pump whose pump-mode BEP can be converted to that target via published conversion factors, and (iii) verifying by lab or in-situ testing [7, 8, 9, 22, 30]. Construction, mechanical seals, bearings, couplings, and pipe flanges are all standard pump-industry components. Day-to-day operation is largely automatic, and maintenance crews familiar with centrifugal pumps require little additional training [7, 22, 23]. The principal design-side complication is that pump manufacturers do not normally publish turbine-mode characteristic curves, so several empirical or analytical methods (specific-speed, BEP-shift, polynomial regression, ANN, CFD) must be used to predict turbine performance, with reported errors typically ±10–20 % [8, 9, 22, 30, 31].

### 1.5 Expected Service Life

Micro-hydro runners and generators are heavy-duty, slow-speed machines, generally designed for a 30- to 50-year operating life with periodic overhauls (bearing replacement every ~10 years, runner rebalancing/rewinding every 15–25 years) [16, 21, 32, 33].

A PAT inherits the expected life of a centrifugal pump. With proper material selection (stainless steel for drinking-water service), bearing and seal maintenance, and avoidance of cavitation, a duty life of 20–30 years is commonly cited [22, 23, 24]. Several field installations from the late 1980s/early 1990s (e.g. the Breech plant, UK, with KSB Etanorm pumps running in turbine mode since 1989) remain in service, supporting the long-life claim under good maintenance [34]. Multi-stage PATs, in contrast, are reported to suffer accelerated wear and lower efficiency because of inter-stage leakage and uneven pressure distribution; single-stage pumps in series or single-stage pumps with trimmed impellers are preferred [35, 36].

### 1.6 Market Availability

Micro-hydroturbines are supplied by a relatively small number of specialist manufacturers (Gilkes, Ossberger, ANDRITZ, Canyon Industries, Toshiba, and a few regional builders) [1, 17]. Spare parts and service are usually tied to the original supplier, and the smallest capacity (sub-10 kW) units are often produced in low volumes.

PATs draw on the global centrifugal-pump industry — many thousands of catalogue models covering every conceivable head/flow combination, available from global vendors (KSB, Grundfos, Xylem, Sulzer, Kirloskar, etc.) as well as regional manufacturers, with well-developed after-sales support [7, 22, 23, 34]. The lead time for a standard pump is typically weeks rather than the months often quoted for a custom turbine [22, 23, 24]. This mature supply chain is the single largest reason PATs are favoured in WDN applications where installed capacities are typically 5–500 kW and many small sites are scattered across the network [7, 12, 13, 14].

A few cross-cutting notes help interpret the table. First, the specific speed (Ns = N·√P / H^5/4) is the engineering lens that ties a runner to a head/flow point: Pelton sits at Ns ≈ 10–35, Francis at Ns ≈ 60–300, and Kaplan at Ns ≈ 300–1000 [1, 2, 37]. Mismatching a runner to a site is the single largest source of under-performance. Second, the head/flow ranges in the table are engineering envelopes, not universal limits; some manufacturers offer Pelton runners for heads below 50 m and Francis runners for heads above 600 m, but efficiency and cost-effectiveness fall off the edges. Third, the pressure-regulation column captures the design intent: any of these runners can be combined with a downstream valve to provide some pressure control, but the regulation authority — speed, accuracy, and stability of response — is highest for reaction machines with built-in adjustable elements (Francis, Kaplan) and lowest for atmospheric impulse runners (Pelton, Turgo, Crossflow).

## 4. Economic and Technical Challenges in Installing Energy-Recovery Equipment in Urban Water Networks

The challenges are not symmetric. Micro-hydroturbines and PATs each expose the WDN operator to a different mix of obstacles, and several are common to both. The dominant technical challenges are tightly coupled to WDN hydraulics; the dominant economic challenges are tied to the small unit size and dispersed nature of recovery sites.

### 4.1 Economic Challenges

- Small unit size, low revenue per site. Recoverable energy in WDNs is concentrated at pressure-reducing valve (PRV) sites and reservoir inlets, where individual power outputs are commonly 5–50 kW and rarely exceed 100 kW. Per-kW installed cost rises sharply as capacity falls below ~100 kW because civil works, electrical interconnection, and engineering are not proportional [16, 17, 18, 43]. Recoverable annual energy at a single site is typically modest — 10–400 MWh depending on tariff, head, and flow variability [12, 13, 15, 43].
- Payback period sensitivity to electricity price and tariff structure. Reported payback periods range from 53 days (Antalya, Turkey) [25] and 2.1 years (Brazil) [26] to 4–8 years (Europe) and 6–9 years (Asia-Pacific) [43]. Where feed-in tariffs are generous, PAT-based schemes recover costs quickly; where grid export is poorly valued, the same installation can become uneconomic [11, 26, 27, 43].
- High relative cost of pressure-control and grid-connection hardware. The variable-speed drive (for electrical regulation), the by-pass PRV (for hydraulic regulation), and the grid-tie inverter/transformer are the dominant cost items in a PAT installation, often exceeding the cost of the pump itself [10, 11, 17, 18].
- Permitting, regulatory and environmental compliance. Even when the equipment is small, the permitting pathway (water rights, environmental flow, FERC or national-equivalent licensing, drinking-water safety approvals) can take 9–18 months and add 5–15 % to project cost [16, 18, 33]. Approval for in-pipe drinking-water installations is especially demanding because the turbine materials must be certified for potable-water contact.
- Capital cost versus revenue uncertainty. Recoverable energy is calculated from often-incomplete flow and pressure records; the Brussels case study (Vivaqua) shows that theoretical potentials of 650 kW at a single site shrink to 50 kW in normal operating conditions once long-term data are accounted for [44]. Without multi-year monitoring, projects risk being sized for potentials that never materialise.
- Tariff design and electricity-market participation. For small distributed schemes, participating directly in wholesale or ancillary-service markets is impractical. The value of the recovered energy depends on whether it offsets retail-priced import or is exported at a (typically much lower) feed-in rate [11, 43, 44].

### 4.2 Technical Challenges

- Highly variable flow and pressure. WDNs are driven by end-user demand, which varies on diurnal, weekly, and seasonal timescales, often by ±50 % or more around the mean [12, 13, 14, 15]. Micro-hydroturbines and PATs both suffer efficiency loss off-design; PATs are more vulnerable because they lack an internal flow-control element, but a poorly matched Francis or Crossflow can be equally off-target for large parts of the day.
- Off-design operation and BEP prediction. The actual operating point of an installed PAT is rarely at its BEP. Reported average conversion efficiency at WDN sites is typically 30–50 % for the whole system and 50–65 % for the PAT itself [11, 14, 15]. For micro-hydroturbines, similar losses are reported when the design head or flow is mis-estimated, but the design process reduces this risk.
- Lack of published performance data for PATs. Pump manufacturers do not normally supply turbine-mode characteristic curves, so the designer must rely on empirical methods (specific-speed conversion, polynomial regression, ANN, CFD). Prediction errors of ±10–20 % in head, flow, and efficiency are common [7, 8, 9, 22, 30, 31]. Each candidate machine still benefits from laboratory or in-situ characterisation before deployment.
- Pressure regulation and water hammer. The hydraulic transients associated with sudden load rejection, pump trip, or valve closure can produce high pressure surges (water hammer). PATs, like all turbomachines, must be protected by a surge tank, flywheel, or by-pass valve. The Breech installation limits PAT speed to 1,500–1,504 rpm via a regulation loop to keep transients within safe bounds [34]. Micro-hydroturbines require similar protection, but the wider range of governor options for Francis and Kaplan makes surge management more straightforward.
- Cavitation. Cavitation is a recurrent concern for reaction machines (Francis, Kaplan, PAT) operating at high head or with insufficient net positive suction head (NPSH). PATs are particularly exposed because their turbine-mode NPSH requirement is higher than their pump-mode requirement at the same flow. Reported mitigations include lowering the installation elevation (Breech site [34]), trimming impeller diameter, and using cavitation-resistant materials. Spherical and other in-pipe designs avoid this problem by maintaining a fully flooded runner at low head [40, 41].
- Drinking-water quality and material compatibility. Equipment installed in a potable WDN must use certified materials (typically stainless steel, bronze, or specific polymer-lined components) and must not introduce lubricants, wear particles, or biofilms into the supply. PATs in certified drinking-water pumps are widely accepted; custom micro-turbines require case-by-case approval [22, 23, 24, 34].
- Sediment, debris and biofouling. Real WDN water carries fine sediment, scale, and biofilm precursors; runners — particularly Pelton nozzles and small Crossflow blades — are vulnerable to wear and blockage. Coanda screens, desanders, and self-cleaning mechanisms are now standard at the intake [32, 40, 41, 42].
- Grid connection and electrical infrastructure. Synchronous generators, induction generators, and VSDs each impose different power-quality, anti-islanding, and protection requirements under standards such as IEEE 1547 (North America) or EN 50160 (Europe) [16, 43]. For small sites, the cost of grid-tie hardware and protection relays can be a significant share of the project total.
- Operational priority: water supply first. Energy recovery is a secondary objective. The system must continue to deliver water at the required pressure and quality even when the generator is tripped, the inverter fails, or the upstream supply changes. This priority shapes both control design (PRV by-passes, dump loads, flywheel inertia) and the choice of regulating strategy [6, 34, 44].
- Lack of long-term operational data. The technology is in active deployment but most published installations have less than ten years of monitored performance. Multi-year datasets (such as the Vivaqua 4-year analysis in Brussels [44]) are still rare; they consistently show that the "expected" recoverable energy is materially above what is actually delivered, mainly because of upstream/downstream coupling, off-design operation, and grid-side constraints.

These economic and technical challenges are not independent. Off-design efficiency losses (technical) lengthen the payback (economic); permitting delays (regulatory) erode net present value; pressure-regulation complexity (technical) drives inverter and valve cost (economic). A project that succeeds in real WDNs is one that has adequately characterised the long-term hydraulic regime, selected a runner type that can deliver a useful efficiency at the operating point that actually occurs, sized the pressure-control hardware for the worst case, and secured a tariff that values the recovered energy.
