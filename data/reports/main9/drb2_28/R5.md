# Energy Recovery in Urban Water Supply Systems: Conventional Micro-hydroturbines vs. Pumps as Turbines (PATs)

*A research report based on publicly available information up to and including early 2024*

---

## Introduction

Urban water distribution networks are pressurized systems, and where topography or pumping creates more pressure than consumers need, the surplus is routinely destroyed in pressure-reducing valves (PRVs). Dissipating that excess head as heat, noise and cavitation is the default practice today, but the same pressure drop can instead drive a turbine and produce electricity, turning a pure loss into a resource while still performing the pressure-management duty the network requires. The two mainstream hardware families competing for this duty are **conventional micro-hydroturbines** (purpose-designed machines of the Pelton, Turgo, crossflow, Francis or Kaplan families, together with newer in-conduit spherical turbines) and **Pumps as Turbines (PATs)**, in which a standard centrifugal pump is simply run in reverse. This report compares the two families in detail, tabulates the technical parameters of the main turbine types, and closes with the economic and technical challenges that currently shape real-world deployment in municipal networks.

The evidence base is the public technical literature (peer-reviewed journals, conference proceedings and doctoral theses), engineering handbooks and institutional guides, manufacturers' published catalogues and case studies, and documented municipal installations, restricted to material available up to early 2024. Quantitative claims are attributed inline at the point where they appear, and the figures in this report are original syntheses drawn from those cited sources. Where values from different sources disagree — which happens often for cost and efficiency figures, because they depend strongly on unit size and site conditions — the spread is reported rather than a single number, so the ranges below should be read as indicative envelopes, not guarantees for any specific project.

## Part 1 — Detailed Comparison of Conventional Micro-hydroturbines and PATs

### 1.1 Operational Efficiency: Best Efficiency Point and Partial-Load Behaviour

At their best efficiency point (BEP), purpose-built micro-hydroturbines hold a clear but not overwhelming edge. Generic manufacturer-independent efficiency tables compiled for micro-hydro buyer guidance put modern Pelton, Francis and Kaplan machines at roughly 80–90% design-point efficiency, with Turgo quoted as high as 80–95% and crossflow somewhat lower at 65–85% ([NRCan efficiency table via UTAR thesis](http://eprints.utar.edu.my/149/1/MH-2011-0703227-1.pdf)). A well-selected PAT, by contrast, typically peaks around 60–85%: documented experience suggests a ceiling near 85% for good selections, a value reported in experimental work on reversed pumps ([IST Lisbon thesis](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/844820067128630/TeseMiguel_vsCorrigida.pdf)), while ANDRITZ quotes efficiencies of up to 87% for its engineered small pump-turbine range, showing that the best PATs now approach conventional-machine territory at the design point ([ANDRITZ small pump turbines](https://www.andritz.com/products-en/pumps/small-pump-turbines)). The caveat is scale: efficiency collapses for very small standard centrifugal pumps, with measured values of only 30–31% for small centrifugal PATs against 70–75% for semi-axial designs in experimental comparisons ([Granville PAT study, Water (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).

The sharper difference appears away from the design point, which matters enormously in water networks where demand — and therefore flow through any energy-recovery device — swings widely over a day. Impulse machines have famously flat efficiency curves: multi-jet Peltons hold high efficiency from about 10% to 100% of design flow (single-jet units from ~30%), and Turgo wheels from roughly 20% to 100%, while a nozzle deflector also protects them from runaway and water-hammer excursions ([European Small Hydropower Guide](https://energycommunityplatform.eu/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Merged-Guide-Develop-a-Small-Hydropower-Plant.pdf)). Crossflow turbines similarly hold an efficiency slightly above 80% down to about one-sixth of design flow, a consequence of their divided two-cell runner ([ESHA Layman's Guidebook](https://fenix.ciencias.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/281612415669658/Laymans%20Guide%20to%20Hydro_ESHA.pdf)). Reaction machines are less forgiving: Francis units become unstable below roughly 40–50% of design flow, double-regulated Kaplan units tolerate operation down to about one-fifth, and fixed-blade propeller units have narrow high-efficiency bands ([ESHA Layman's Guidebook](https://fenix.ciencias.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/281612415669658/Laymans%20Guide%20to%20Hydro_ESHA.pdf); [PSI/SFOE final report](https://www.psi.ch/sites/default/files/import/lea/HomeEN/Final-Report-BFE-Project.pdf)). The PAT sits at the weak end of this spectrum: with no guide vanes and no moving control element at all, its efficiency falls steeply on both sides of the BEP, and off-design operation is estimated to cost 3–10 percentage points of efficiency ([J. Sustainable Development (sdewes)](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321)).

Typical part-load efficiency curves of micro/small hydropower machines

Figure 1 synthesizes these behaviours into indicative efficiency-versus-load curves: impulse machines (Pelton, Turgo, crossflow) and double-regulated Kaplan units stay flat and high across a wide band, Francis and semi-Kaplan machines are mid-pack, the fixed-blade propeller collapses off-design, and the fixed-speed PAT shows the narrowest, lowest dome of all. A practical complication unique to PATs is that pump manufacturers do not publish turbine-mode characteristic curves, so the BEP in reverse operation must be estimated with prediction methods or measured — a gap that motivated the REDAWN project to build an open PAT performance database ([IST Lisbon thesis](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/844820067128630/TeseMiguel_vsCorrigida.pdf); [University of Bath REDAWN case study](https://www.bath.ac.uk/case-studies/reducing-energy-dependency-in-atlantic-area-water-networks-redawn/)). Finally, plant-level efficiency depends on the control architecture, not just the machine: a hydraulic-regulation scheme (series PRV plus bypass) applied to a PAT plant in Pompeii delivered 49–59% overall plant efficiency, while an electric-regulation (variable-speed) scheme in South Tyrol raised annual energy recovery by 23% relative to a fixed-speed baseline ([Renewable Energy review of PAT regulation (2022)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960148122007790)).

### 1.2 Costs: Procurement, Installation, and Operation & Maintenance

Procurement cost is the PAT's signature advantage. Because the machine is a mass-produced pump, its purchase price is reported at roughly one-tenth of that of a purpose-made turbine of comparable duty ([PAT technology review (2024)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10881369/)), and for units below 50 kW, less than half the cost of a conventional turbine ([J. Sustainable Development (sdewes)](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321)). Published unit-cost figures for small PAT installations cluster in the low hundreds of euros per kilowatt: about 200–400 €/kW for schemes below 40 kW in work by Ramos and colleagues, and 230 €/kW for the PAT plus 115 €/kW for the generator in Carravetta's analysis ([Ramos et al., Water (2019)](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/11/1/149)). Even the conservative electro-mechanical equipment cost model of De Marchis and colleagues, at roughly 2,000 €/kW for PAT plus generator, sits at the bottom of conventional-turbine territory ([De Marchis et al., Journal of Hydroinformatics (2015)](https://iwaponline.com/jh/article/17/3/347/3663/)).

Installation and civil works, however, compress much of that gap, because they dominate total project cost for any in-conduit scheme regardless of machine type. In the De Marchis cost model, electro-mechanical equipment accounts for only 35–40% of total project cost and civil works add about 30% on top of equipment ([PAT technology review (2024)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10881369/)). For conventional micro-hydro, a Canadian buyer's guide quotes total installed system costs of roughly US$1,500–2,500 per kW, noting explicitly that PAT-based 50-kW-class schemes come out cheaper ([NRCan Micro-Hydro Buyer's Guide (2004)](https://www.builditsolar.com/Projects/Hydro/CanadaMicroHydroGuide.pdf)); a global IRENA analysis of small hydropower reports turnkey costs of US$1,300–8,000 per kW ([IRENA cost analysis via Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2016/8037892)); and for low- and ultra-low-head sites Ontario's guidance cites US$2,000–9,000 per kW installed ([Ontario micro-hydro systems](http://www.ontario.ca/page/micro-hydro-systems)). Novel in-pipe machines can exceed even that: Portland's four-unit LucidPipe spherical-turbine installation cost about US$1.7 million for 200 kW, or roughly US$8,500 per kW ([The Oregonian (2015)](https://www.oregonlive.com/environment/2015/01/portland_company_that_built_ci.html)).

Installed-cost and LCOE comparison

On operation and maintenance, the PAT again benefits from being a pump: spares and service skills are available worldwide, and the De Marchis model assumes annual maintenance of about 15% of the (low) PAT purchase cost ([De Marchis et al., Journal of Hydroinformatics (2015)](https://iwaponline.com/jh/article/17/3/347/3663/)). The economics can be striking where pressure surplus is continuous: a PAT installation in Antalya, Turkey (130–300 m³/h at about 1 bar, average efficiency ~60%) reported a payback of only 53 days ([Antalya case study, Water Science (2018)](https://iwaponline.com/ws/article-abstract/18/1/130/38216)). Conventional machines carry higher service costs but also longevity, while some newer in-conduit products target minimal maintenance by design — Voith's StreamDiver claims a ten-year maintenance-free interval ([Voith StreamDiver brochure](https://www.voith.com/corp-en/t_3390_e_StreamDiver_20150907_screen.pdf)). On a levelized basis, an Indian study estimated generation costs of about US$0.012/kWh for a PAT scheme versus US$0.059/kWh for an equivalent Francis scheme, while the Portland LucidPipe project reported an LCOE of 5–12 US cents per kWh ([PAT technology review (2024)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10881369/); [Lucid Energy Portland case study](https://climatesmartwater.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/LucidEnergy-PortlandCaseStudy-2016-10-lr.pdf)).

### 1.3 Pressure Regulation Capability

Pressure regulation is where the fundamental physics of the two families diverge most sharply, and where marketing claims most often outrun engineering reality. A fixed-speed PAT is a passive hydraulic element: the head it absorbs is set by the flow passing through it, so it cannot, on its own, hold a target downstream pressure as network demand fluctuates — the literature is explicit that a PAT lacks the ability to regulate downstream pressure by itself ([Water Resources Management (2023)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-023-03575-0)). Two regulation philosophies have therefore been developed around it ([Renewable Energy review of PAT regulation (2022)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960148122007790)). **Hydraulic regulation (HR)** pairs the PAT with a series control valve and/or a parallel bypass, so that valves — not the machine — absorb the difference between available head and target pressure; the Pompeii demonstration achieved 49–59% plant efficiency this way. **Electric regulation (ER)** uses a variable-frequency drive to move the PAT's operating point; it captures more energy (the South Tyrol installation recorded a 23% gain) but requires oversizing the machine, still cannot control back-pressure under all conditions, and adds drive cost and complexity. Hybrid hydro-electric regulation (HER) couples both approaches and has been analysed as the most effective compromise ([Carravetta et al., Water (2013)](https://doi.org/10.3390/w5031211)).

Conventional turbines, in contrast, carry their regulation on board: Francis and Kaplan machines modulate flow through adjustable guide vanes, and impulse machines use spear valves and deflectors, so they can follow load (or flow) while holding efficiency, and the Turgo's deflector specifically protects the line against runaway and water hammer during rejection ([European Small Hydropower Guide](https://energycommunityplatform.eu/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Merged-Guide-Develop-a-Small-Hydropower-Plant.pdf)). But an important caution applies to both families in the municipal context: classical mini-turbines were designed for hydropower sites, "can hardly be scaled down to the size of water distribution systems", and no turbine alone provides the fail-safe pressure control a potable network demands ([Water Resources Management (2023)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-023-03575-0)). In practice every serious architecture — PAT or turbine — retains a PRV in parallel as a bypass that guarantees supply and pressure control during machine outages, low-demand periods and fire-flow events; pump-supplier engineering guidance describes exactly this bypass-plus-control-valve integration for turbine-mode pumps ([KSB lexicon: turbine mode](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)).

PRV vs PAT-with-bypass schematic

The practical verdict is therefore nuanced. Where the control objective is precise downstream pressure with energy recovery as a by-product, the PAT-plus-valves architecture is today the dominant and most cost-effective pattern, and packaged products formalize it — for example InPipe Energy's In-PRV combines a micro-turbine with a control valve to actively regulate pressure while generating, as documented at the Hillsboro, Oregon installation producing about 203,000 kWh per year ([InPipe Hillsboro white paper](https://www.inpipeenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/InPipe_Micro-Hydro_Hillsboro_White-Paper.pdf)). Spherical in-pipe turbines such as LucidPipe extract only 1–5 psi per unit and deliberately leave pressure management to the network's existing valves ([MSW Magazine on Portland LucidPipe](https://www.mswmag.com/editorial/2016/07/converting_water_pressure_into_power)). Where the site behaves more like a classic hydropower scheme — a break-tank or reservoir inlet with steady, well-characterized flow — a conventional turbine with its own governor can take on more of the regulation duty directly, as in mining break-tank installations using PATs above 300 kW with about 2.5 GWh/yr output ([break-tank mining PAT study](https://juniperpublishers.com/imst/pdf/IMST.MS.ID.555595.pdf)).

### 1.4 Design and Operational Complexity

The PAT concept is mechanically the simplest possible: a standard, unmodified centrifugal pump, an off-the-shelf asynchronous or permanent-magnet generator, and pipework. There are no wicket gates, no governor hydraulics, no draft tube, and no bespoke runner design, which is why suppliers describe turbine-mode operation of their pumps as a routine configuration with bypass, control valves and a standard asynchronous generator ([KSB lexicon: turbine mode](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)). The complexity hides one level up, in **selection**: because manufacturers do not publish turbine-mode curves, choosing a pump that will hit its turbine-mode BEP at the site's actual flow and head requires prediction correlations, CFD or testing, and a wrong choice strands the unit far down its part-load curve ([IST Lisbon thesis](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/844820067128630/TeseMiguel_vsCorrigida.pdf)). The REDAWN project attacked precisely this barrier by developing selection tools and an open PAT performance database, and by demonstrating "PAT = BAT" (Best Available Technology) for PRV replacement in Atlantic-area networks ([University of Bath REDAWN case study](https://www.bath.ac.uk/case-studies/reducing-energy-dependency-in-atlantic-area-water-networks-redawn/); [REDAWN ebook](https://www.ateneapolieditore.it/redawn/redawn_ebook_inglese.pdf)).

Conventional micro-hydroturbines move the complexity from selection into engineering and operation: each unit is effectively a small hydropower plant, with site-specific runner and gate design, a governor, protection systems, and more substantial civil works, and conduit-hydro practitioners warn of network-side failure modes that must be engineered around — most notably the overpressure surge that propagates when a generator breaker trips and the machine unloads suddenly, which can threaten pipeline integrity ([Canyon Hydro conduit hydro](https://www.canyonhydro.com/projects/conduit.html)). Operational staffing is correspondingly heavier for governor-equipped machines, whereas PAT stations and packaged in-conduit products are designed for unattended operation with remote monitoring; turnkey PAT skid packages (e.g., dual-PAT 112 kW installations) show how far the "package plant" philosophy has been pushed by suppliers ([Canyon PAT system packages](https://www.canyonhydro.com/products/CANYON_PAT-SystemPackages_WEB-EN.pdf)). For network operators whose core competence is water rather than power, this operational simplicity — plus the fact that a failed PAT is swapped like a pump, often within days — is frequently the decisive practical argument, and it is reinforced by variable-speed drives and control valves that water utilities already use throughout their systems.

### 1.5 Expected Service Life

Purpose-built turbines are designed as long-life infrastructure: Canadian provincial guidance cites turbine lifetimes of at least 25 years for micro-hydro installations ([Ontario micro-hydro systems](http://www.ontario.ca/page/micro-hydro-systems)), and in-pipe products target even longer — Lucid Energy quotes a 40-year design life for LucidPipe ([MSW Magazine on Portland LucidPipe](https://www.mswmag.com/editorial/2016/07/converting_water_pressure_into_power)), while InPipe Energy specifies a 30-year design life for its Hillsboro installation ([InPipe Hillsboro white paper](https://www.inpipeenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/InPipe_Micro-Hydro_Hillsboro_White-Paper.pdf)). A PAT, built to pump-duty standards, is generally credited with a shorter life in turbine service: a recent review puts typical PAT life at around ten years against roughly 25 for a Francis machine in comparable duty ([Klein et al., RSER (2022)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S1364032122007808)). The reasons are mundane rather than catastrophic — bearings, seals and wear rings sized for intermittent pump duty run continuously in turbine duty, and any cavitation at off-design operation accelerates erosion.

The shorter life must, however, be priced rather than feared: because the machine costs a tenth of a purpose-built turbine, one or even two mid-life replacements can still leave total ownership cost far below the conventional alternative, a point embedded in life-cycle cost models such as Novara's, which explicitly annualizes PAT replacement ([Novara et al., J. Water Resour. Plann. Manage. (2019)](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/abs/10.1061/(ASCE)WR.1943-5452.0001063)). REDAWN's demonstration economics — energy recovery valued around 0.036 €/m³/yr across its pilots — were computed on exactly this basis ([REDAWN ebook](https://www.ateneapolieditore.it/redawn/redawn_ebook_inglese.pdf)). For owners, the practical implications are to (i) specify continuous-duty bearings and monitor cavitation margins at the expected operating points, (ii) treat the PAT as a replaceable asset with a stocked spare, and (iii) evaluate projects on 20–30-year levelized cost rather than first cost, in which framework the PAT usually still wins at small scale ([PAT technology review (2024)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10881369/)).

### 1.6 Market Availability

PATs ride on the largest fluid-machine supply chain in the world: any centrifugal-pump catalogue is, potentially, a turbine catalogue, and procurement is competitive, fast and local almost everywhere. Dedicated engineered PAT lines also exist — ANDRITZ markets ACT axial pump-turbines up to 0.8 m³/s and 250 kW and FPT radial units up to 6 m³/s and 2 MW at heads up to 80 m with efficiencies to 87% ([ANDRITZ small pump turbines](https://www.andritz.com/products-en/pumps/small-pump-turbines); [ANDRITZ pump-turbine brochure](https://www.andritz.com/resource/blob/34004/fd9cdefb249b7fa645228add62d9aa26/hy-andritz-pumps-as-turbines-en-data.pdf)) — and North American suppliers sell complete PAT skid packages for conduit applications ([Canyon PAT system packages](https://www.canyonhydro.com/products/CANYON_PAT-SystemPackages_WEB-EN.pdf)). PATs are applicable across an exceptionally wide envelope: documented applications span roughly 10–150 m head and 0.003–0.5 m³/s for radial units, up to 50–1000 m for multistage units, and down to 2–7 m for axial pump-turbines, with PAT use reported at heads up to at least 75 m in water-supply practice ([PAT technology review (2024)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10881369/); [IST Lisbon thesis](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/844820067128630/TeseMiguel_vsCorrigida.pdf)).

Conventional micro-hydroturbines are likewise a mature, global market, but a more specialized one: Gilkes reports more than 1,000 Turgo installations across 65 countries ([Gilkes](https://www.gilkes.com/new-hydropower-projects/turbines-control-systems/)), Ossberger has delivered over 10,000 crossflow turbines ([France Hydro](https://www.france-hydro-electricite.fr/fournisseur/ossberger-gmbh/)), and Mavel lists more than 400 projects in 43 countries in the 30 kW–30 MW band ([Mavel/JHD](https://jhd.co.jp/en/products/)). The in-conduit niche tailored to water networks is younger and thinner but real: Lucid Energy's LucidPipe holds NSF/ANSI 61 drinking-water certification for its 24-inch and 42-inch units and operated commercially in Portland from 2015 ([Renewable Energy World](https://www.renewableenergyworld.com/hydro-power/lucid-energys-in-pipe-hydropower-plant-begins-operation-in-portland/)); InPipe Energy launched its HydroXS product line in 2022 and by early 2024 had installations including East Bay MUD's 130,000 kWh/yr unit ([PRWeb HydroXS launch](https://www.prweb.com/releases/inpipe-energy-announces-the-hydroxs-next-generation-technology-that-produces-renewable-energy-from-water-pipelines-848143253.html); [WaterPower Magazine on EBMUD](https://www.waterpowermagazine.com/news/ebmud-installs-inpipe-energys-hydroxs-in-conduit-hydroelectricity-system-11418287/)); and Rentricity has documented in-pipe projects from Halifax to New York, including a two-turbine scheme that took a water facility to net-zero energy ([Rentricity Halifax case study](https://rentricity.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Halifax-Case-Study-2014.pdf); [AWWA Opflow via Rentricity](https://rentricity.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Rentricity-AWWA-Opflow-12-2017-In-pipe-Hydro-Achieves-Net-Zero-Energy.pdf)). Certification matters here as a market gate: NSF/ANSI 61 (with 372) is effectively mandatory for potable-contact equipment across 48 US states and 11 Canadian provinces and territories ([Valve User magazine](https://www.valveuser.com/2764-oxford-flow-awarded-drinking-water-nsfansi-61-and-372-accreditation.htm)).

## Part 2 — Summary Table

The table below condenses Part 1 into the six requested dimensions, stating the characteristic advantages and disadvantages of each family. Two cross-cutting observations should be kept in mind while reading it. First, the comparison is asymmetric by design: a PAT is a component (a pump) repurposed as a turbine, whereas a conventional micro-hydroturbine is an engineered machine, so the PAT's weaknesses (efficiency, regulation, life) can be partially bought back with control equipment, and the turbine's strengths must be paid for in capital and engineering hours. Second, the right question for a water utility is rarely "which machine is better" but "which architecture fits this pressure-reduction duty", because the network's flow variability, redundancy requirements and certification obligations usually decide the outcome before efficiency or cost do.

Read as a whole, the table explains the observed market pattern: where flow is steady and heads are high (reservoir inlets, break tanks), conventional turbines defend their efficiency and longevity advantage; where the duty is pressure reduction under highly variable urban demand — the dominant case in distribution networks — the PAT's cheapness, simplicity and replaceability outweigh its weaker efficiency, which is why the recent literature and the vendor landscape (REDAWN, ANDRITZ, Canyon, packaged in-PRV products) concentrate on PAT-based architectures for that niche ([REDAWN ebook](https://www.ateneapolieditore.it/redawn/redawn_ebook_inglese.pdf)). Either way, the electricity is only part of the value: better pressure management itself cuts leakage and main-break frequency substantially — one vendor's analysis of 112 systems associates optimized pressure control with a 38% average pressure reduction, 53% fewer pipe breaks and a 38% leakage reduction — so energy-recovery retrofits that improve pressure control inherit these network benefits regardless of machine choice ([Danfoss pressure management](https://www.danfoss.com/en-us/industries/water-and-wastewater/dcs/water-supply-network-pressure-management/)).

## Part 4 — Main Economic and Technical Challenges for Deployment in Urban Water Supply Networks

### 4.1 Technical Challenges

**Mismatch between machine physics and network duty.** A distribution network's flow is set by consumer demand, not by the energy-recovery device, so any turbine installed in a main operates mostly away from its design point — precisely the regime where PATs and fixed-geometry machines lose efficiency fastest (Figure 1). Classical mini-turbines, moreover, were designed for hydropower sites and "can hardly be scaled down" economically to distribution-system sizes, and no turbine by itself provides downstream pressure regulation, so auxiliary control equipment is unavoidable ([Water Resour. Manage. (2023)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-023-03575-0)). For PATs the selection problem compounds this: manufacturers publish pump-mode curves only, so the turbine-mode best efficiency point at the site's actual head and flow must be predicted or measured, and a mis-selected unit quietly underperforms for years — the gap that the REDAWN performance database and selection tools were created to close ([IST thesis](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/844820067128630/TeseMiguel_vsCorrigida.pdf); [University of Bath REDAWN](https://www.bath.ac.uk/case-studies/reducing-energy-dependency-in-atlantic-area-water-networks-redawn/)).

**Hydraulic safety and redundancy.** Inserting a rotating machine into a potable main creates failure modes the utility must engineer away: when a generator breaker trips, the machine unloads almost instantly and the resulting overpressure surge can threaten pipeline integrity, so surge analysis and relief/bypass provisions are mandatory in conduit-hydro design ([Canyon Hydro](https://www.canyonhydro.com/projects/conduit.html)). Cavitation at off-design operation erodes runners and injects noise; fire-flow and maintenance outages demand a bypass capable of carrying full demand at controlled pressure; and all of this must fit inside existing vaults or new chambers on congested urban sites. Water-quality compliance is a further gate — equipment contacting drinking water effectively requires NSF/ANSI 61 (and 372) certification across 48 US states and 11 Canadian provinces and territories, which only a few in-conduit products have obtained ([Valve User magazine](https://www.valveuser.com/2764-oxford-flow-awarded-drinking-water-nsfansi-61-and-372-accreditation.htm); [Renewable Energy World](https://www.renewableenergyworld.com/hydro-power/lucid-energys-in-pipe-hydropower-plant-begins-operation-in-portland/)).

**Electrical integration.** Units of 20–250 kW must either feed the grid through certified protection, metering and interconnection arrangements — often disproportionately costly at this scale — or displace on-site consumption at a pump station or treatment plant, which caps usable output at the site's own demand. Variable-speed (ER) configurations add drive harmonics and still cannot guarantee back-pressure control under all network states, which is why hybrid HR/ER architectures and conservative bypass-dominated designs prevail in practice ([Renewable Energy (2022)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960148122007790)). The operational consequence is that energy recovery is almost always subordinated to the network's primary pressure-management function, and machine dispatch follows hydraulics rather than electricity prices.

### 4.2 Economic Challenges

**Small absolute revenues against lumpy costs.** Even a well-sited urban unit produces hundreds of megawatt-hours per year at most — Portland's 200 kW LucidPipe array yields about 1,100 MWh/yr, Hillsboro's In-PRV about 203 MWh/yr, and EBMUD's HydroXS about 130 MWh/yr ([Lucid case study](https://climatesmartwater.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/LucidEnergy-PortlandCaseStudy-2016-10-lr.pdf); [InPipe white paper](https://www.inpipeenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/InPipe_Micro-Hydro_Hillsboro_White-Paper.pdf)). REDAWN's pilots valued recovered energy at only around 0.036 €/m³ supplied per year ([REDAWN ebook](https://www.ateneapolieditore.it/redawn/redawn_ebook_inglese.pdf)). At typical wholesale or retail electricity prices, outputs of this size translate into gross revenues of only tens of thousands of dollars per year per site, from which fixed charges for interconnection, metering, insurance and any telemetry must still be deducted. Against this stand the diseconomies of small scale documented in Part 1: turnkey small-hydro costs of US$1,300–8,000/kW, low-head installations up to US$9,000/kW, and Portland's ~US$8,500/kW in-conduit build ([IRENA via Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2016/8037892); [The Oregonian](https://www.oregonlive.com/environment/2015/01/portland_company_that_built_ci.html)).

**Payback dispersion and revenue risk.** Reported economics span an extraordinary range — from a 53-day payback at Antalya's high-utilization PAT site to the Portland project, which required a 20-year power purchase agreement at 8 US¢/kWh with a 4% annual escalator to be financeable ([Water Science (2018)](https://iwaponline.com/ws/article-abstract/18/1/130/38216); [MSW Magazine](https://www.mswmag.com/editorial/2016/07/converting_water_pressure_into_power)). Revenue is hostage to demand variability, to electricity prices that rarely reward sub-MW generators, and to regulatory treatment of self-generation; capital grant dependence is common in the documented municipal projects. Life-cycle analysis therefore matters more than first cost: models that annualize PAT replacement every ~10 years still find PAT schemes competitive at small scale, but the same models show returns collapsing when sites have low capacity factors or short daily pressure surplus windows ([Novara 2019](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/abs/10.1061/(ASCE)WR.1943-5452.0001063)).

**Soft costs and organizational barriers.** Feasibility studies, hydraulic transient analysis, certification, interconnection applications and procurement overheads do not scale down with unit size, so they weigh disproportionately on the smallest — and otherwise most numerous — urban sites. The REDAWN project explicitly catalogued such barriers for Atlantic-area water utilities: lack of performance data, absence of standardized selection procedures, and institutional unfamiliarity with generation assets among water-only organizations ([University of Bath REDAWN](https://www.bath.ac.uk/case-studies/reducing-energy-dependency-in-atlantic-area-water-networks-redawn/)). Utilities' core regulatory duty is safe, continuous supply, so decision-makers rationally discount energy revenue against any perceived risk to pressure, quality or redundancy — a structural conservatism that packaged, certified, bypass-guaranteed products are only beginning to overcome ([InPipe white paper](https://www.inpipeenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/InPipe_Micro-Hydro_Hillsboro_White-Paper.pdf)).

## Conclusions

Within the scope of information available up to early 2024, the comparison supports a differentiated verdict rather than a universal winner. Conventional micro-hydroturbines retain clear advantages in best-point efficiency (80–90%+), partial-load robustness (especially impulse machines and double-regulated Kaplan units), onboard flow regulation, and 25-year-plus service life, making them the natural choice at steady, well-characterized, hydropower-like sites such as reservoir inlets and break tanks. PATs counter with an order-of-magnitude lower procurement cost, global off-the-shelf availability, minimal mechanical complexity and adequate 60–85% peak efficiency, at the price of a narrow efficiency dome, no inherent pressure regulation and roughly a decade of service life — weaknesses that are managed, not eliminated, by hydraulic or electric regulation architectures and by treating the machine as a replaceable component. For the characteristic urban duty — replacing PRVs under highly variable demand — the documented installations and the research literature converge on PAT-based or packaged in-conduit architectures with PRV bypasses as the pragmatic mainstream, while purpose-designed turbines defend the high-head, steady-flow fringe.

The remaining challenges are less about machine efficiency than about systems and institutions: predicting turbine-mode performance without manufacturer curves, guaranteeing fail-safe pressure control and surge protection, achieving potable-water certification, financing small revenue streams against lumpy soft costs, and building utility confidence in generation equipment. Progress on each of these fronts — open PAT performance databases, certified in-pipe products, hybrid regulation schemes and standardized packages — was visible in the public record up to early 2024 and determines how much of the considerable urban pressure-surplus resource is ultimately recovered. For practitioners, the near-term opportunity lies in systematic screening of existing PRV stations for continuous pressure surplus, since the documented successes share one trait: they monetized pressure reduction that the network had to perform anyway, turning a mandatory operating cost into the project's revenue foundation.
