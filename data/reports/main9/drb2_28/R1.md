## Conventional micro-hydroturbines versus pumps as turbines

A useful starting point is that “conventional micro-hydroturbine” is not one machine. It includes impulse machines such as Pelton, Turgo, and crossflow turbines and reaction machines such as Francis, Kaplan, semi-Kaplan, propeller, and tubular variants. Their behavior therefore depends strongly on turbine family, head, flow, and regulating hardware. By contrast, a pump-as-turbine (PAT) is a standard pump operated with reversed flow so that the impeller supplies shaft power rather than absorbing it. Pump manufacturers normally optimize and publish curves for pumping operation, not turbine operation, which is one reason PAT selection is less straightforward than its mechanical simplicity might suggest. [[1]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1155/2014/139868)

**BEP and peak efficiency.** Conventional turbines can be hydraulically optimized for the particular site. EPRI reports design-flow efficiency above 90% for Pelton units and peak efficiency above 90% for well-designed Francis turbines; its representative conduit-hydropower curves show Francis reaching about 94% near its optimum and full Kaplan roughly 92–93% over much of its upper operating range. These curves are indicative rather than guaranteed micro-scale values: efficiency generally falls as unit size decreases and depends on fabrication quality, generator losses, specific speed, and installation. [[2]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

For conventional units, the **best efficiency point (BEP)** is the operating condition at which turbine efficiency reaches its maximum; hydraulic behavior degrades as the operating point moves away from BEP, and sufficiently severe off-design operation can increase vibration, pressure pulsation, or cavitation risk. EPRI's representative curves illustrate the substantial difference among turbine families: Pelton, crossflow, and full Kaplan retain relatively high efficiency over broad flow ranges, while Francis and especially fixed-blade propeller machines have steeper part-load deterioration. [[3]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

A PAT also has a BEP, but a standard fixed-speed PAT has essentially one preferred turbine-mode head-flow operating point because it lacks the wicket gates, adjustable runner blades, or jet needles used by regulated conventional turbines. An experimental/review literature synthesis reported turbine-mode efficiencies of roughly **60–80%** for tested centrifugal pumps, although individual units can be lower or higher; another 2022 study notes PAT mechanical efficiency at BEP can reach about 85%. Critically, turbine-mode BEP occurs at different head and flow from the manufacturer's pump-mode BEP, and manufacturers normally do not provide the reverse-mode performance curve. [[4]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1155/2014/139868)

**Part-load efficiency.** This is where the conventional/PAT distinction is especially important for urban water networks, whose demand varies hourly. The EPRI reference curve gives a full Kaplan about 80% efficiency at 20% of design flow and roughly 92–93% from around 40% upward; Pelton remains around 80% even at roughly 10% design flow and near the high-80s over most of its operating range. A segmented crossflow curve is similarly broad. Francis, by contrast, falls from roughly 94% near 85–90% design flow to around 72% at 50% and approximately 60% at 40%; a fixed propeller turbine has a particularly narrow high-efficiency region. These values are representative curves, not universal guarantees. [[5]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

A single fixed-speed PAT is generally less tolerant of a strongly variable head-flow pattern. When demand shifts away from the selected turbine-mode BEP, its head-drop and efficiency no longer necessarily match what the distribution network requires. Published WDN studies therefore use bypass valves, parallel PATs, variable rotational speed, or combinations of PAT and PRV control. The need for such regulation is not merely an energy issue: excessive turbine head loss can reduce downstream pressure below the service requirement. [[6]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211)

**Procurement and equipment cost.** PAT's principal economic advantage is that pumps are standardized, mass-produced products with mature supply chains. The Dŵr Uisce demonstration program reports compact dimensions, shorter delivery, easier maintenance, unrestricted spare-parts availability, reduced installation cost, and—in some applications—PAT purchase cost as low as approximately one-tenth that of a bespoke hydroturbine of comparable output. A separate microhydro review likewise identifies mass production and low initial cost as core PAT advantages. Those ratios should be treated as project examples, not universal procurement factors: the generator, variable-speed drive, bypass assembly, controls, instrumentation, civil works, and grid equipment can greatly reduce the apparent whole-project saving. [[7]](https://www.dwr-uisce.eu/news/2019/11/4/demonstrating-the-pump-as-turbine-as-a-creative-means-of-energy-recovery)

Conventional microturbines generally have higher equipment cost because the runner, casing, control hardware, and sometimes generator are selected or engineered to a specific hydraulic duty. The benefit is that the machine can be deliberately matched to site head, flow, cavitation limits, downstream pressure, and expected flow-duration curve rather than accepting a commercial pump geometry and then adapting system controls around it. [[8]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

**Installation and total project cost.** Equipment price alone is a poor proxy for urban in-conduit project cost. California's public in-conduit guidebook reports that total project cost includes civil, mechanical, electrical, regulatory, and permitting costs. In its survey of California opportunities, projects below 100 kW ranged from roughly **$10,000–$30,000/kW**, averaging about $28,000/kW; 100–1,000 kW projects averaged about $9,000/kW and 1–5 MW projects about $3,500/kW. The guide emphasizes that these are California project-level estimates, not universal turbine prices, and that economies of scale are substantial. [[9]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

This helps explain an important practical point: replacing a bespoke turbine with a cheap PAT does **not** reduce excavation, isolation valves, bypass piping, electrical switchgear, metering, protection, SCADA integration, buildings/vaults, engineering, permitting, or interconnection costs in proportion to the machine-price saving. PATs can therefore deliver a dramatic reduction in turbine procurement cost while producing a much smaller percentage reduction in total installed project cost. [[10]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

**Operation and maintenance.** PATs benefit from conventional pump construction, familiar bearings and seals, widely available replacement parts, and a broad maintenance knowledge base. A 2022 PAT study describes pumps as robust, low-maintenance machines and notes that pump life can exceed 20 years in suitable service. [[11]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148122007790)

Public guidebook estimates do not demonstrate a huge O&M-cost difference once a complete conduit plant is installed. California's planning worksheet used rough annual allowances of approximately **$6,000/year for a PAT, $5,000–$7,000/year for Pelton/Turgo, and $10,000/year for Francis**. Those are planning defaults, not normalized $/kW values and therefore should not be generalized across project sizes; they are most useful in showing that the cost distinction depends on the complete installation, not simply whether a pump or turbine runner is used. [[12]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

**Pressure regulation.** Conventional reaction turbines have the strongest inherent fit where a pressurized downstream network must be preserved. Francis turbines can regulate flow with guide/wicket vanes; Kaplan machines add adjustable runner pitch, giving broad operating flexibility, although at greater mechanical and hydraulic-control complexity. The California guidebook explicitly notes that where downstream pressure must be retained, reaction turbines are generally preferable; impulse machines such as Pelton and Turgo normally discharge to atmosphere. [[13]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

Pelton and Turgo offer excellent **flow regulation** through needle valves and jet deflectors, but that is different from maintaining a pressurized downstream main: conventional versions discharge into an atmospheric chamber, so the residual pressure downstream is lost. This can make them unsuitable as direct substitutes for a PRV where downstream pressure must remain above a prescribed level. [[14]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

A fixed-speed PAT has no inherent adjustable hydraulic regulating apparatus. Pressure control is therefore a **system-level** function obtained through a bypass valve, residual PRV, variable-speed drive, multiple PATs in parallel, or other supervisory control. PATs can consequently perform the energy-recovery role of a PRV, but their pressure-control quality is highly dependent on controls and correct sizing against the network's demand pattern. [[15]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1155/2014/139868)

**Design and operational complexity.** A conventional regulated turbine is mechanically more sophisticated: a Kaplan may require blade-pitch servomotors and guide-vane control, and Francis machines rely on governing/wicket-gate systems. That complexity buys a wider controllable operating envelope. EPRI explicitly describes the variable-blade Kaplan hydraulic system as relatively complex. [[16]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

The PAT itself is mechanically simple, but selection and plant control can be deceptively complex. Because manufacturers generally publish only pump-mode curves, turbine-mode head, flow, efficiency, and BEP must be measured or predicted; published PAT-selection work reports substantial uncertainty among empirical prediction methods. A WDN operator must then ensure that any chosen PAT and controller satisfy minimum pressure for every relevant demand state, including abnormal and low-demand operation. [[4]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1155/2014/139868)

**Service life.** Conventional hydropower has unusually long asset lives. IRENA gives a typical small-hydropower project life of roughly **40 years**, while noting that actual life varies; its broader cost work notes that electromechanical hydropower equipment commonly has design lives of 30 years or more before major replacement. These figures describe plants/electromechanical assets rather than guaranteeing the life of every microturbine bearing, seal, or runner. [[17]](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2012/RE_Technologies_Cost_Analysis-HYDROPOWER.pdf)

For PATs, the evidence is less standardized. Techno-economic WDN studies often assume **15 years** for generic mechanical/electrical equipment, whereas operational literature notes centrifugal pumps can exceed **20 years** under appropriate service. Accordingly, a defensible comparison is not “40 years versus 15 years” as a hard rule, but rather that bespoke hydropower has stronger evidence for long design lives and refurbishment, while PAT life is closer to standard industrial-pump practice and depends strongly on bearings, seals, rotational speed, water quality, cavitation, and off-design duty. [[18]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960148120313537)

**Market availability.** PATs have a decisive supply-chain advantage because standard centrifugal pumps are manufactured in large volumes, are available across numerous head/flow classes, and have broadly interchangeable service knowledge and spare parts. The tradeoff is that a pump catalog is not a turbine catalog; reverse-performance data may be absent. [[19]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1155/2014/139868)

Conventional microturbines are commercially mature but are supplied by a smaller, specialist hydropower market. Equipment can be very well matched to the hydraulic site, but procurement tends to involve more application engineering and potentially longer delivery than an off-the-shelf pump. In conduit hydropower, EPRI also warns that some unusual high-head/low-flow or low-head/high-flow operating points fall outside standard conventional-turbine envelopes, which can narrow the vendor choice further. [[20]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

Two cautions are particularly important when using such tables. First, the application envelope is not an efficiency guarantee: a machine near an envelope boundary may be technically feasible but economically unattractive. Second, **pressure regulation is a plant property as well as a turbine property**. A PAT with a VFD and controlled bypass can regulate a WDN more effectively than a bare fixed-speed PAT, while even a high-efficiency impulse turbine may be unusable if it discharges to atmosphere and the downstream main requires substantial residual pressure. [[44]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

The “spherical” category should also be treated differently from mature Pelton/Francis/Kaplan classifications. Public California guidance described LucidPipe as an inline spherical turbine for pressurized pipelines, available in pipe diameters roughly 24–60 inches with nominal unit capacities of about 18–100 kW and minimum flows of 35–198 ft³/s, but the research literature remains much more geometry-specific than for conventional turbines. Consequently, a universal head or efficiency envelope for “spherical turbines” would imply a maturity and standardization that the evidence does not support. [[45]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

## Economic and technical challenges in urban water-distribution energy recovery

**The water network remains the primary asset.** In a conventional hydro project the developer can often optimize water routing for generation; in a conduit project, available head and flow are determined by the water system's existing operating requirements. EPRI emphasizes that these conditions generally cannot be altered simply to maximize electricity generation and that the principal design objective is to avoid degrading the reliability or operation of the water-transfer system. A water-supply outage can have consequences far larger than lost electricity revenue. [[33]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

That constraint fundamentally changes turbine sizing. Urban consumption produces hourly, daily and seasonal variation in flow and pressure. The turbine must therefore be selected from a **flow-duration and pressure-duration distribution**, not merely from average values. A machine chosen for peak power can spend too much time at inefficient part load; a machine chosen for average flow may impose excessive head loss at high demand or require substantial bypassing. PAT research repeatedly identifies variability and off-design operation as central selection problems. [[46]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

**Residual-pressure obligations limit recoverable energy.** The theoretical hydraulic power in a pipe section is not all available to the turbine. A minimum downstream pressure must remain for customers, firefighting, storage filling, treatment processes and other service requirements. California's conduit guidance therefore lists head/friction loss, flow availability/stability and downstream pressure as primary turbine-selection inputs and observes that reaction turbines are favored where downstream line pressure must be retained. [[47]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

This is especially important for PRV-replacement schemes. A PRV may tolerate large instantaneous changes in demand while maintaining a pressure set point, whereas a fixed-speed PAT has a fixed head-flow characteristic. If the demanded flow would force excessive PAT head drop, a bypass must open or speed must change. Conversely, at low flow a PAT may recover little energy while the network still needs pressure management. The optimization objective is therefore dual: maximize recovered energy **subject to** hydraulic service constraints. [[30]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211)

**Part-load operation, transients and cavitation create reliability risk.** Operation far from BEP can produce unfavorable internal flow, vibration, pressure pulsation and cavitation. EPRI specifically links deteriorating off-design conditions to mechanical damage and reduced equipment longevity, while Bureau of Reclamation research similarly identifies off-design cavitation, pressure pulsation and vibration as turbine O&M concerns. [[48]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

Urban systems also experience pump switching, valve movement, reservoir-level changes and emergency flow events. An energy-recovery installation adds rotating inertia and controlled hydraulic resistance to an already transient network. Safe design therefore requires surge/water-hammer analysis, fail-safe bypassing, overspeed protection, isolation valves and control logic ensuring that a power or communication failure cannot compromise water supply. This is an engineering consequence of the primary-system reliability requirement emphasized in conduit-hydropower guidance. [[33]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

**Controls can erase some of PAT's apparent simplicity.** A fixed-speed, stable-flow PAT can be mechanically simple, but a variable urban network may need flow and pressure transducers, PLC control, a variable-frequency drive or power-electronic converter, an automatic bypass, and a retained PRV for fail-safe operation. Multiple PATs may be installed in parallel to obtain a wider efficient operating envelope. These additions improve energy recovery and pressure management but increase capital cost, commissioning effort, control-system failure modes and O&M requirements. [[31]](https://doi.org/10.3390/en12244738)

**Reverse-performance uncertainty complicates PAT procurement.** Pump catalogs normally characterize forward pumping mode. Turbine-mode BEP, runaway speed, efficiency, head-flow curves and cavitation behavior may therefore need prediction or testing. A 2022 PAT regulation study notes that empirical prediction uncertainty can be large, while earlier work similarly identifies the lack of manufacturer turbine-mode curves as a central difficulty. This uncertainty matters economically because a poorly matched PAT can spend most operating hours away from BEP. [[49]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148122007790)

**Small projects suffer from fixed costs and diseconomies of scale.** The California in-conduit survey is unusually clear on this issue: projects below 100 kW were estimated at roughly $10,000–$30,000/kW, while larger installations had much lower average unit costs. Civil works, electrical systems, regulatory/permitting tasks and engineering remain necessary even when the turbine itself is only a few kilowatts. Thus a very inexpensive PAT can still be embedded in an expensive project. [[9]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

**Interconnection can be disproportionate to project value.** California case studies reported interconnection costs ranging from zero to approximately $250,000 depending on grid location and infrastructure, and the guidebook explicitly warns that such costs can overwhelm small projects. Where generated power is consumed behind the meter at a treatment plant or pumping facility, economics may be much better than where a small generator requires a costly export interconnection. [[9]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

**Revenue depends on when and where electricity is used.** Financial viability depends on capital cost, annual O&M, energy production and the value of that energy. Behind-the-meter generation can offset retail electricity purchases, whereas exported electricity may receive a lower tariff and incur more interconnection expense. Consequently, identical hydraulic resources can have very different payback periods depending on utility tariff, self-consumption fraction and financing assumptions. [[50]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

**Payback estimates are exceptionally site sensitive.** Published PAT case studies range from very attractive short paybacks to uneconomic outcomes. One dynamic WDN analysis found roughly 1–3 year capital payback at favorable installation points but as much as 14–15 years when location was poorly chosen; another optimization study found attractive cases below three years but other configurations with very long payback. These results should not be transferred directly between cities because head, flow duration, energy tariff, civil works and equipment sizing differ. [[51]](https://iwaponline.com/jh/article/17/3/347/3663/Pump-as-turbine-implementation-in-a-dynamic)

**Physical integration into existing urban infrastructure is difficult.** Conduit projects often face restricted vault dimensions, buried mains, limited crane access, traffic-management constraints, existing valves and meters, and inability to take the pipeline out of service for long periods. EPRI notes that conduit installations must fit the existing primary-use system and often face spatial limitations that strongly influence turbine orientation and selection. [[52]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)

**Potable-water requirements constrain equipment and materials.** Any wetted machine in a drinking-water main must satisfy applicable sanitary/material requirements and avoid contamination from lubricants, coatings, corrosion products or maintenance activity. Some standard pump families already have drinking-water certifications, which can be an advantage for PAT deployment, but certification is jurisdiction- and model-specific rather than automatic. A 2022 PAT study specifically identifies sanitary conformity as one practical advantage available for suitable pump products. [[11]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148122007790)

**O&M access and lifecycle economics must include more than the runner.** Hydropower's long asset life is an advantage only if bearings, seals, actuators, generators, power electronics and control systems are inspectable and replaceable. PATs may benefit from inexpensive pump spares; bespoke turbines may benefit from longer runner/design life. Either way, life-cycle analysis must account for periodic overhaul and possible replacement rather than comparing only first cost. IRENA notes that hydropower electromechanical equipment commonly has multi-decade design life but still requires eventual refurbishment. [[53]](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2013/Overview_Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2012.pdf)

**Data quality can determine whether a project succeeds.** Reliable high-frequency records of flow, upstream pressure, downstream pressure and demand are necessary to estimate recoverable energy and select a turbine. Public conduit guidance identifies reliable flow data and flow stability as feasibility issues. Designs based solely on nominal pipe size, a single pressure reading or average annual flow risk materially overstating annual energy yield. [[47]](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-05/CEC-500-2020-030.pdf)

Taken together, the evidence through early 2024 indicates that **PATs have the strongest advantage where the recoverable head is moderate, the flow-duration curve is reasonably concentrated, standard pump sizes fit the duty, and low equipment cost is decisive**. Conventional regulated microturbines become progressively more attractive where high peak efficiency, broad part-load efficiency, precise pressure retention, or long service life has sufficient economic value to justify higher procurement cost. In urban WDNs, however, the decisive feasibility question is usually not turbine efficiency alone: it is whether the complete installation can recover enough otherwise-wasted pressure energy while preserving water-service reliability and absorbing the substantial fixed costs of civil integration, controls and electrical interconnection. [[54]](https://restservice.epri.com/publicdownload/000000003002006369/0/Product)
