# Turo and Getaround: A Comparative Performance and Valuation Study (2020–2025)

## I. Background and Key Terms

Turo and Getaround are the two largest American peer-to-peer (P2P) car-sharing platforms. Both were founded in 2009 in San Francisco, and both run online marketplaces that connect private car owners (called "hosts") with people who want to rent a vehicle for a short period (called "guests"). Unlike traditional rental car companies such as Hertz, Avis, or Enterprise, neither Turo nor Getaround owns the cars on its platform; instead, the companies take a commission—typically 10% to 40% of the rental fee—on each transaction.

To follow the story of their financial performance, a few venture capital (VC) and stock-market terms are useful:

- **Valuation** is the total price investors are willing to pay for a company, calculated by multiplying the price per share by the total number of shares. A "unicorn" is a private startup valued at $1 billion or more.
- **Venture capital (VC) funding** is money raised from investors in exchange for partial ownership of a private company. These rounds are labeled Series A, B, C, etc., with later letters generally meaning the company is more mature and each round carrying a higher valuation.
- An **IPO (Initial Public Offering)** is when a private company first sells shares to the general public on a stock exchange.
- A **SPAC (Special Purpose Acquisition Company)** is a "blank-check" shell company that goes public first and then merges with a private company, taking the private company public without a traditional IPO.
- **Market capitalization (market cap)** is the share price multiplied by the number of outstanding shares; it changes constantly as the stock price moves.
- An **S-1 filing** is the legal document a company must submit to U.S. regulators (the SEC) before going public; it contains detailed financial and operational data.
- **Adjusted EBITDA** is a profitability measure that strips out interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, plus one-time items. Investors watch it closely for high-growth companies.
- **Delisting** happens when a public company's stock is removed from a major exchange, usually because the share price has fallen too low, and the stock moves to over-the-counter (OTC) "penny stock" markets.

By 2020, both companies had reached unicorn status. What followed was a sharp divergence: Turo grew into the dominant survivor; Getaround collapsed.

## II. Turo's Trajectory: From Pandemic Boom to IPO Withdrawal

### II.1 The 2020 Pandemic Shock and 2021 Rebound

Just before the pandemic, in July 2019, Turo had raised $250 million in a Series E round led by IAC (InterActiveCorp, a U.S. media company) at a valuation above $1 billion, formally making it a unicorn. In February 2020, it added a $30 million extension to that round, putting its paper valuation at about $1.24 billion [1][2].

In 2020, COVID-19 halted almost all travel. Turo's revenue fell to $149.9 million, only 6% higher than 2019, and the company posted a net loss of $97.1 million [3]. However, the pandemic ultimately helped Turo in a critical way: the major traditional rental companies (Hertz, Avis, Enterprise) sold off large portions of their fleets to take advantage of high used-car prices, and when travel resumed, they could not restock fast enough. With rental cars scarce, Turo's peer-to-peer model suddenly looked very attractive. In 2021, Turo's revenue jumped 213% to $469 million [3][4].

### II.2 Profitability and the IPO Filing (2022)

In 2022, Turo's revenue climbed another 59% to $746.6 million, and the company posted its first annual net profit of $154.7 million [3]. On the strength of these results, Turo confidentially filed for an IPO in late 2021 and publicly filed its S-1 in January 2022, with plans to list on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker "TURO" [5][6]. At the time, analysts estimated the company could be valued at $2–3 billion and was hoping to raise roughly $300 million [6].

### II.3 Growth Deceleration and the 2025 IPO Withdrawal

After 2022, Turo's growth rate slowed sharply. Revenue grew 18% in 2023 to $879.7 million, then only 9% in 2024 to $958 million; net income collapsed from $154.7 million in 2022 to $14.7 million in 2023, and the first nine months of 2024 actually showed a $9.7 million operating loss [3][7]. The slowdown was driven by traditional rental companies rebuilding their fleets, more cautious consumer spending, rising customer acquisition costs, and heavier investment in safety and insurance.

On January 1, 2025, two Turo-rented vehicles were used in deadly attacks: a Ford F-150 Lightning truck was driven into a New Year crowd in New Orleans, killing 15 people, and a Tesla Cybertruck exploded outside Trump International Hotel in Las Vegas, killing one person [8][9]. Both renters had passed Turo's background checks, but the incidents triggered heavy public scrutiny of the platform's vetting process and prompted Turo to shut down its keyless "Turo Go" service by the end of January 2025 [9][10].

In February 2025, Turo's board formally withdrew the IPO filing, with CEO Andre Haddad stating, "now is not the right time for Turo to pursue a public offering" [5][6]. The combination of decelerating growth, reputational damage, and an unfavorable IPO market made a public debut unattractive. In April 2025, Turo laid off about 15% of its workforce, roughly 150 employees, as part of a cost-cutting pivot toward remaining private [7][11].

### II.4 Turo's Valuation and Workforce Trajectory

Turo's last primary funding round was the September 2024 secondary share sale, which valued the company at roughly $1.7–$1.9 billion [1][12]. As of late 2025, secondary-market indicators from platforms such as Forge and Hiive put Turo's implied valuation in the $1.2–$1.7 billion range, well below the $2–$3 billion floated in early 2022 [12][13]. The Hurun Global Unicorn Index 2025 listed Turo at $2.0 billion in enterprise value, ranking it 418th [14]. The valuation compression reflects slower growth, the failed IPO, and the January 2025 safety incidents.

The April 2025 layoff of ~150 people was a direct consequence of the IPO withdrawal and the company's shift to cost control as a private business.

## III. Getaround's Trajectory: From SPAC Boom to Insolvency

### III.1 Pre-2020 Background and the COVID Cliff

Getaround had raised $300 million in a 2018 Series D round led by Japan's SoftBank Vision Fund, reaching a valuation above $1 billion and also achieving unicorn status. In April 2019, the company paid $300 million to acquire Drivy, Europe's largest peer-to-peer car-sharing platform, instantly making Getaround the biggest P2P car-sharing operator in Europe [17][18].

COVID-19 hit Getaround much harder than Turo. In January 2020, the company laid off about 150 people, or 25% of its workforce, and in March 2020, Bloomberg reported that demand had dropped roughly 75% and that the company was running low on cash and looking for a buyer [19][20]. Later in 2020, Getaround raised roughly $140 million in a debt-and-equity round from Reinvent Capital (the investment firm of LinkedIn co-founder Reid Hoffman and Zynga founder Mark Pincus) to keep operating [21].

### III.2 The SPAC Gamble of December 2022

Facing a difficult IPO market, Getaround chose a SPAC merger instead of a traditional IPO. In May 2022 it announced a deal to merge with InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp., and the deal closed on December 9, 2022, with Getaround trading on the NYSE under the ticker "GETR" at an initial equity value of $1.2 billion [22][23][24].

The market reaction was brutal. The stock opened at around $10 per share and fell as much as 65% on its first day; by the end of 2022, it traded around $0.65, giving the company a market cap of about $241 million—roughly 80% below the SPAC valuation [22][25][26]. In 2023, the company received a series of delisting warnings from the NYSE because its share price stayed below $1 for long stretches, and because it delayed filing audited financial statements [27].

### III.3 The Downward Spiral (2023–2024)

Getaround's financial situation worsened in 2023:

- February 2023: 10% layoff, about 42 employees, as CEO Sam Zaid warned of a "challenging near-term macroeconomic outlook" [28].
- May 2023: Acquired the assets of bankrupt gig car-sharing platform HyreCar for $9.45 million to try to add revenue [29].
- Late 2023: The company gave up its San Francisco headquarters and moved to Oakland, paying $3.6 million to terminate its office lease [30].
- February 2024: Laid off 30% of its remaining North American workforce; Sam Zaid stepped down as CEO, and Eduardo Iniguez took over [31][32].

By late 2024, Getaround's market cap had fallen to roughly $6 million, and in July 2024 the NYSE suspended trading and delisted the stock; it moved to the OTC Markets under the same ticker "GETR" [33][34]. As of November 2025, the share price was about $0.0001, and the company's market cap had effectively evaporated to a few thousand dollars [26].

### III.4 The U.S. Wind-Down and Dissolution (2025–2026)

On February 11, 2025—just one day before Turo withdrew its IPO—Getaround announced the orderly wind-down of its U.S. operations, including both its core car-sharing service and the HyreCar business. Interim CEO AJ Lee said that an "ongoing lack of liquidity" had made U.S. operations "no longer viable" [35][36]. Nearly all of Getaround's U.S. employees were laid off, with most separations taking effect by February 14, 2025. AJ Lee stepped down with a $150,000 severance payment, and CFO Patricia Huerta became acting CEO [35].

In July 2025, restructuring specialist Mauricio Rivera was appointed as Chief Restructuring Officer and acting principal executive officer to lead the company's pivot to Europe [37]. By April 2026, Getaround sold its remaining European business to Danish company GoMore, and in June 2026, the board announced a plan to dissolve the company entirely [38].

After the February 2024 layoff, the U.S. headcount was further reduced, and the February 2025 wind-down eliminated nearly all remaining U.S. positions. Each round of job cuts was a direct consequence of the cash crisis triggered by the 2022 SPAC failure and the inability to attract new capital.

## V. Why the Two Companies Diverged

Three forces explain the different outcomes.

1. **Scale and unit economics.** Turo had a much larger host and guest base, a broader geographic footprint, and avoided the heavy capital expenditure of Getaround's connected-car hardware (the "Getaround Connect" in-car device). Turo's take rate and gross margin were consistently higher, allowing it to reach profitability and remain EBITDA-positive for four straight years [3][40]. Getaround, by contrast, never reached adjusted-EBITDA profitability; its cumulative net losses exceeded $700 million between 2020 and 2024 [25].

2. **Capital structure and timing.** Turo raised its last primary round in 2020 and chose to remain private when IPO conditions soured in 2022, avoiding a forced public-market valuation cut. Getaround's 2022 SPAC merger locked in a $1.2 billion valuation just as the SPAC bubble popped and as the company's own performance deteriorated; the resulting 90%+ stock collapse foreclosed its access to capital markets. With the stock below $1, the NYSE delisting followed, and new investors would not step in.

3. **Brand and trust.** Despite the January 2025 New Orleans and Las Vegas incidents, Turo retained a more diversified product, stronger brand recognition, and a partnership with Uber for car sharing announced in August 2024, all of which helped absorb reputational damage [41]. Getaround's earlier and more frequent safety and theft problems (a Washington, D.C. attorney general settlement in 2021, for example) eroded both consumer and investor confidence [42].

## VI. Effects on Employees

For both companies, valuation declines translated almost mechanically into layoffs.

- **Turo** never had a dramatic fire-sale moment; instead, when its IPO was withdrawn in early 2025, it followed with a measured 15% workforce reduction in April 2025 (about 150 people out of roughly 1,000). Headcount has continued to drift down since, with the workforce at about 713 in 2026 [15][16].
- **Getaround** experienced repeated, larger rounds: 25% in January 2020, 10% in February 2023, 30% in February 2024, and finally the elimination of nearly all remaining U.S. jobs in February 2025 when the business shut down [19][28][31][35]. The U.S. team, which had once numbered in the hundreds, was reduced to essentially zero in roughly five years.

The pattern illustrates a basic rule of VC-backed companies: when a private company cannot reach the growth and profit profile that public-market investors demand, capital becomes harder to raise, and the only adjustable cost is people.

---

## VII. Conclusion

Between 2020 and 2025, Turo and Getaround moved in opposite directions despite starting from similar positions. Turo survived the pandemic, briefly turned profitable, attempted an IPO, and then pivoted to staying private and cutting costs after safety incidents and slowing growth dented its valuation from a hoped-for $2–3 billion to roughly $1.2–$1.7 billion on the secondary market. Getaround, weakened by an aggressive 2019 European acquisition, devastated by COVID-era demand collapse, and undone by a poorly timed 2022 SPAC merger, saw its valuation crater from $1.2 billion to essentially zero; it shut down its U.S. operations, sold its European business, and began dissolving in 2026.

For employees, the difference was the difference between a painful but manageable restructuring and an outright collapse: Turo's workforce shrank gradually, while Getaround's U.S. team was eliminated in stages as the company ran out of cash. The wider lesson is that in the P2P car-sharing business, scale and disciplined unit economics matter more than hype; without them, even hundreds of millions of dollars in venture capital cannot save a company once investor confidence turns.
