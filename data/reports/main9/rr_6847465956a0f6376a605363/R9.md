# Research Report: Turo vs. Getaround Performance, Economic Events, and Valuation Effects, 2020–2025

## Executive Summary

From 2020 to 2025, Turo and Getaround moved through the same macroeconomic cycle but produced sharply different outcomes. Both benefited from the post-COVID rental-car shortage, when traditional rental companies had sold vehicles, travel demand rebounded faster than expected, and rental prices spiked. However, Turo converted that shock into scale, revenue growth, and intermittent profitability, while Getaround converted similar demand tailwinds into a short-lived public-market story followed by severe valuation collapse, delisting, layoffs, and a U.S. shutdown.

My assessment is that **Turo was the structurally stronger company** because it had a larger consumer marketplace, stronger brand recognition, better ability to monetize through host protection and service fees, and enough financial performance to keep an IPO option alive for several years. Getaround, by contrast, appears to have been a weaker public-market candidate: it listed via SPAC in late 2022 during a hostile market for speculative growth companies, lost investor confidence quickly, and later lacked the liquidity to sustain U.S. operations.

The most important valuation lesson is that **the same industry boom did not create equal enterprise value**. Turo’s private valuation likely faced pressure after 2021 because public marketplace multiples compressed sharply, but its business remained viable. Getaround’s valuation collapse was concrete and extreme: it went public at about a $1.2 billion valuation in December 2022, fell as much as 65% on its first trading day, was suspended by the NYSE in July 2024 for failing the $15 million average-market-capitalization rule, and announced the wind-down of U.S. operations in February 2025 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html); [Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-announces-nyses-suspension-trading-and-start-delisting-process-2024-07-10); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/)).

## 1. Background: How Peer-to-Peer Car Rental Works

Turo and Getaround are peer-to-peer car-sharing marketplaces. Instead of owning large fleets like Hertz, Avis, or Enterprise, they allow private car owners or small fleet operators to rent vehicles to guests through an app. This matters economically because the model is less capital-intensive than traditional car rental: the platform does not need to buy every vehicle it rents.

However, the model still has costs and risks. The marketplace must attract guests, recruit hosts, process payments, manage trust and safety, handle insurance or protection plans, and resolve claims. For Turo specifically, insurance and host protection are central to monetization. Hosts can choose different protection plans, and Turo’s platform share can range from 10% to 40% depending on coverage choice; if hosts opt out because they carry their own commercial policy, the platform share cited by MarketScale falls to 7.5%, implying lower monetization from professional operators ([MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/transportation/turo-is-scaling-its-business-model-can-its-insurance-offerings-keep-up)). Turo also offers hosts up to $750,000 in third-party liability insurance in the U.S., while physical damage and theft reimbursement vary by plan, making claims exposure a central unit-economic variable ([MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/transportation/turo-is-scaling-its-business-model-can-its-insurance-offerings-keep-up)).

For readers less familiar with venture capital and U.S. public markets: fast-growing private companies often raise venture funding at high valuations based on expected future growth. An IPO is a sale of shares to the public through a stock exchange. A SPAC is a publicly traded shell company that merges with a private company to take it public. SPACs were extremely popular in 2020–2021 but became much less attractive after interest rates rose, technology stocks fell, and many post-SPAC companies performed poorly.

## 2. Industry Events That Shaped Both Companies

### 2.1 COVID Collapse in 2020

The pandemic initially damaged all car-rental demand. U.S. car rentals fell from 44.5 million vehicles in 2019 to 17.3 million in 2020, then rebounded to 29.2 million in 2021, with estimates projecting a return above 2019 levels by 2024–2025 ([RateGenius](https://www.rategenius.com/how-covid-19-affected-car-rentals-over-past-year)). Traditional rental companies sold large portions of their fleets in 2020 to preserve cash and match lower demand, leaving the market short of cars when travel returned ([MMCG Invest](https://www.mmcginvest.com/post/u-s-car-rental-industry-2025-2030-market-analysis-investment-perspective)).

### 2.2 2021 Rental-Car Shortage and Price Spike

When travel demand returned in 2021, traditional rental companies did not have enough vehicles. Semiconductor shortages limited new-car availability, while staffing constraints further reduced capacity. Rental-car prices hit record highs in July 2021; by 2024, rental prices remained about 35% above pre-pandemic levels, compared with about 23% inflation across all items ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/travel/learn/car-rental-pricing-statistics)).

This created an unusual opening for Turo and Getaround. Travelers turned to peer-to-peer platforms because traditional rental chains lacked supply and because peer-to-peer platforms offered unique vehicles, neighborhood pickup, shorter rentals, and more personalized service ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/08/10/an-auto-rental-shortage-sparked-a-boom-in-peer-to-peer-car-sharing-.html)). Turo in particular gained visible market share: Bloomberg Second Measure reported that Turo’s sales in June 2021 were 442% higher than in June 2020 and 290% higher than in June 2019, while Turo’s share among top rental competitors rose from 2% to 6% between June 2019 and June 2021 ([Bloomberg Second Measure](https://secondmeasure.com/datapoints/top-car-rental-companies-competitors-turo-getaround-hertz-avis-enterprise/)).

### 2.3 Valuation Multiple Compression After 2021

The sector’s valuation environment changed dramatically after the 2020–2021 boom. Public marketplace companies traded at unusually high revenue and EBITDA multiples in 2020 and early 2021, helped by low interest rates and pandemic-related demand shifts. Those multiples later compressed sharply: Aventis Advisors reports that median public marketplace EV/revenue was 2.3x in 2025 versus a long-term average of 5.6x, while EV/EBITDA peaked around 53x in early 2021 before falling to 18.0x in 2025 ([Aventis Advisors](https://aventis-advisors.com/marketplace-valuation-multiples/)).

This matters for both companies. Even if revenue grew, investors were no longer willing to pay the same price for each dollar of revenue. Growth companies also faced higher discount rates as interest rates rose, making distant future profits less valuable.

## 4. Turo: Performance and Valuation Narrative

### 4.1 2020–2021: From Shock to Shortage-Driven Growth

Turo benefited from a major mismatch in the rental-car market. Traditional rental companies had reduced fleets in 2020, but consumers began traveling again in 2021. This drove travelers to alternatives, including Turo. Turo’s value proposition was broader than simply “cheaper rental cars.” It offered unique and luxury vehicles, airport or neighborhood pickup, and a host-driven experience that traditional rental counters could not always match ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/08/10/an-auto-rental-shortage-sparked-a-boom-in-peer-to-peer-car-sharing-.html)).

This translated into strong revenue growth. Turo’s revenue rose 213% in 2021 to $469 million, then 59% in 2022 to $746.6 million, and 18% in 2023 to about $879.8 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/)). The deceleration is important: it shows that Turo’s boom was partly cyclical. The company captured extraordinary demand during the rental shortage, but as the market normalized, growth slowed.

### 4.2 2022–2025: IPO Delay, Profitability Volatility, and Valuation Pressure

Turo publicly filed its S-1 in January 2022, amended it repeatedly, updated filings in 2023 and 2024, and planned to list on the NYSE under the ticker TURO ([IPO Scoop](https://www.iposcoop.com/ipo/turo-inc/)). But the IPO did not happen. Turo officially withdrew its IPO plans in February 2025 after years of delay ([The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/how-to-invest/stocks/how-to-invest-in-turo-stock/)).

Turo’s profitability record appears mixed. It reportedly earned $154.7 million of net income on $746.6 million of revenue in 2022, but net income fell to $19.4 million on $722 million of revenue through the first nine months of 2024 ([The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/how-to-invest/stocks/how-to-invest-in-turo-stock/)). That does not mean Turo was failing; it means investors would likely ask whether 2022 profitability was repeatable or partly a product of unusually favorable rental-market conditions.

The likely valuation effect was negative compared with 2021 expectations. Turo did not publish a public market capitalization because it remained private. But the external evidence points to a lower IPO valuation environment: marketplace multiples compressed after 2021, tech IPO appetite weakened, and Turo cited that it did not wish to conduct a public offering at that time amid a weak venture-backed IPO pipeline ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/shrinking-ipo-pipeline-unicorn-turo-hits-pause/); [Aventis Advisors](https://aventis-advisors.com/marketplace-valuation-multiples/)). My view is that Turo’s management rationally avoided a down-round IPO. In other words, not going public likely preserved optionality for existing shareholders, but it also confirmed that the company could not command the kind of growth-market valuation that might have been available in 2021.

### 4.3 Employee Effects

The provided evidence does not show a major Turo layoff wave comparable to Getaround’s. That absence matters. Turo’s revenue scale and continued profitability, even at a reduced level, appear to have protected it from the same employment collapse. The company’s 2025 host-facing materials even describe 5.5 million trips, 26.6 million trip days, and $1.4 billion of host earnings in 2025, suggesting continued marketplace activity, although these are company-published operating metrics and should be treated as promotional rather than audited financial data ([Turo](https://turo.com/us/en/car-rental/united-states/2025-in-rearview)).

## 5. Getaround: Performance and Valuation Narrative

### 5.1 2020–2021: Pandemic Shock, Recovery, and Venture Funding

Getaround was hurt sharply by the pandemic. A secondary data source reports that Getaround bookings initially dropped 75% in March 2020, with layoffs of 100 employees after earlier cuts of 150 in January; because this is not an SEC filing or direct company statement, it should be treated as useful but lower-confidence context ([Multiples.vc](https://multiples.vc/public-comps/getaround-valuation-multiples)). The broader market evidence supports the direction of the shock: U.S. rental demand collapsed in 2020, and travel-related platforms were under severe pressure ([RateGenius](https://www.rategenius.com/how-covid-19-affected-car-rentals-over-past-year)).

Like Turo, Getaround later benefited from the rental shortage. CNBC reported that travelers turned to both Turo and Getaround as traditional rental chains faced shortages, and that some hosts expanded fleets in response to rising demand ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/08/10/an-auto-rental-shortage-sparked-a-boom-in-peer-to-peer-car-sharing-.html)). However, Getaround’s demand profile appears different from Turo’s. CNBC described Getaround customers as often needing vehicles for errands or short neighborhood rentals, sometimes for only a few hours or one day, whereas Turo became more associated with travel, airport, and unique-vehicle use cases ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/08/10/an-auto-rental-shortage-sparked-a-boom-in-peer-to-peer-car-sharing-.html)).

### 5.2 2022: SPAC Listing at the Wrong Time

Getaround went public in December 2022 through a SPAC merger with InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp. The transaction valued Getaround at about $1.2 billion, but shares fell by as much as 65% on the first trading day ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html)).

This was not just a Getaround-specific problem. SPAC appetite had collapsed. CNBC noted that investors had become skeptical because of rising interest rates, volatility, and poor post-deal performance, with the CNBC SPAC Post Deal Index down more than 60% over the prior year ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html)). The SPAC boom had peaked earlier: 247 SPACs raised about $80 billion in 2020, and 613 SPAC listings raised $145 billion in 2021, before weak performance and regulatory scrutiny damaged the model ([Certuity](https://certuity.com/insights/what-happened-to-spacs/)).

A deeper SPAC issue is dilution. A de-SPAC’s real economics should be analyzed through net cash per share: public SPAC cash plus PIPE or forward purchase proceeds, minus expenses, warrant value, and other equity-derivative value, divided by all relevant shares. Redemptions can remove roughly $10 of cash per redeemed public share while fixed dilution from sponsor promotes, warrants, and fees remains, damaging the value delivered to ordinary shareholders ([Yale Journal on Regulation](https://www.yalejreg.com/bulletin/net-cash-per-share-the-key-to-disclosing-spac-dilution/)). The provided research does not include Getaround-specific redemption or PIPE figures, so a precise net-cash-per-share calculation is not possible. Still, the framework explains why many SPAC companies entered public markets with less cash and more dilution than headline deal values suggested.

### 5.3 2023–2025: Liquidity Crisis, Delisting, Layoffs, and U.S. Exit

Getaround’s post-listing deterioration was severe. In February 2024, it announced a restructuring that included laying off 30% of its North American staff to reduce costs, extend cash runway, globalize operations, and accelerate a path to profitability ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/transportation/ridesharing/2025/carsharing-marketplaces-getaround-and-hyrecar-wind-down-us-operations/)). In July 2024, the NYSE suspended trading and began delisting proceedings because Getaround failed NYSE Rule 802.01B, which requires listed companies to maintain an average global market capitalization of at least $15 million over 30 consecutive trading days ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-announces-nyses-suspension-trading-and-start-delisting-process-2024-07-10)). Getaround said it intended to appeal, expected OTC trading under GETR, and would remain a public reporting company during the appeal ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-announces-nyses-suspension-trading-and-start-delisting-process-2024-07-10)).

By February 2025, Getaround announced it would shut down U.S. operations and its HyreCar business, focusing on Europe. Interim CEO and COO AJ Lee said that despite profitability improvements and restructuring, the company faced an “ongoing lack of liquidity” that made U.S. operations no longer viable ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/)). PYMNTS reported that most U.S. employees would separate as of February 14, 2025, with others separating during or after the wind-down, expected to be substantially completed by the end of 2025; European employees were not affected ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/transportation/ridesharing/2025/carsharing-marketplaces-getaround-and-hyrecar-wind-down-us-operations/)).

The valuation effect was not merely a “lower multiple.” It was a collapse in market credibility and liquidity. A company valued at about $1.2 billion in its SPAC merger became too small to satisfy a $15 million NYSE market-capitalization requirement less than two years later. That is a decline of more than 98% if one compares the merger valuation to the NYSE threshold, although the exact equity value at each date depends on capital structure, debt, cash, and share count.

## 6. Why Turo Outperformed Getaround

### 6.1 Scale and Brand

Turo appears to have achieved greater marketplace scale. It was active in thousands of cities and had a stronger travel-rental identity. Earlier company data reported more than 14 million members and more than 450,000 listed vehicles, although this was from a company spokesperson and should be treated as company-provided context ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/startups/turo-raises-another-30m-to-series-e/)). Its 2025 host materials claimed 5.5 million trips and $1.4 billion in host earnings in the U.S., reinforcing that Turo remained active after withdrawing its IPO ([Turo](https://turo.com/us/en/car-rental/united-states/2025-in-rearview)).

### 6.2 Better Monetization, but With Insurance Risk

Turo’s take rate is linked to protection plans, which can raise platform revenue but also expose the company to claims cost. This model can generate strong revenue in favorable periods, but it also means profitability depends on insurance pricing, claims frequency, damage severity, and host plan selection ([MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/transportation/turo-is-scaling-its-business-model-can-its-insurance-offerings-keep-up)). Getaround also had insurance complexity, and HyreCar’s 2025 message that rentals and extensions were unavailable because of changes in its insurance program shows how insurance can directly disrupt marketplace operations ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/transportation/ridesharing/2025/carsharing-marketplaces-getaround-and-hyrecar-wind-down-us-operations/)).

### 6.3 Timing of Capital Markets

Turo delayed and ultimately withdrew its IPO. That was frustrating for investors seeking liquidity, but it avoided the reputational and valuation damage of a failed public listing. Getaround chose or needed to access public markets through a SPAC in December 2022, after investor appetite had already weakened. In my judgment, this was the decisive difference in valuation outcomes. Turo stayed private through a bad market; Getaround became publicly marked every trading day during a bad market.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Turo and Getaround were exposed to the same economic forces: COVID demand collapse, a 2021 rental shortage, high car-rental prices, rising interest rates, declining tech valuations, and tighter investor standards. But their outcomes diverged.

Turo’s valuation likely suffered relative to a 2021 IPO scenario because marketplace multiples compressed and its growth slowed. Still, Turo remained a substantial private company with hundreds of millions of dollars in annual revenue, positive though inconsistent net income, and continuing marketplace activity. Its IPO withdrawal in 2025 should be interpreted less as business failure and more as a refusal to accept unfavorable public-market pricing.

Getaround’s decline was more fundamental. It entered the public market through a SPAC at about $1.2 billion, immediately traded down, restructured, laid off staff, fell below NYSE market-capitalization requirements, moved toward OTC trading, and then exited the U.S. market because of insufficient liquidity. The company’s valuation collapse directly affected employees through layoffs in 2024 and broad U.S. separations in 2025.

The objective conclusion is that **Turo survived the post-pandemic normalization as a delayed-IPO marketplace, while Getaround became an example of SPAC-era overvaluation meeting weak unit economics and insufficient liquidity**. For investors, employees, and hosts, the distinction is critical: a delayed IPO can be a sign of valuation discipline, while a forced delisting and operating shutdown indicate a much deeper loss of economic viability.
