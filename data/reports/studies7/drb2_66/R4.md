# I've recently been focusing on colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment, and I've heard that gut microbiota significantly impacts treatment efficacy and side effects

## Gut Microbiota in Colorectal Cancer: Foundational Context

### Dysbiosis as a Driver of CRC Pathogenesis and Treatment Relevance

Gut microbial dysbiosis—defined as a compositional and functional imbalance characterized by loss of beneficial microbes, expansion of pathobionts, and reduced microbial diversity—is now recognized as a key contributor to colorectal cancer (CRC) initiation and progression ([Moreira et al. 2024, *Frontiers in Oncology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). Critically, the same carcinogenic mechanisms driven by dysbiosis—genotoxicity, chronic inflammation, and immunosuppression—also reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) and modulate response to therapy, establishing why microbiota composition matters not only for CRC risk but for treatment outcomes across surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy.

#### Bacterial Species Consistently Enriched in CRC

Multiple meta-analyses have identified a reproducible microbial signature distinguishing CRC patients from healthy controls. Across studies, *Fusobacterium nucleatum* and *Bacteroides* spp. are the most frequently enriched taxa in CRC versus healthy individuals, followed by *Escherichia coli*, *Streptococcus gallolyticus*, and *Peptostreptococcus* spp. ([Gut Bacterial Microbiome Profiles Associated with CRC Risk, *EMJ Reviews*](https://www.emjreviews.com/gastroenterology/article/gut-bacterial-microbiome-profiles-associated-with-colorectal-cancer-risk-a-narrative-review-and-meta-analysis/); [PMC9431300](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9431300/)). A 2024 global meta-analysis linked approximately 23–40% of gut bacterial species to CRC or health, split nearly evenly between CRC-associated and health-associated taxa ([Nature Scientific Reports, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-70702-1)). Commensal taxa depleted in CRC include *Akkermansia muciniphila*, *Lactobacillus rhamnosus*, *Bifidobacterium breve*, and butyrate-producing genera such as *Faecalibacterium* and *Roseburia* ([Moreira et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full); [Kang et al. 2023, *Cell Host & Microbe*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579466/)).

#### Key Carcinogenic Mechanisms

**pks⁺ *Escherichia coli* and Colibactin-Induced DNA Damage.** Strains of *E. coli* carrying the polyketide synthase (pks) genomic island produce colibactin, a genotoxic metabolite that alkylates DNA and induces interstrand cross-links and double-strand breaks (DSBs) in host colonocytes ([PMC11205849](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11205849/); [Nature Communications, 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-21162-y)). Repeated exposure to pks⁺ *E. coli* leads to chronic DSB formation, genomic instability, and a mutational signature detectable in CRC tissues, promoting tumor initiation and progression ([PMC11205849](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11205849/)). Colibactin-producing bacteria are classified as "driver" bacteria that initiate carcinogenesis through direct DNA damage, as opposed to "passenger" bacteria that expand in response to the tumor microenvironment.

**Enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis* (ETBF) and BFT-Driven Inflammation.** ETBF secretes the *Bacteroides fragilis* toxin (BFT), a zinc-dependent metalloprotease that cleaves E-cadher and disrupts tight junctions, increasing intestinal permeability. BFT triggers a procarcinogenic signaling cascade from colonic epithelial cells to a mucosal Th17 immune response, resulting in selective NF-κB activation in distal colon epithelial cells and sustained IL-17 production ([PMC4592451](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4592451/); [Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology, 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2019.00449/full)). This chronic Th17/IL-17–mediated inflammation fosters a tumor-promoting microenvironment. The *bft* gene is significantly more prevalent in the colon mucosa of CRC patients than in healthy controls, and mucosal BFT exposure is considered a risk factor for CRC development ([PMC6727388](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6727388/)). BFT also upregulates claudin-2, further compromising barrier function and facilitating bacterial translocation.

***Fusobacterium nucleatum*: Adhesion, Inflammation, and Immunosuppression.** *F. nucleatum* employs multiple virulence factors to promote CRC. Its FadA adhesin binds to E-cadher on colonic epithelial cells, activating β-catenin/Wnt signaling and upregulating oncogenes, annexin A1, and transcription factors that drive epithelial cell proliferation ([Rubinstein et al. 2013, *Cell Host & Microbe*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3770529/)). Fap2, another adhesin, mediates bacterial attachment to Gal-GalNAc residues on host cells, while lipopolysaccharide (LPS) activates TLR4 signaling, promoting M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) via a TLR4-dependent mechanism ([PMC9745098](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9745098/)). Beyond these surface molecules, *F. nucleatum* produces formate as a microbial metabolite that acts as an oncometabolite: formate activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway, increasing cancer stemness, glutamine dependency, and invasiveness ([Nature Microbiology, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35437333/)). Through these combined mechanisms—FadA/E-cadher/β-catenin signaling, LPS/TLR4-mediated inflammation, formate/AhR-driven stemness, and TAM/MDSC-mediated immunosuppression—*F. nucleatum* shapes a TME that is not only pro-carcinogenic but also resistant to subsequent therapies.

***Streptococcus gallolyticus* subsp. *gallolyticus* (Sgg).** Sgg has been associated with CRC since the 1970s, when *Streptococcus bovis* biotype 1 bacteremia was linked to right-sided colonic neoplasia. Approximately 25–80% of patients with *S. bovis* bacteremia have underlying CRC or adenomas ([PMC3032743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3032743/); [Nature Scientific Reports, 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19941-7)). Sgg stimulates CRC cell proliferation and promotes tumor growth in mouse models, though the precise molecular mechanisms remain under investigation ([AACR 2020](https://aacrjournals.org/cancerres/article/80/8_Supplement/A21/647698/Abstract-A21-Promotion-of-colorectal-cancer-by)).

**Oral Pathobionts: *Parvimonas micra*, *Porphyromonas gingivalis*, and *Peptostreptococcus* spp.** An intestinal dysbiotic microbiota enriched in oral cavity bacteria has been described in CRC patients. *Parvimonas micra*, an oral pathobiont, promotes colorectal tumorigenesis and is associated with poor prognosis in CRC patients ([Bergsten et al. 2023, *Gut Microbes*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10580862/)). *Porphyromonas gingivalis* and *Peptostreptococcus* spp. are also consistently enriched in CRC cohorts, contributing to chronic inflammation and TME remodeling ([Moreira et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)).

#### Dysbiosis and the Tumor Microenvironment

Dysbiosis alters the TME through multiple interconnected pathways that have direct implications for therapy response. Chronic inflammation driven by ETBF, *F. nucleatum*, and other pathobionts increases immunosuppressive cell populations—including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and M2-polarized TAMs—within the tumor stroma ([Frontiers in Molecular Biosciences, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/molecular-biosciences/articles/10.3389/fmolb.2023.1140325/full)). This immunosuppressive milieu not only facilitates tumor immune evasion but also predicts resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors. The loss of beneficial SCFA-producing bacteria further depletes colonocyte energy supply and weakens mucosal barrier integrity, increasing the risk of bacterial translocation and systemic inflammation—factors that can exacerbate treatment toxicity ([Moreira et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). These TME alterations create a self-reinforcing cycle: dysbiosis promotes immunosuppression and inflammation, which in turn favors the expansion of pathobionts and resistance to therapy.

#### SCFAs in Colonocyte Health and Tumor Suppression

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)—primarily butyrate, propionate, and acetate—are fermentation products of dietary fiber by commensal gut bacteria and play central roles in colonocyte health. Butyrate serves as the principal energy source for colonocytes, maintains mucosal barrier integrity, reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines, and induces apoptosis in cancerous colonocytes ([Moreira et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). Its antineoplastic effects are mediated through inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs), which alters chromatin structure and gene expression to suppress proliferation and promote differentiation ([PMC10584782](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10584782/); [Kalyanaraman et al. 2024, *Archives of Biochemistry and Biophysics*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003986124002947)). Propionate and acetate also exhibit anti-carcinogenic properties, including HDAC inhibition and GPCR-mediated signaling effects on colonocyte homeostasis ([McNabney & Henagan, 2017, *Nutrients*](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/9/12/1348)). In CRC, the depletion of butyrate-producing genera—particularly *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* and *Roseburia* spp.—reduces colonic SCFA levels, diminishing these protective effects and creating a permissive environment for tumor growth ([Kang et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579466/); [La Rosa et al. 2019, *Nature Communications*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-08812-y)). The loss of butyrate producers is one of the most consistently reported dysbiosis signatures across CRC studies.

#### Does Dysbiosis Precede or Result from Tumor Development?

Evidence supports a bidirectional relationship. On one hand, studies in germ-free and gnotobiotic mouse models demonstrate that the intestinal microbiota acts as a significant co-stimulant for tumorigenesis: axenic mice exhibit reduced incidence of pre-neoplastic lesions compared to conventionally raised mice, and pks⁺ *E. coli* exposure induces DNA damage that precedes tumor formation ([PMC11205849](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11205849/)). The mutational signatures induced by colibactin are detectable in early-stage tumors, suggesting that bacterial genotoxicity contributes to tumor initiation. On the other hand, established tumors alter the local microenvironment—increasing permeability, changing nutrient availability, and creating anaerobic niches—that favor the expansion of "passenger" bacteria such as *F. nucleatum* and *S. gallolyticus* ([PMC11205849](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11205849/)). Thus, dysbiosis both precedes and results from tumor development: early dysbiosis (driver bacteria, SCFA depletion) initiates and promotes carcinogenesis, while established tumors further reshape the microbiota to favor pathobiont expansion, creating a feedback loop that has implications for treatment resistance at every stage.

### General Mechanisms of Microbiota–Treatment Interaction

The gut microbiota influences CRC treatment outcomes through four broad, interconnected mechanisms: (1) direct pharmacokinetic modulation of drug activity via bacterial enzymes, (2) systemic and local immune modulation that shapes antitumor immunity, (3) regulation of intestinal barrier integrity with consequences for bacterial translocation and inflammation, and (4) metabolite-mediated effects on tumor cell biology and treatment sensitivity. These mechanisms do not operate in isolation — for example, barrier disruption amplifies inflammation driven by bacterial metabolites, and microbial drug metabolism can simultaneously reduce efficacy and increase toxicity.

#### 1. Pharmacokinetic Modulation: Bacterial Enzymes That Activate or Inactivate Drugs

Bacterial enzymes encoded by the gut microbiome can directly biotransform chemotherapeutic agents, altering their bioavailability, efficacy, and toxicity. The most clinically characterized example is **bacterial β-glucuronidase (β-GUS)**, which reactivates the inactive glucuronide metabolite of irinotecan. After hepatic conversion of irinotecan (CPT-11) to its active metabolite SN-38, the liver detoxifies SN-38 via UDP-glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 (UGT1A1), producing the inactive SN-38 glucuronide (SN-38G), which is excreted into the intestinal lumen via bile. Gut bacteria — particularly *Escherichia coli* and *Clostridium perfringens* — express β-GUS enzymes that cleave the glucuronic acid moiety from SN-38G, regenerating active SN-38 in the intestinal lumen. This reactivation causes dose-limiting gastrointestinal toxicity, including severe diarrhea and mucositis, occurring in 50–80% of patients receiving irinotecan ([Yue et al. 2021, *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2021.710945/full)). The structural characterization of microbiome β-GUS enzymes has enabled the development of specific inhibitors (e.g., Inh-1, UNC-10201582) that reduce SN-38 reactivation and intestinal damage in preclinical models without compromising antitumor efficacy ([Wallace et al. 2015, *Cell Chemical Biology*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1074552115003257); [Bhatt et al. 2020, *PNAS*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1918095117)).

A second major pharmacokinetic mechanism involves the **preTA operon**, which encodes reductases that inactivate 5-fluorouracil (5-FU). Spanogiannopoulos et al. (2022) demonstrated that 47 human gut bacterial strains could metabolize 5-FU, and that the preTA operon was necessary and sufficient for 5-FU inactivation in *E. coli*, exhibiting high catalytic efficiency for the reductive catabolism of the drug ([Spanogiannopoulos et al. 2022, *PMC*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9530025/)). This microbial inactivation of 5-FU may reduce local drug availability in the colonic lumen, potentially contributing to chemoresistance. The clinical DPD inhibitor gimeracil, used to preserve 5-FU efficacy by blocking host dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase, also inhibits preTA activity, suggesting that host and bacterial enzymes share convergent metabolic pathways for 5-FU degradation ([Xu et al. 2024, *Pharmacology & Therapeutics*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41134670/)). Together, these findings establish that gut bacteria can directly modulate the pharmacokinetics of both irinotecan and 5-FU, with consequences for both toxicity and efficacy.

#### 2. Immune Modulation: Microbiota Shaping Systemic and Local Immunity

The gut microbiota educates and modulates the host immune system, influencing both systemic immune tone and the tumor microenvironment (TME) in ways that directly affect treatment response. Specific bacterial taxa and their metabolites regulate T-cell infiltration, myeloid cell differentiation, and checkpoint inhibitor efficacy through multiple pathways.

The **Akkermansia muciniphila–inosine–CD73–IFN-γ axis** represents the most mechanistically defined microbiota-immune pathway relevant to checkpoint blockade. Mager et al. (2020) demonstrated that microbiome-derived inosine, produced by *A. muciniphila* and other commensals, is transported into host cells and enhances the effector function of CD8+ T cells and NK cells via the CD73 (ecto-5′-nucleotidase) pathway, leading to increased IFN-γ production and enhanced anti-tumor immunity. In germ-free or antibiotic-treated mice, this pathway was abrogated, and oral supplementation with *A. muciniphila* or inosine restored checkpoint inhibitor efficacy ([Mager et al. 2020, *Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abc3421)). This finding established a direct molecular link between a specific gut microbial metabolite and the systemic immune response required for immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) activity.

Beyond the inosine axis, the gut microbiota shapes the TME through **Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling**. *Fusobacterium nucleatum* engages TLR4 on immune and tumor cells, activating NF-κB signaling that upregulates anti-apoptotic genes (e.g., BIRC3) and promotes a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory milieu ([Jiang et al. 2023, *Cell Host & Microbe*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37130518/); [s12672-025-03574-0, *Springer*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12672-025-03574-0)). The **STING–cGAS pathway** provides another critical bridge between microbiota and antitumor immunity. Gut microbiota-derived DNA can activate the cGAS-STING pathway in dendritic cells and macrophages, inducing type I interferon (IFN-I) responses that prime systemic antiviral and antitumor immunity. Erttmann et al. (2022) showed that microbiota-driven tonic IFN-I signaling was dependent on cGAS-STING but not on TLR signaling, and that this pathway was essential for immune surveillance against tumors ([Erttmann et al. 2022, *Immunity*](https://www.cell.com/immunity/fulltext/S1074-7613(22)00177-7)). In CRC specifically, STING signaling has been shown to play a tumor-suppressive role in colitis-associated cancer by activating innate immune responses ([Ke et al. 2022, *FASEB Journal*](https://faseb.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1096/fj.202101199R)).

The distinction between **intratumoral microbiota** and **luminal (fecal/mucosal) microbiota** is critical for understanding treatment interactions. Intratumoral microbes — including *F. nucleatum*, *B. fragilis*, and *Parvimonas micra* — reside within the tumor stroma and interact directly with tumor and immune cells, whereas luminal microbiota exert effects primarily through metabolite absorption and immune conditioning. Intratumoral microbiota are more closely aligned with the core functional microenvironment of CRC lesions and may promote progression through inflammatory pathways, metabolic alterations, and direct modulation of treatment resistance ([PMC review on intratumoral microbiome](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11464362/)). The spatial separation of these niches means that fecal microbiome profiling may not fully capture the microbial communities most relevant to intratumoral drug metabolism or immune evasion.

#### 3. Barrier Function: Mucosal Integrity, Bacterial Translocation, and Inflammation

The intestinal barrier — composed of the mucus layer, tight junctions between epithelial cells, and the underlying immune lamina propria — is a critical determinant of treatment-related complications. Cancer therapies, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, directly damage this barrier. Chemotherapy increases intestinal epithelial permeability by disrupting tight junction proteins (claudins, occludin, ZO-1) and damaging crypt stem cells, leading to increased bacterial translocation from the gut lumen into the bloodstream and mesenteric lymph nodes ([Dahlgren et al. 2023, *Seminars in Cancer Biology*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0753332223004328); [Kouzu et al. 2022, *PMC*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8962358/)). This translocation can cause systemic infections, sepsis, and amplification of the inflammatory cascade that drives treatment complications such as mucositis, diarrhea, and hepatotoxicity.

Radiotherapy similarly damages the intestinal mucosal barrier, particularly the biological and immune barriers, leading to bacterial translocation and radiation enteritis ([Jingjing et al. 2025, *Frontiers in Immunology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2025.1590219/full)). The resulting dysbiosis — depletion of beneficial butyrate-producing bacteria (*Faecalibacterium*, *Roseburia*) and enrichment of pro-inflammatory pathobionts (*Fusobacterium*, *Proteus*) — further weakens barrier function, creating a vicious cycle of inflammation and tissue injury ([s12967-024-05709-3, *Springer*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12967-024-05709-3)). Conversely, butyrate produced by commensal bacteria strengthens barrier integrity by promoting mucin production, enhancing tight junction assembly, and providing energy for colonocyte metabolism, thus protecting against treatment-induced barrier damage.

#### 4. Metabolite-Mediated Effects: SCFAs, Succinic Acid, and Secondary Bile Acids

Microbial metabolites serve as signaling molecules that directly influence CRC cell biology and treatment sensitivity.

**Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)** — primarily butyrate, propionate, and acetate — are produced by bacterial fermentation of dietary fiber by butyrate-producing genera including *Faecalibacterium* and *Roseburia*. Butyrate is the most potent SCFA in CRC treatment contexts, acting through multiple mechanisms: (i) **histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition** — due to the Warburg effect, CRC cells accumulate butyrate rather than oxidizing it, leading to HDAC inhibition, increased histone acetylation, and re-expression of tumor-suppressor genes including p21, while suppressing anti-apoptotic genes such as XIAP and IDO-1 ([PMC review on butyrate](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6007476/); [tandfonline review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19490976.2025.2483780)); (ii) **GPR109a signaling** — butyrate activates GPR109a on immune cells, inhibiting inflammatory cytokine production and suppressing NF-κB-mediated inflammation ([s12967-024-05709-3, *Springer*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12967-024-05709-3)); (iii) **enhanced apoptosis and ferroptosis sensitivity** — butyrate increases ferroptosis sensitivity in CRC cells by suppressing xCT expression, and promotes apoptosis through modulation of the FOS promoter and chromatin accessibility ([ScienceDirect, *Biomedicine & Pharmacotherapy*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213231723002239); [Frontiers, *Cellular and Infection Microbiology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1023806/full)). These combined effects make butyrate a key mediator of treatment sensitivity, and its depletion in CRC-associated dysbiosis may reduce the efficacy of chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

**Succinic acid** from *Fusobacterium nucleatum* modulates anti-PD-1 response through a distinct mechanism. Jiang et al. (2023) showed that in metastatic CRC patients who failed anti-PD-1 therapy, *F. nucleatum* abundance and serum succinic acid levels were significantly elevated. Mechanistically, F. nucleatum-derived succinic acid suppressed the **cGAS–interferon-β (IFN-β) pathway** in tumor cells, consequently dampening the antitumor immune response by limiting CD8+ T-cell infiltration and function. Fecal microbiota transfer from responders with low *F. nucleatum* abundance conferred sensitivity to anti-PD-1 in mice, while transfer from *F. nucleatum*-high non-responders did not ([Jiang et al. 2023, *Cell Host & Microbe*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37130518/)). This finding identifies succinic acid as a microbiota-derived metabolite that directly suppresses innate immune sensing and promotes immunotherapy resistance. Notably, a subsequent study by Wang et al. (2024) reported that *F. nucleatum* can paradoxically sensitize MSS CRC to anti-PD-1 in certain contexts, indicating that the Fn–succinate–immune axis is context-dependent and influenced by tumor molecular subtype and baseline immune milieu ([Wang et al. 2024, *Cancer Cell*](https://www.cell.com/cancer-cell/fulltext/S1535-6108%2824%2900318-0)).

**Secondary bile acids**, including deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA), are produced by gut bacteria (e.g., *Desulfovibrio* and other sulfate-reducing bacteria) from primary bile acids. These metabolites have dual roles in CRC treatment: at high concentrations, DCA and LCA are carcinogenic, promoting DNA damage and oxidative stress; however, they also modulate treatment responses by influencing cell proliferation, apoptosis, and immune cell function ([s12967-024-05709-3, *Springer*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12967-024-05709-3); [PMC review on secondary bile acids](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9097900/)). Emerging evidence indicates that secondary bile acids can modulate the efficacy of radiation and chemotherapy, though the clinical implications remain under investigation ([Frontiers in Oncology, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2025.1514872/full)).

#### Summary: An Integrated Mechanistic Framework

These four mechanisms — pharmacokinetic modulation, immune regulation, barrier function, and metabolite signaling — are deeply interconnected. Bacterial β-glucuronidase reactivates irinotecan in the gut lumen, simultaneously increasing local toxicity and potentially altering systemic drug exposure. Barrier disruption from chemotherapy or radiotherapy permits bacterial translocation, which amplifies systemic inflammation through TLR and STING–cGAS signaling, further impairing treatment tolerance. Meanwhile, microbial metabolites — butyrate, succinic acid, inosine, and secondary bile acids — act as molecular switches that modulate tumor cell apoptosis, immune cell function, and the TME's responsiveness to therapy. The composition of the gut microbiome thus serves as both a predictive biomarker and a modifiable determinant of treatment outcomes across surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy, as detailed in the subsections that follow.

## Surgery and Gut Microbiota

### Microbiota Influence on Postoperative Outcomes

Preoperative gut microbial composition is increasingly recognized as a modifiable determinant of postoperative outcomes after colorectal cancer (CRC) surgery. The most feared complication — anastomotic leak (AL) — occurs in roughly 3–15% of colorectal resections and up to 20% after ultra-low anterior resection, with incidence figures varying widely (1–24%) depending on diagnostic criteria ([Williamson et al. 2021, PMC8610638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8610638/)). Despite advances in surgical technique and perioperative care, including Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols, AL rates have remained largely unchanged, suggesting that biological factors beyond mechanical construction contribute to anastomotic failure ([Frontiers in Medicine, 2026 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1781458/full)).

#### Microbial Signatures Associated with Anastomotic Leak

Specific bacterial taxa and community-level shifts have been consistently linked to AL risk. **Collagenolytic bacteria** — principally *Enterococcus faecalis* and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* — are the two most common organisms isolated from anastomotic leak sites and are the highest collagenase-producing bacteria in the gastrointestinal tract ([Williamson et al. 2021, PMC8610638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8610638/)). Seminal work by Shogan et al. (2015) demonstrated that *E. faecalis* promotes AL in rats by degrading collagen and activating tissue matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9); crucially, topical antibiotics prevented AL whereas intravenous antibiotics did not, underscoring the importance of local microbial ecology at the anastomotic interface ([Shogan et al. 2015, Science Translational Medicine](http://stm.sciencemag.org/content/7/286/286ra68); [Gut Microbiota for Health summary](https://www.gutmicrobiotaforhealth.com/enterococcus-faecalis-linked-to-development-of-anastomotic-leak/)). *P. aeruginosa* has been shown to adopt an anastomotic-disrupting phenotype under host stress cues such as those induced by neoadjuvant radiation, destroying tight junctions and epithelial monolayers ([Williamson et al. 2021, PMC8610638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8610638/)).

At the community level, patients who develop AL exhibit markedly reduced abundance of *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, a major butyrate-producing genus, compared with those who heal without complication ([Williamson et al. 2021, PMC8610638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8610638/)). Van Praagh et al. (2016) found that the *Lachnospiraceae* family was significantly enriched in AL patients (p = 0.001), and that high BMI — associated with lower microbial diversity — was a risk factor for both dysbiosis and AL ([Williamson et al. 2021, PMC8610638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8610638/)). Within hours of colonic anastomosis, dramatic community shifts occur: Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes populations drop by approximately 90%, while *E. faecalis* blooms up to 500-fold, creating a permissive environment for tissue-disruptive phenotypes ([Williamson et al. 2021, PMC8610638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8610638/)).

#### Protective Role of Butyrate-Producing Bacteria

Butyrate-producing bacteria — particularly *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* and *Roseburia* spp. — are central to anastomotic healing through multiple mechanisms. Butyrate serves as the preferred energy source for colonocytes, sustaining the metabolic demands of epithelial proliferation and mucosal restoration ([Frontiers in Medicine, 2026 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1781458/full)). SCFAs, including butyrate, regulate the postoperative inflammatory microenvironment through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and Toll-like receptors (TLRs), shaping neutrophil and macrophage recruitment to create a pro-repair milieu rather than excessive inflammation ([Frontiers in Medicine, 2026 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1781458/full)). Commensal communities also reinforce mucus production and tight junction architecture — critical for barrier reconstruction at the anastomotic site — and may regulate angiogenesis via VEGF-associated signaling to support oxygen delivery to healing tissue ([Frontiers in Medicine, 2026 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1781458/full)). Depletion of these butyrate producers, as observed in CRC patients and those who develop AL, removes these protective functions precisely when the anastomosis is most vulnerable.

#### Mechanical Bowel Preparation and Perioperative Antibiotics as Iatrogenic Disruptors

Mechanical bowel preparation (MBP) combined with oral antibiotics (typically neomycin + metronidazole) is standard practice to reduce infectious complications, but both components substantially disrupt the gut microbiome. MBP alone, without oral antimicrobials, does not reduce surgical site infection (SSI) or AL risk; the addition of oral antibiotics is necessary for benefit ([Cochrane summary, NCBI Bookshelf](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK536423/)). Historical evidence supports this: Clarke et al. (1977) conducted the first randomized placebo-controlled trial demonstrating that oral antibiotics prevent AL following colon surgery, and Schardey et al. (1997) showed that topical oral antibiotics reduced AL from 10.6% to 2.9% in a prospective, double-blind, multicenter trial ([Williamson et al. 2021, PMC8610638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8610638/)). More recently, Wirth et al. demonstrated that local anastomotic decontamination with topical antibiotics reduced AL rates by nearly 50% ([Williamson et al. 2021, PMC8610638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8610638/)).

However, this "slash and burn" approach carries a cost: broad-spectrum perioperative antibiotics suppress beneficial commensal populations, potentially removing the very organisms that support healing. The observation that most postoperative infections arise from the patient's own intestinal microbiota — often after suppression of beneficial bacteria — highlights the double-edged nature of antibiotic prophylaxis ([Williamson et al. 2021, PMC8610638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8610638/)). Hajjar et al. (2024) reinforced the clinical stakes in a preclinical study showing that AL worsens oncologic survival by enabling local implantation and dissemination of CRC cells, providing a mechanistic rationale for why preventing AL — including through microbiota modulation — is paramount to improving cancer outcomes ([Hajjar et al. 2024, Clinical Cancer Res 30(3):616](https://aacrjournals.org/clincancerres/article/30/3/616/733547/)).

#### Critical Counterpoint: The 2024 Nature Study

A pivotal 2024 study published in *Scientific Reports* directly challenges the assumption that microbiome changes caused by bowel preparation drive AL. The study, titled "Anastomotic leak occurs independently from microbiota shifts associated with surgical bowel preparation" (Sci Rep 14, 21711), demonstrated that AL develops independently of the microbiota shifts associated with surgical bowel preparation ([Nature Scientific Reports, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-72520-x)). This finding is a critical counterpoint to the microbiome-AL hypothesis: it suggests that AL is a multifactorial process in which microbiome perturbations from MBP may not be a direct causal factor. The study does not refute that microbiota composition influences healing — the mechanistic evidence for collagenolytic bacteria and butyrate producers remains robust — but it tempers the interpretation that perioperative microbiome disruption *per se* is sufficient to cause AL. Preoperative microbiota profiling may still serve as a predictive biomarker for AL risk, but the evidence is not definitive and must be weighed against this counterpoint indicating that AL pathogenesis extends beyond microbial composition alone.

#### Postoperative Ileus and Microbiota-Mediated Inflammation

Postoperative ileus (POI) — the transient cessation of coordinated gastrointestinal motility after abdominal surgery — is another complication linked to gut microbiota. Pilot studies have shown that patients who develop POI harbor a distinct gut microbiome signature during the perioperative period, characterized by altered community composition that may amplify the inflammatory cascade ([Pilot study, JOGS 2020](https://www.jogs.org/article/S1091-255X(23)01626-8/abstract)). Surgical manipulation of the bowel initiates a localized stress response, activating resident mast cells and muscularis externa macrophages; the resulting inflammatory mediators, including prostaglandins and nitric oxide, inhibit smooth muscle contractility ([PMC, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10223893/)). Dysbiotic microbiota may exacerbate this inflammation through bacterial translocation across a disrupted barrier, with microbial products (e.g., LPS) further activating TLR4-mediated inflammatory pathways that perpetuate ileus ([Frontiers in Medicine, 2026 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1781458/full)).

#### Surgical Site Infections and *C. difficile*

The gut microbiome is linked not only to anastomotic complications but also to distant surgical site infections, even in the absence of overt intestinal barrier breach — a "Trojan horse" mechanism whereby translocated bacteria seed remote surgical wounds ([PMC, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9946714/)). Perioperative antibiotic use, while reducing SSI risk, simultaneously increases susceptibility to *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI). CRC patients are at elevated CDI risk due to the convergence of dysbiotic gut microbiota, surgical stress, and prolonged antibiotic exposure ([Raeisi et al. 2025, PMC12167592](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12167592/)). Antibiotic-induced loss of colonization resistance — the protective effect of commensal microbiota against pathogen expansion — is the primary mechanism by which CDI develops, as broad-spectrum agents eliminate competing flora while sparing *C. difficile* spores.

#### Preoperative Probiotic and Synbiotic Supplementation: RCT Evidence

A systematic review by Moreira et al. (2024) encompassing 24 randomized controlled trials evaluated the impact of pre-, pro-, and synbiotic supplementation on CRC treatment outcomes, including surgical endpoints. The review found that supplementation improved surgical outcomes — reducing infectious and non-infectious postoperative complications, accelerating return to normal gut function, shortening hospital length of stay, and decreasing antibiotic usage ([Moreira et al. 2024, Frontiers in Oncology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). Specific strains with the strongest evidence include *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG and *Bifidobacterium* spp. (e.g., *B. breve*, *B. longum*), which have been shown to enhance barrier function, competitively exclude pathogens, and modulate immune responses. Synbiotic formulations combining prebiotic fibers (e.g., galactooligosaccharides) with probiotic strains demonstrated potential advantages over probiotic alone, though evidence on optimal strains, dosages, and duration remains scarce and heterogeneous. The authors concluded that while these results are promising, larger RCTs with standardized interventions are needed to confirm benefits and establish clinical guidelines ([Moreira et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)).

In summary, the gut microbiota exerts multifaceted influence on postoperative outcomes after CRC surgery. Collagenolytic pathobionts (*E. faecalis*, *P. aeruginosa*) and depletion of butyrate producers (*F. prausnitzii*, *Roseburia*) are mechanistically linked to AL, while perioperative interventions (MBP, antibiotics) and host factors (BMI, neoadjuvant therapy) interact with microbial composition to shape healing. The 2024 Nature study provides an essential cautionary note that AL is multifactorial and not solely attributable to microbiome shifts from bowel preparation. Preoperative microbiome profiling and probiotic/synbiotic supplementation represent promising strategies, but the evidence base — while growing — requires further validation through large, well-designed RCTs.

### Microbiota-Based Strategies to Improve Surgical Recovery

Several perioperative interventions targeting the gut microbiota have been investigated to improve surgical outcomes in CRC patients. These include probiotic and synbiotic supplementation, prebiotic fiber, selective digestive decontamination (SDD), and emerging preoperative microbiome screening. The evidence base, while promising, is characterized by heterogeneity in strains, doses, and outcome measures, and by a predominance of small randomized trials.

**Probiotic and Synbiotic Supplementation**

The most comprehensive systematic review to date — Moreira et al. (2024), covering 24 RCTs — found that perioperative pre-, pro-, and synbiotic supplementation improved surgical outcomes in CRC patients, including reductions in infectious and non-infectious postoperative complications, faster return to normal gut function, shorter hospital length of stay, and reduced antibiotic usage ([Moreira et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). The review also noted that evidence on the optimal strains, dosage, and duration of intervention remains scarce.

A pivotal meta-analysis by Veziant et al. (2022) pooled 21 RCTs (15 evaluating probiotics, 6 evaluating synbiotics) and demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in overall infectious complications after colorectal surgery (risk ratio 0.59, 95% CI 0.47–0.75) ([Veziant et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/15/3066)). The effect size was more pronounced for pulmonary and urinary infections than for surgical site infections (SSIs) or anastomotic leaks. Subgroup analyses found no significant difference between probiotics and synbiotics in reducing infectious complications, though the authors noted that questions regarding optimal formulation, strain composition, and timing (preoperative vs. perioperative vs. postoperative) remain unresolved ([Veziant et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/15/3066)).

A systematic review by Pitsillides et al. (2021) focusing specifically on CRC surgery included 15 RCTs and found that probiotics reduced bacterial translocation to mesenteric lymph nodes (e.g., 18% vs. 28% in placebo in Liu et al. 2011; 13% vs. 28% in Liu et al. 2013), maintained intestinal mucosal permeability, and improved the ratio of beneficial to pathogenic microorganisms ([Pitsillides et al. 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/5/1451)). However, heterogeneity across trials was high, influenced by factors including the probiotic species used, single-strain versus multi-strain formulations, duration of administration, and location of bowel resection ([Pitsillides et al. 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/5/1451)).

Specific strains tested in RCTs for CRC surgery include *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG, *Lactobacillus plantarum*, *Bifidobacterium breve*, and *Bifidobacterium longum*, often in multi-strain formulations. A four-probiotics regimen significantly decreased postoperative complications including infections and mechanical ventilation in a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled study ([Flesch et al. 2017](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/5b9f/c6b54338e8e9f199b3415940a792a8c298b3.pdf)). Yang et al. (2016) demonstrated anti-infective effects of perioperative probiotic treatment in CRC patients ([Yang et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4885004/)). The 2025 umbrella review by Gao et al. confirmed that probiotics/synbiotics in perioperative CRC patients improve gastrointestinal discomfort and reduce infection rates, with the placebo group exhibiting a postoperative infection incidence of 29.1% versus approximately 60% lower odds in the probiotic group ([Gao et al. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2025.1635409/full); [Zhang et al. 2025](https://www.spandidos-publications.com/10.3892/ol.2025.15261)).

**Prebiotic Fiber and Butyrate Support**

Prebiotic fiber supplementation — including inulin, galactooligosaccharides (GOS), fructooligosaccharides (FOS), and resistant starch — aims to support butyrate-producing bacteria and maintain mucosal barrier integrity. Moreira et al. (2024) included prebiotic trials within their systematic review and found benefits for surgical outcomes, though evidence was less robust than for probiotics ([Moreira et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). Notably, a study using an animal model demonstrated that a short course of dietary prehabilitation with a low-fat, high-fiber diet could reverse the adverse effects of a high-fat Western-type diet on anastomotic healing via microbiota modulation, improving overall diversity and decreasing postoperative collagenolytic *Enterococcus* ([Hyoju et al. 2020, cited in Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2023.1110787/full)). Butyrate, a major product of anaerobic bacteria, was identified as a potential candidate biomarker to assess microbiota readiness for surgery in the context of dietary prehabilitation ([Keskey et al. 2022, cited in Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2023.1110787/full)).

**Selective Digestive Decontamination (SDD)**

SDD employs non-absorbable enteral antimicrobials — typically oral neomycin combined with metronidazole — to eliminate potentially pathogenic flora while preserving anaerobic commensals. The SELECT trial, a randomized clinical trial of SDD in elective colorectal cancer surgery, demonstrated that SDD reduces anastomotic leakage and costs ([Bogner et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9468075/)). Abis et al. (2013) similarly found that SDD reduces infections following colorectal surgery ([Abis et al. 2013](https://www.jogs.org/article/S1091-255X(23)04056-8/fulltext)). However, the impact of SDD on the broader gut microbiota composition is a concern; van Doorn-Schepens et al. (2022) evaluated SDD's effect on gut microbiota in elective CRC surgery patients and found measurable alterations in microbial composition ([van Doorn-Schepens et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9556388/)). Standard perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis with neomycin and metronidazole, often combined with mechanical bowel preparation, remains the most widely adopted microbiome-targeted intervention in colorectal surgery, though its primary mechanism is pathogen reduction rather than microbiome restoration ([Toh et al. 2018](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2706492)).

**Preoperative Microbiome Screening for Anastomotic Leak Risk**

The emerging concept of using preoperative microbiome composition to stratify AL risk is supported by several lines of evidence. A study published in *Annals of Surgery* (2019) demonstrated that the mucosal microbiome of anastomotic tissue during surgery has predictive value for colorectal anastomotic leakage ([PMC10978556](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10978556/)). A 2024 study in *Scientific Reports* (Nature) investigated longitudinal changes in the mucosal microbiome before, during, and after surgery, aiming to determine if microbial signatures could predict anastomotic insufficiency ([Nature 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-65320-w)). A study in *Heliyon* (2024) found that the baseline fecal microbiome differs significantly between patients who develop AL and those who do not ([Heliyon 2024](https://www.cell.com/heliyon/fulltext/S2405-8440(24)16647-9)). A systematic review in *BJS Open* concluded that evidence implicates the gut microbiome as a factor associated with AL in CRC surgery, with three studies suggesting a causal role ([BJS Open 2026](https://academic.oup.com/bjsopen/article/10/2/zrag005/8637248)). However, this must be weighed against the 2024 Nature study (s41598-024-72520-x) showing that AL occurs independently of microbiota shifts associated with surgical bowel preparation, suggesting that AL is multifactorial and that microbiome changes from bowel prep may not directly cause AL (discussed in S2.1). Preoperative microbiome profiling thus shows promise as a predictive biomarker but is not yet definitive.

**Laparoscopic vs. Open Surgery: Differential Impact on Gut Microbiota**

Minimally invasive surgery may differentially affect gut microbiota compared to open surgery. The MIRACLe study demonstrated that implementation of gut microbiota monitoring could prevent anastomotic leaks in laparoscopic colorectal surgery for cancer ([MDPI Cancers 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6694/16/12/2236)). A study by Scholten et al. (2023) found that the hypothesis behind SDD — that the patient's own gut microbiota play a pivotal role in postoperative infections — is particularly relevant in the context of surgical approach, with laparoscopic resection generally associated with lower rates of postoperative mortality and complications compared to open surgery ([Scholten et al. 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1091255X23004699)). However, a meta-analysis by Hasanovic et al. (2024) found that the laparoscopic group did not exhibit superiority over the open group concerning postoperative ileus, anastomotic leak, and lymph node dissection in obstructive CRC ([Hasanovic et al. 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00464-024-10710-4)). The differential GM impact likely relates to reduced surgical stress, less tissue exposure, and shorter operative times in laparoscopic surgery, though direct comparative microbiome studies remain limited. A pilot study found that colorectal surgery with or without bowel resection did not appear to significantly impact the gut microbiome among surgical patients overall, but surgical bowel preparation itself was a major disruptor ([Nature 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-14819-1)).

**Evidence Quality and Limitations**

The evidence base for microbiota-based surgical interventions suffers from several limitations. Most RCTs are small, with significant heterogeneity in probiotic strains (single vs. multi-strain), formulations (probiotic vs. synbiotic vs. prebiotic), dosing, timing (preoperative vs. perioperative vs. postoperative), and outcome definitions ([Moreira et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full); [Veziant et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/15/3066); [Pitsillides et al. 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/5/1451)). The Moreira et al. review explicitly called for more RCTs with larger sample sizes and less heterogeneity to confirm potential benefits and determine optimal strains, dosage, and duration ([Moreira et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). No consensus exists on whether synbiotic supplementation outperforms probiotic alone; Veziant et al. (2022) found no significant difference between the two in subgroup analysis ([Veziant et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/15/3066)).

**Safety Concerns**

A critical safety concern is the risk of probiotic translocation in immunocompromised or critically ill surgical patients. Liong (2008) documented health-damaging effects of probiotic translocation in immunocompromised patients ([Liong 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18366533/)). Kothari et al. (2019) similarly concluded that probiotics might not be harmless to immunocompromised patients, with heightened bacterial load in the small intestine increasing translocation risk ([Kothari et al. 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0753332218345657)). Case reports of probiotic-associated bacteremia and fungemia — particularly with *Lactobacillus* and *Saccharomyces* species — have been documented in critically ill patients ([Vitko 2018](https://jeccm.amegroups.org/article/view/4779/html)). The Norwegian Scientific Committee for Food and Environment found no evidence of beneficial effect from probiotic supplementation in critically ill hospitalized patients ([VKM risk assessment](https://vkm.no/english/riskassessments/allpublications/riskassessmentoftheuseofprobioticsoncriticallyillpatients.4.72c3261615e09f2472f496a9.html)). Additionally, probiotic microorganisms carrying transferable antimicrobial resistance genes may increase the risk of horizontal gene transfer to resident flora ([Yazdankhah et al. 2009](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.3109/08910600903495046)). These concerns underscore the need for careful patient selection, strain-level safety profiling, and vigilant monitoring when using probiotics in the perioperative CRC setting, particularly in patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy or those with compromised mucosal barriers.

## Chemotherapy and Gut Microbiota

### Bacteria-Mediated Chemoresistance

Gut bacteria directly subvert the efficacy of standard colorectal cancer (CRC) chemotherapeutics through multiple molecular mechanisms. The most extensively characterized is *Fusobacterium nucleatum* (Fn), which promotes resistance to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), oxaliplatin, and capecitabine via at least two distinct pathways: upregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein BIRC3 through the TLR4/NF-κB axis, and suppression of chemotherapy-induced pyroptosis through the Hippo/YAP/BCL2/Caspase-3/GSDME axis. Additional species—including *Bacteroides fragilis* and pks⁺ *Escherichia coli*—further contribute to chemoresistance through tumor microenvironment remodeling and immune evasion.

#### BIRC3 Upregulation via the TLR4/NF-κB Pathway

Rubinstein et al. (2019) demonstrated that Fn infection of CRC cell lines (HCT116, HT29) significantly upregulates BIRC3 (baculoviral IAP repeat-containing protein 3), a member of the inhibitor of apoptosis (IAP) family that directly inhibits caspases and thereby blocks chemotherapy-induced apoptosis. Whole-genome microarray analysis identified BIRC3 as the most significantly upregulated gene following Fn infection. Mechanistically, Fn activates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on CRC cells, triggering downstream NF-κB signaling, which in turn drives BIRC3 transcription. Fn-infected CRC cells showed markedly reduced chemosensitivity to 5-FU in vitro, and this effect was reversed by BIRC3 knockdown, confirming its causal role ([Rubinstein et al. 2019, *J Exp Clin Cancer Res*, PMC6327560](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6327560/)).

Critically, this finding was validated in a clinical cohort of 94 advanced CRC patients who received standard 5-FU-based adjuvant chemotherapy after radical surgery at Fudan University Cancer Center (2007–2017). Patients with high Fn abundance in tumor tissues exhibited significantly greater chemoresistance and a 47.8% recurrence rate (45/94 patients recurred during a median follow-up of 38.5 months). High Fn abundance correlated with BIRC3 protein expression levels, establishing the Fn–BIRC3 axis as a clinically relevant chemoresistance mechanism ([Rubinstein et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6327560/)). These results have been corroborated by multiple independent studies across Asian and European cohorts, where Fn enrichment in CRC tissues was consistently associated with shorter recurrence-free survival and poor response to 5-FU-based regimens ([Lee et al. 2019, *Front Oncol*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2019.00566/pdf)).

#### Inhibition of Chemotherapy-Induced Pyroptosis via the Hippo/YAP/BCL2/Caspase-3/GSDME Axis

A second, mechanistically distinct pathway by which Fn promotes chemoresistance was elucidated by Wang et al. (2024). Beyond suppressing apoptosis, Fn was found to inhibit chemotherapy-induced pyroptosis—a pro-inflammatory, lytic form of programmed cell death mediated by gasdermin E (GSDME). When CRC cells are treated with oxaliplatin or 5-FU, GSDME is cleaved by Caspase-3, forming membrane pores that cause cell swelling, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, and inflammatory cell death. Fn co-culture significantly inhibited this process: it reduced cell swelling, attenuated membrane pore formation (confirmed by transmission electron microscopy), decreased LDH release, and suppressed Caspase-3 and GSDME cleavage ([Wang et al. 2024, *Gut Microbes*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19490976.2024.2333790)).

Mechanistically, Fn regulates the Hippo pathway, leading to yes-associated protein (YAP) activation, which promotes BCL2 expression. Elevated BCL2 then suppresses the Caspase-3/GSDME pyroptosis axis. BCL2 siRNA knockdown reversed the inhibitory effect of Fn on pyroptosis, confirming BCL2's central role. In clinical samples, Fn abundance was significantly higher in CRC tissues from recurrent versus non-recurrent patients after chemotherapy. Fn abundance was positively correlated with tumor size and was an independent predictor of shorter recurrence-free survival (RFS), with a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) area under the curve (AUC) of 0.875—superior to AJCC staging (AUC 0.800, *p* = .001) ([Wang et al. 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19490976.2024.2333790)). This study, along with a comprehensive review by Dadgar-Zankbar et al. (2024), confirmed that Fn's chemoresistance mechanisms converge on multiple cell death pathways—autophagy, apoptosis, and pyroptosis—making it a particularly formidable driver of treatment failure ([Dadgar-Zankbar et al. 2024, *Cancer Cell Int*, PMC11566256](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11566256/)).

#### *Fusobacterium nucleatum*-Derived Exosomes as Vectors of Chemoresistance

Fn also propagates chemoresistance through exosome-mediated intercellular transfer. Hui et al. (2024) demonstrated that Fn-infected CRC cells produce exosomes enriched in the circular RNA hsa_circ_0004085. Fn infection promoted hsa_circ_0004085 formation via hnRNP L and packaged it into exosomes via hnRNP A1. These exosomes were transferred to recipient CRC cells, where hsa_circ_0004085 bound to RRBP1 to stabilize GRP78 mRNA and promoted ATF6p50 nuclear translocation, collectively relieving endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and thereby conferring resistance to both oxaliplatin and 5-FU. Plasma levels of hsa_circ_0004085 were significantly elevated in colon cancer patients with intracellular Fn and were associated with poor clinical response to oxaliplatin/5-FU-based chemotherapy, suggesting that Fn-derived exosomes serve as both a resistance mechanism and a liquid-biopsy biomarker ([Hui et al. 2024, *J Nanobiotechnology*, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12951-024-02331-9)).

#### *Bacteroides fragilis* Enrichment in Chemoresistant CRC

*Bacteroides fragilis* has been identified as a key contributor to chemoresistance in CRC. Ding et al. (2025) reported that *B. fragilis* was the top enriched bacterium in stool samples from CRC patients who were resistant to chemotherapy, identified across two independent cohorts. Its abundance was significantly associated with poor survival. Mechanistically, *B. fragilis* colonizes colon tumors and its surface protein SusD/RagB binds to the Notch1 receptor on CRC cells, activating Notch1 signaling and inducing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and cancer stemness, which collectively suppress chemotherapy-induced apoptosis. Either deletion of SusD/RagB or pharmacological blockade of Notch1 signaling abrogated *B. fragilis*-mediated chemoresistance. Notably, a *B. fragilis*-targeting bacteriophage (VA7) selectively suppressed the bacterium and restored chemosensitivity in preclinical CRC xenograft and AOM/DSS mouse models, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of pathobiont-targeted approaches ([Ding et al. 2025, *Cell Host & Microbe*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40446807/)).

#### pks⁺ *Escherichia coli* and the Immunosuppressive Tumor Microenvironment

pks⁺ *E. coli*, which produce the genotoxin colibactin, contribute to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that indirectly impairs chemotherapy efficacy. Colibactin causes DNA double-strand breaks in colonocytes, driving mutagenesis and genomic instability. Beyond its direct carcinogenic effects, pks⁺ *E. coli* alters the tumor microenvironment toward immunosuppressive lipid overload, creating a milieu that facilitates CRC progression and chemoresistance. This immunosuppressive TME also impairs anti-PD-1 immunotherapy response, as the same inflammatory and immune-evasion mechanisms that promote tumor survival under chemotherapy also limit immune checkpoint inhibitor efficacy ([de Oliveira Alves et al. 2024, *Gut Microbes*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19490976.2024.2320291); [Sadeghi et al. 2024, *Microorganisms*, PMC11205849](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11205849/)). The pks island-encoded colibactin synthesis machinery thus positions pks⁺ *E. coli* as both a "driver" bacterium in CRC initiation and a modulator of treatment response.

#### Clinical Correlations Between Baseline Fn Abundance and Chemotherapy Outcomes

Multiple clinical studies have correlated baseline Fn abundance with progression-free survival (PFS) and recurrence in CRC patients receiving chemotherapy. In the Rubinstein cohort (n = 94), high Fn abundance was the strongest predictor of recurrence after 5-FU-based adjuvant chemotherapy, outperforming conventional staging. Yu et al. (2017) similarly found that Fn abundance was significantly higher in recurrent CRC patients across three independent Shanghai cohorts (n = 31, 92, and 173 patients, respectively), with high Fn levels consistently associated with shorter RFS ([Lee et al. 2019, *Front Oncol*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2019.00566/pdf)). In European cohorts, Flanagan et al. confirmed that Fn positivity in CRC tissues was associated with poor prognosis and reduced chemotherapy responsiveness. Collectively, these studies establish Fn abundance as a predictive biomarker for chemotherapy outcomes across diverse populations and treatment regimens—including FOLFOX (5-FU + oxaliplatin), FOLFIRI (5-FU + irinotecan), and CAPOX (capecitabine + oxaliplatin).

#### Summary of Chemoresistance Mechanisms

| Bacteria | Chemotherapy Affected | Molecular Mechanism | Clinical Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| *F. nucleatum* | 5-FU, oxaliplatin | TLR4/NF-κB → BIRC3 upregulation → anti-apoptosis | 94-patient cohort; 47.8% recurrence; AUC 0.875 for recurrence prediction |
| *F. nucleatum* | Oxaliplatin, 5-FU | Hippo/YAP → BCL2 ↑ → Caspase-3/GSDME pyroptosis suppression | AUC 0.875 vs. 0.800 AJCC; shorter RFS |
| *F. nucleatum* (exosomes) | Oxaliplatin, 5-FU | hsa_circ_0004085 → ER stress relief (GRP78/ATF6p50) | Plasma hsa_circ_0004085 correlates with poor response |
| *B. fragilis* | 5-FU, oxaliplatin | SusD/RagB → Notch1 → EMT/stemness → anti-apoptosis | Two independent cohorts; poor survival; phage VA7 restores sensitivity |
| pks⁺ *E. coli* | Indirect (TME modulation) | Colibactin → DNA damage; lipid overload → immunosuppressive TME | Preclinical; impairs anti-PD-1 and chemoresistance |

These findings collectively demonstrate that gut bacteria—particularly *F. nucleatum*, *B. fragilis*, and pks⁺ *E. coli*—employ diverse but convergent mechanisms to undermine chemotherapy efficacy in CRC, targeting multiple cell death pathways and reshaping the tumor microenvironment. The clinical validation of Fn as a predictive biomarker across multiple cohorts underscores the translational potential of microbiome-informed treatment strategies.

### Microbiota Modulation of Chemotherapy Toxicity

Gut microbiota profoundly shape the toxicity profile of colorectal cancer (CRC) chemotherapy through direct enzymatic drug metabolism, modulation of mucosal barrier integrity, and feedback loops in which treatment-induced dysbiosis itself amplifies tissue damage. Gastrointestinal adverse events (GIAEs) — encompassing diarrhea, mucositis, nausea, and abdominal pain — occur in 40–100% of CRC patients receiving chemotherapy, representing a leading cause of dose reduction, treatment interruption, and diminished quality of life ([Yue et al. 2021, *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2021.710945/full)).

#### Irinotecan: Bacterial β-Glucuronidase as a Mediator of Dose-Limiting Toxicity

Irinotecan (CPT-11) toxicity is the paradigmatic example of microbiota-driven chemotherapy adverse effects. After hepatic conversion to its active metabolite SN-38 by carboxyl esterases (CES1/CES2), SN-38 is glucuronidated to the inactive SN-38G by UGT1A1 in hepatocytes and excreted via bile into the intestinal lumen. Gut bacterial β-glucuronidase (βG) enzymes — produced prominently by *Escherichia coli*, *Clostridium perfringens*, and other commensal species — deconjugate SN-38G back to the cytotoxic SN-38 within the intestinal lumen, where it directly damages epithelial cells, disrupts tight junctions, and triggers inflammation, producing the characteristic delayed-onset severe diarrhea and mucositis ([Yue et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2021.710945/full); [Mahdy et al. 2023, *BMC Microbiology*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12866-023-02791-3)). Clinically, irinotecan causes diarrhea in 50–80% of patients, with grade 3–4 severity in 22–44% ([Yue et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2021.710945/full)). Beyond diarrhea, irinotecan-induced mucositis involves reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, protein and DNA oxidation, and activation of inflammatory pathways, while translocation of opportunistic pathobionts (*Enterococcus* spp., *Streptococcus* spp., *Enterobacteriaceae*) across the compromised barrier can precipitate sepsis ([Mahdy et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12866-023-02791-3)).

**Therapeutic targeting of bacterial β-glucuronidase** has emerged as a strategy to uncouple irinotecan's antitumor efficacy from its intestinal toxicity. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that selective bacterial GUS inhibitors — including compounds such as Inh-1 and UNC-10201582 — partially alleviate irinotecan-induced gastrointestinal tract damage and diarrhea in murine models without compromising antitumor activity ([Bhatt et al. 2020, *PNAS*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7132129/); [Lin et al. 2021, *Nature Communications*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-021-01815-w)). Lin et al. (2021) showed that pharmacological inhibition of bacterial β-glucuronidase prevented irinotecan-induced diarrhea while preserving SN-38 plasma levels and antitumor efficacy, confirming that the intestinal reactivation of SN-38 is the primary driver of dose-limiting toxicity rather than a contributor to systemic drug exposure ([Lin et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-021-01815-w)). These inhibitors remain in preclinical development and have not yet entered clinical trials in oncology.

#### 5-Fluorouracil: Dysbiosis-Driven Mucositis and Barrier Disruption

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU), a backbone of FOLFOX and FOLFIRI regimens, causes intestinal mucositis in up to 80% of treated patients ([ScienceDirect, *Biomedicine & Pharmacotherapy*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0006295226001243)). The pathogenesis is bidirectional: 5-FU directly damages intestinal crypt stem cells and disrupts tight junctions, but it also induces gut dysbiosis — characterized by depletion of beneficial taxa and expansion of pro-inflammatory pathobionts — which in turn exacerbates mucosal inflammation and barrier permeability ([PMC6660349](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6660349/)). Preclinical evidence confirms that 5-FU-induced dysbiosis and the ensuing inflammation lead to exacerbated intestinal mucositis, creating a vicious cycle in which microbial imbalance amplifies tissue injury ([PMC6660349](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6660349/)). Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid produced by commensal bacteria, has been shown to mitigate 5-FU's mucositic effects, suggesting that preservation of butyrate-producing communities may be protective ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2468202023000384)).

#### Capecitabine: GI Toxicity and Microbial Perturbation

Capecitabine, the oral prodrug of 5-FU used in CAPOX regimens, produces gastrointestinal toxicity including diarrhea, mucositis, and colitis ([Wiley, *Molecular Nutrition & Food Research*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mnfr.70023)). Grade 3 diarrhea occurs in approximately 24% of patients receiving capecitabine-based therapy ([Ovid/ScienceDirect, *Clinical Colorectal Cancer*](https://www.ovid.com/journals/cclc/pdf/10.1016/j.clcc.2021.10.004~the-role-of-intestinal-microbiota-in-metastatic-colorectal)). Capecitabine administration alters gut microbial composition, increasing the abundance of certain taxa while depleting others, though the specific shifts appear less pronounced than those seen with irinotecan ([ScienceDirect, *Clinical Colorectal Cancer*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S153300282100102X)). Notably, a 2023 study demonstrated that capecitabine-loaded prebiotic nanoparticles (SCXN) could simultaneously deliver chemotherapy and modulate the gut microbiota toward probiotic proliferation and enhanced SCFA production, achieving a tumor inhibition rate of 71.78% versus 5.29% with free capecitabine in a CRC mouse model — illustrating the potential of microbiota-targeted drug delivery to reduce toxicity while improving efficacy ([Nature Communications 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40439-y)).

#### Oxaliplatin: Microbiota-Dependent Peripheral Neurotoxicity

Oxaliplatin, a platinum-based agent used in FOLFOX, induces peripheral neuropathy (OIPN) in more than 30% of patients, and accumulating evidence implicates the gut microbiota in its pathogenesis ([PMC5575957](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575957/)). Germ-free mice and antibiotic-treated mice show significantly attenuated oxaliplatin-induced mechanical hyperalgesia and systemic inflammation compared to conventionally colonized mice, demonstrating that gut microbiota are critical for the development of OIPN ([PMC5575957](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575957/); [read.qxmd.com](https://read.qxmd.com/read/42025932/gut-microbiota-a-critical-regulator-of-oxaliplatin-induced-peripheral-neurotoxicity-development)). Mechanistically, oxaliplatin disrupts intestinal barrier integrity, increases lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels, and promotes systemic inflammation that drives neuroinflammation and neuropathic pain ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0161813X26000811)). This gut–neuropathy axis suggests that microbiota-targeted interventions could mitigate OIPN, though clinical translation remains preliminary.

#### Protective Role of Butyrate-Producing Bacteria

Butyrate-producing bacteria, predominantly belonging to *Clostridium* cluster XIVa (e.g., *Faecalibacterium*, *Roseburia*) and cluster IV within the phylum Firmicutes, play a protective role against chemotherapy-induced intestinal injury ([Singh et al. 2023, *Frontiers in Microbiology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2022.1103836/full)). These mucosal butyrate producers generate butyrate in close proximity to the intestinal epithelium, enhancing butyrate bioavailability and supporting epithelial cell health, tight junction integrity, and anti-inflammatory signaling ([Van den Abbeele et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23235287/)). Chemotherapy-induced depletion of these taxa removes a critical protective mechanism: butyrate directly mitigates 5-FU-induced mucositis ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2468202023000384)), and recent preclinical data demonstrate that butyrate protects against chemotherapy-induced gut injury through both microbiota-dependent and microbiota-independent mechanisms, including HDAC inhibition and GPCR-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling ([Cheatham et al. 2025, *PMC12779942*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12779942/)). Preservation or restoration of butyrate-producing communities — whether through dietary fiber, prebiotics, or probiotic supplementation — represents a rational strategy to reduce chemotherapy-associated intestinal toxicity.

#### Probiotics for Chemotherapy-Induced Diarrhea (CID): Clinical Evidence

Probiotic supplementation has been evaluated across multiple randomized controlled trials for the prevention and management of CID in CRC patients:

- ***Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG (LGG)**: The landmark RCT by Österlund et al. (2007) in 152 CRC patients receiving 5-FU-based chemotherapy demonstrated that LGG supplementation was well tolerated and reduced the frequency of severe diarrhea (grade 3–4) and abdominal discomfort compared to placebo ([Österlund et al. 2007, *British Journal of Cancer*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2360429/)). A subsequent multicenter phase III randomized, double-blind trial (Mego et al. 2023, *Frontiers in Oncology*) further assessed LGG efficacy in preventing grade 3/4 diarrhea in CRC patients, confirming the probiotic's role in reducing CID incidence ([Mego et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2023.1168654/full)).

- **Multi-strain formulations**: A mixture of *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum*, *Lactobacillus acidophilus*, and *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* significantly relieved irinotecan-induced diarrhea in a murine model by reducing β-glucuronidase expression, lowering ROS levels, and protecting gut epithelium against dysbiosis and crypt injury ([Mahdy et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12866-023-02791-3)). Multi-strain probiotics also reduced mild-to-moderate CID episodes in an ESMO 2018-reported study ([eCancer 2018](https://ecancer.org/en/news/14985-esmo-2018-multi-strain-probiotic-reduces-chemotherapy-induced-diarrhoea)).

- ***Saccharomyces boulardii***: This yeast probiotic has demonstrated anti-diarrheal and anti-inflammatory properties in CID, with meta-analytic evidence supporting its use in reducing chemotherapy-related diarrhea incidence ([Lu et al. 2019, *Asia Pacific Journal of Clinical Nutrition*](https://apjcn.qdu.edu.cn/2019-0162-final.pdf)).

- **Meta-analytic evidence**: A 2025 meta-analysis of 18 RCTs confirmed that probiotics significantly reduced the incidence of diarrhea following chemotherapy across cancer types, with the effect most pronounced for all-grade diarrhea ([Yang et al. 2025, *PMC12237357*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12237357/)). A separate meta-analysis in CRC patients specifically reported a significant reduction in chemoradiotherapy-induced diarrhea (RR significantly below 1.0) ([Yang et al. 2025, *Springer*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12876-025-03914-y)).

Despite this evidence, probiotic use in immunocompromised or critically ill CRC surgical and chemotherapy patients carries a theoretical risk of translocation and bacteremia/fungemia, particularly with *Lactobacillus* and *Saccharomyces* strains, necessitating careful strain selection and patient screening ([Mahdy et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12866-023-02791-3)).

#### The Feedback Loop: Chemotherapy Reshapes the Microbiome, Amplifying Toxicity

A critical and often underappreciated dimension of microbiota–chemotherapy interaction is the feedback loop in which chemotherapy itself disrupts gut microbial composition, and the resulting dysbiosis in turn amplifies treatment toxicity. Irinotecan treatment alters intestinal microbiota: 16S rRNA sequencing of stool samples from irinotecan-treated patients revealed increased abundance of *Veillonella*, *Clostridium*, *Butyricicoccus*, and *Prevotella* compared to healthy controls, with a general shift away from the *Firmicutes*-dominant profile seen in health toward *Bacteriodetes* enrichment ([Mahdy et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12866-023-02791-3)). 5-FU similarly induces gut dysbiosis characterized by diversity loss and expansion of pro-inflammatory taxa, which then drives further mucositis and barrier disruption ([PMC6660349](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6660349/)). This chemotherapy-driven dysbiosis reduces microbial diversity and functional redundancy, creating an environment in which *Enterobacteriaceae* and other pathobionts bloom, and in which *Clostridioides difficile* infection risk is elevated — a particularly dangerous complication in already immunocompromised CRC patients ([Kulecka et al. 2023, *PMC10413277*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10413277/)). Diarrheal-associated gut dysbiosis in cancer patients is further exacerbated by *C. difficile* infection, compounding morbidity ([Kulecka et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10413277/)). This self-reinforcing cycle — chemotherapy causes dysbiosis, dysbiosis amplifies toxicity, and toxicity necessitates dose reductions or treatment delays — underscores the rationale for microbiota-protective strategies (probiotics, prebiotics, enzyme inhibitors, dietary fiber) as integral components of CRC chemotherapy management rather than merely adjunctive measures.

## Immunotherapy and Gut Microbiota

### Microbiota as a Determinant of Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Response in CRC

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) — primarily anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies such as [pembrolizumab](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37130518/) and nivolumab — have transformed the treatment of mismatch repair-deficient / microsatellite-instability-high (dMMR/MSI-H) colorectal cancer, as demonstrated by the [KEYNOTE-177 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37130518/) establishing pembrolizumab as first-line therapy for MSI-H CRC. However, the majority of CRC patients (~85%) have microsatellite-stable (MSS/pMMR) tumors that are largely resistant to ICI monotherapy. The gut microbiota has emerged as a critical modifiable determinant of ICI response across cancer types, and mounting evidence — though predominantly preclinical — indicates that specific bacterial taxa and their metabolites can either potentiate or undermine anti-PD-1 efficacy in CRC. The key mechanisms converge on the capacity of microbial metabolites to shape the tumor immune microenvironment, particularly CD8+ T-cell infiltration and function, regulatory T-cell (Treg) activity, and interferon signaling.

#### The F. nucleatum Paradox: Resistance Versus Sensitization

The most striking and seemingly contradictory finding in this field involves *Fusobacterium nucleatum* (Fn), a pathobiont consistently enriched in CRC. Two landmark studies published within a year of each other arrived at opposite conclusions about Fn's role in anti-PD-1 response.

**Resistance mechanism (Jiang et al. 2023, *Cell Host & Microbe*).** In a cohort of metastatic CRC patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy, Jiang and colleagues found that non-responders had significantly greater Fn abundance and elevated fecal succinic acid levels compared with responders. Fecal microbiota transfer (FMT) from responders with low Fn abundance — but not from Fn-high non-responders — conferred anti-PD-1 sensitivity in mouse models. Mechanistically, Fn-derived succinic acid suppressed the **cGAS–interferon-β (IFN-β) pathway**, consequently dampening the antitumor response by limiting CD8+ T-cell activity. Succinic acid signals through its receptor **SUCNR1** (GPR91), and this SUCNR1-mediated signaling in tumor cells or immune cells was shown to impair the cGAS-STING-IFN axis that is critical for antitumor immunity and ICI efficacy ([Jiang et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37130518/)).

**Sensitization mechanism (Wang et al. 2024, *Cancer Cell*).** In apparent contradiction, Wang and colleagues demonstrated that Fn paradoxically **sensitizes MSS CRC to anti-PD-1** therapy. Using humanized mouse models (genO-hPD-1/hCTLA-4 mice engrafted with MSS CRC tumors), they showed that FMT from Fn-high MSS CRC patients into germ-free mice resulted in significantly enhanced anti-PD-1 responses compared with FMT from Fn-low patients. Furthermore, Fn abundance was negatively associated with PD-1 expression in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes from MSS CRC patients, suggesting that Fn may lower the threshold for PD-1 blockade efficacy. The mechanism involves Fn-mediated modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment, including effects on Treg populations and CTLA-4 expression patterns, though the precise metabolic mediators remain under investigation ([Wang et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39303724/)).

**Resolving the paradox.** The discrepancy between these studies likely reflects differences in experimental context: (1) **tumor stage and setting** — Jiang studied metastatic CRC patients (treatment-refractory), whereas Wang used localized MSS CRC models; (2) **Fn abundance threshold** — the balance between immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive effects of Fn may be dose-dependent, with moderate Fn levels enhancing T-cell infiltration while high Fn abundance drives succinic acid–mediated immunosuppression; (3) **MSS vs. MSI-H context** — the immune landscape differs fundamentally between these subtypes, and Fn's interaction with anti-PD-1 may differ accordingly; (4) **mechanistic focus** — Jiang emphasized the metabolic axis (succinic acid → SUCNR1 → cGAS-IFN-β suppression), whereas Wang emphasized immune-cell modulation (Treg/CTLA-4 dynamics). Both findings underscore that Fn's role in ICI response is context-dependent and cannot be simplistically categorized as purely detrimental or beneficial.

#### Beneficial Taxa That Enhance ICI Response

**Akkermansia muciniphila — the inosine–CD73–IFN-γ axis.** A landmark study by [Mager et al. (2020, *Science*)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abc3421) demonstrated that microbiome-derived inosine, produced by *A. muciniphila* and *Bifidobacterium pseudolongum*, enhances checkpoint blockade immunotherapy. Inosine is translocated systemically, where it is metabolized by the ectonucleotidase **CD73** (NT5E) into adenosine, which signals through the A2A receptor on T cells to promote IFN-γ production and enhance CD8+ T-cell effector function. This inosine–CD73–IFN-γ axis was shown to be critical for anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 efficacy in murine models. While the original study was conducted in melanoma and bladder cancer models, *A. muciniphila* has been detected in CRC patient cohorts and its abundance correlates with improved ICI outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer, suggesting relevance to CRC as well ([Mager et al. 2020](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abc3421); [Routy et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10626011/)).

**Roseburia intestinalis — butyrate-mediated CD8+ T-cell activation.** Kang and colleagues (2023, *Gut*) provided the first CRC-specific evidence that a commensal gut bacterium can directly enhance anti-PD-1 efficacy. They showed that *R. intestinalis* protects against colorectal tumorigenesis by producing butyrate. Critically, *R. intestinalis*–derived butyrate improved anti-PD-1 efficacy in CRC mouse models by **activating cytotoxic CD8+ T cells** — enhancing their proliferation, IFN-γ production, and tumor-killing capacity. This effect was mediated through butyrate's action as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor and its signaling through G-protein-coupled receptors, leading to epigenetic reprogramming that favored effector T-cell differentiation. Depletion of CD8+ T cells abolished the butyrate-mediated enhancement of anti-PD-1, confirming the mechanism's dependence on cytotoxic T-cell activation ([Kang et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37491158/)).

**Bifidobacterium and Faecalibacterium.** Multiple studies across cancer types have identified *Bifidobacterium longum*, *B. adolescentis*, and *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* as taxa enriched in ICI responders. *B. longum* and *B. adolescentis* enhance ICI response through inosine production and systemic immune priming, while *F. prausnitzii* — a major butyrate producer with potent anti-inflammatory properties — has been linked to favorable ICI outcomes and improved progression-free survival in melanoma and NSCLC cohorts. In CRC, *F. prausnitzii* is consistently depleted in tumors compared with healthy controls, and its anti-inflammatory properties (including induction of regulatory T cells that suppress excessive inflammation while maintaining antitumor immunity) may create a more favorable baseline immune environment for checkpoint blockade ([Sadee et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1186/s40169-019-0225-x); [Hwang et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10626011/)).

#### Microbiome Differences Between MSI-H and MSS CRC

The gut microbiome differs substantially between MSI-H and MSS CRC, providing a potential microbial explanation for the differential ICI sensitivity of these subtypes. [Sillo et al. (2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10054450/) reviewed the available evidence and concluded that dMMR/MSI-H and pMMR/MSS CRC harbor "radically different" gut microbiota in terms of both composition and diversity. MSI-H tumors, which arise predominantly in the proximal colon, are associated with distinct microbial community structures compared with MSS tumors, which are more common in the distal colon and rectum. These differences may arise from the gradual alteration in microbiome composition along the length of the large bowel, as well as from the distinct immune environments of MSI-H (inflamed, lymphocyte-rich) versus MSS (immune-excluded) tumors. The high mutational burden of MSI-H tumors generates abundant neoantigens that, in the context of a favorable microbiome, may synergize with checkpoint blockade. In contrast, MSS tumors exhibit lower mutational burden and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that may require microbiome-mediated immune priming — such as that provided by *R. intestinalis* butyrate or *A. muciniphila* inosine — to overcome ICI resistance ([Sillo et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10054450/)).

#### Detrimental Impact of Antibiotics on ICI Efficacy

Convergent evidence from multiple cancer types demonstrates that antibiotic use — particularly broad-spectrum antibiotics — around the time of ICI initiation significantly reduces treatment efficacy. [Hwang et al. (2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10626011/) showed that prior use of broad-spectrum antibiotics causes gut dysbiosis, depletes immunostimulatory commensal bacteria (including *Bifidobacterium* and butyrate producers), and subsequently reduces ICI efficacy. The mechanism involves disruption of the microbial communities that prime systemic antitumor immunity: antibiotic depletion of commensal bacteria reduces tumor-derived chemokine expression, impairs T-cell tracking into tumors, and worsens outcomes in CRC mouse models. A systematic review by [Wekking et al. (2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305737226000034) (post-cutoff) confirmed that cumulative antibiotic use significantly decreases checkpoint inhibitor efficacy across advanced cancers. In the CRC context, these findings suggest that antibiotic stewardship — avoiding unnecessary antibiotics around ICI initiation — may be a clinically actionable strategy to preserve microbiome-dependent ICI responsiveness.

#### Summary of Microbiota–ICI Interactions in CRC

| Bacterial Taxa / Factor | Effect on ICI | Mechanism | Evidence Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| *F. nucleatum* (high abundance) | Resistance (metastatic CRC) | Succinic acid → SUCNR1 → cGAS-IFN-β suppression → CD8+ T-cell limitation | Clinical (Jiang 2023) |
| *F. nucleatum* (moderate, MSS context) | Sensitization (localized MSS CRC) | FMT-enhanced anti-PD-1; negative association with PD-1 expression; Treg modulation | Preclinical + clinical correlation (Wang 2024) |
| *A. muciniphila* | Enhancement | Inosine → CD73 → IFN-γ → CD8+ T-cell activation | Preclinical (Mager 2020); clinical correlation in NSCLC |
| *R. intestinalis* | Enhancement | Butyrate → HDAC inhibition → cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell activation | Preclinical CRC-specific (Kang 2023) |
| *B. longum*, *B. adolescentis* | Enhancement | Inosine production; systemic immune priming | Preclinical + clinical correlation |
| *F. prausnitzii* | Enhancement | Anti-inflammatory; butyrate production; Treg modulation | Clinical correlation across cancers |
| Broad-spectrum antibiotics | Resistance | Depletion of immunostimulatory commensals; dysbiosis | Clinical (multiple cohorts) |

These findings collectively establish the gut microbiota as a significant, modifiable determinant of ICI response in CRC. The central challenge for clinical translation is the predominance of preclinical evidence and the inherent complexity of microbiome–immune–tumor interactions, which are influenced by tumor subtype (MSI-H vs. MSS), disease stage, and the specific microbial taxa and metabolites present. The Fn paradox exemplifies the difficulty of making universal recommendations about microbiome modulation in CRC immunotherapy, as the same organism can have opposing effects depending on context.

### Strategies to Modulate Microbiota for Enhanced Immunotherapy

Several therapeutic strategies are being developed to reshape the gut microbiome and improve responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in colorectal cancer and other malignancies. These approaches span fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), probiotic supplementation, dietary interventions, and defined bacterial consortia, each with distinct evidence profiles and safety considerations in the ICI context.

**Fecal Microbiota Transplantation from ICI Responders**

The landmark proof-of-concept study by [Baruch et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33303685/) (Science) demonstrated that FMT from patients who had achieved a complete response to anti-PD-1 therapy, combined with reinduction of pembrolizumab, could overcome resistance in refractory metastatic melanoma. In this single-arm trial, 3 of 10 anti-PD-1–refractory patients demonstrated clinical responses, including one complete response and two partial responses. Responders exhibited increased intratumoral immune activity, enrichment of T-cell–associated bacterial taxa, and favorable shifts in systemic immune biomarkers (e.g., decreased levels of molecules associated with immunotherapy resistance). The treatment was well tolerated, with only minor pembrolizumab-related side effects such as fatigue ([NCI Press Release, 2021](https://www.cancer.gov/news-events/press-releases/2021/fecal-transplants-cancer-immunotherapy)). While this study was conducted in melanoma rather than CRC, it established the principle that microbiome modulation can convert ICI non-responders to responders—a finding directly relevant to CRC, where the majority of patients (MSS subtype) are resistant to checkpoint blockade.

The **TACITO trial** (presented at ESMO 2024) is the first randomized, placebo-controlled trial of FMT from immunotherapy responders versus placebo in cancer patients. This phase 2 study enrolled 50 treatment-naïve metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) patients randomized 1:1 to receive FMT or placebo at three time points (baseline, 12 weeks, 24 weeks) alongside pembrolizumab + axitinib. The primary endpoint was met: 1-year progression-free survival was 66.7% in the FMT group versus 35% in the placebo group. Median PFS was 14.2 months (FMT) versus 9.2 months (placebo), and the overall response rate was 52% versus 28%, respectively. FMT was well tolerated with no severe adverse events reported ([Ciccarese et al., ESMO 2024](https://www.urotoday.com/conference-highlights/esmo-2024/esmo-2024-kidney-cancer/154853-esmo-2024-fecal-microbiota-transplantation-fmt-versus-placebo-in-patients-receiving-pembrolizumab-plus-axitinib-for-metastatic-renal-cell-carcinoma-preliminary-results-of-the-randomized-phase-2-tacito-trial.html)). Although TACITO was conducted in RCC rather than CRC, its randomized design and positive results provide the strongest clinical evidence to date that FMT from responders can enhance ICI efficacy, with direct implications for designing analogous CRC trials—particularly for MSI-H CRC where pembrolizumab is first-line therapy.

The **FMT-LUMINate trial** (phase 2, multicenter, open-label; post-cutoff) assessed healthy-donor FMT plus anti-PD-1 in NSCLC (n = 20) and melanoma. In the melanoma cohort, the objective response rate was 75% (15/20), and FMT was deemed safe with no grade ≥3 adverse events related to the transplant itself ([Duttagupta et al., Nature Medicine, 2026 (post-cutoff)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-04186-5)). A comprehensive discussion of FMT as a therapeutic modality—including safety, regulatory status, delivery methods, and defined consortia—is provided in S6.2.

**Probiotic Supplementation for ICI Enhancement**

*Bifidobacterium* species have the strongest preclinical evidence for ICI enhancement. In mouse melanoma models, *Bifidobacterium breve* and *B. longum* were shown to enhance anti-PD-L1 efficacy, with the original 2015 study demonstrating that oral administration of *Bifidobacterium* spp. could restore antitumor immunity in antibiotic-treated or germ-free mice ([Gazzaniga et al., JCI, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11785914/)). In clinical cohorts, *Bifidobacterium longum* has been enriched in ICI responders across multiple cancer types, including melanoma and NSCLC. A systematic review of probiotics and ICI outcomes found that probiotic use was associated with a reduced risk of poor progression-free survival in cancer patients treated with ICIs (HR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.54–0.81) ([Frontiers in Immunology, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2025.1538969/full)). However, CRC-specific clinical evidence for *Bifidobacterium* as an ICI adjunct remains limited, and most supporting data derive from melanoma and NSCLC cohorts.

*Clostridium butyricum*, a spore-forming butyrate-producing probiotic, has shown promising ICI-enhancing effects. [Tomita et al. 2020](https://aacrjournals.org/cancerimmunolres/article/8/10/1236/466810/Association-of-Probiotic-Clostridium-butyricum) (Cancer Immunology Research) reported that probiotic *C. butyricum* therapy was associated with improved therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade in cancer patients. More recently, Xie et al. 2025 (Cancer Cell) demonstrated that *C. butyricum* improves anti-PD-1 efficacy in CRC models (Apc^min/+^ mice and AOM/DSS-induced CRC) by inhibiting IL-6–mediated immunosuppression, providing a mechanistic rationale for its use specifically in CRC immunotherapy ([Xie et al., Cancer Cell, 2025 (post-cutoff)](https://www.cell.com/cancer-cell/fulltext/S1535-6108(25)00318-6)). The spore-forming nature of *C. butyricum* offers practical advantages for oral delivery and gastric acid resistance.

**Dietary Interventions**

Dietary strategies to modulate the microbiome for ICI benefit are emerging. High dietary fiber intake has been associated with improved ICI outcomes in melanoma patients, with fiber consumption positively correlated with microbial taxa linked to antitumor immunity ([Zhou et al., 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666379122003652)). Intermittent fasting, high fiber, and low sugar consumption have been significantly associated with better ICI outcomes ([Somodi et al., 2025 (post-cutoff)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12239337/)). These dietary approaches are thought to promote beneficial microbial metabolites (particularly SCFAs) and reduce systemic inflammation, but prospective clinical trials specifically in CRC patients receiving ICIs are lacking.

**Defined Bacterial Consortia**

Defined bacterial consortia represent a next-generation alternative to whole-stool FMT, offering greater reproducibility and safety control. Preclinically, an 11-strain consortium containing *Parabacteroides*, *Alistipes*, *Paraprevotella*, *Bacteroides*, *Eubacterium*, *Clostridiales*, *Phascolarctobacterium*, and *Fusobacterium* has been shown to promote antitumor immunity to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in mouse models ([Gazzaniga et al., JCI, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11785914/); [Kang et al., Cell Reports Medicine, 2024](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-medicine/fulltext/S2666-3791(24)00124-1)). The defined consortium approach allows precise control over strain composition, eliminates the risk of transmitting uncharacterized pathogens, and facilitates regulatory approval under the FDA's Live Biotherapeutic Products (LBP) pathway. However, no defined consortium has yet entered clinical trials specifically for CRC immunotherapy enhancement.

**FMT as Treatment for Refractory ICI Colitis**

Beyond enhancing ICI efficacy against tumors, FMT has demonstrated clinical utility in treating ICI-associated immune-mediated colitis (IMC), a common and sometimes severe immune-related adverse event (irAE). [Wang et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30420754/) (NEJM) reported the first case series of ICI-associated colitis successfully treated with FMT, demonstrating clinical and endoscopic remission. Subsequently, [Halsey et al. 2023](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scitranslmed.abq4006) (Science Translational Medicine) validated FMT as an effective treatment strategy for IMC, with 10 of 12 patients achieving symptom improvement; three required repeat FMT, and two of these had no further recurrence. These findings are clinically relevant because they position FMT as both an efficacy-enhancing strategy (when combined with ICI) and a toxicity-management strategy (for ICI colitis), though the optimal timing, donor selection, and delivery route for each indication differ.

**Safety Concerns and irAE Risk**

The safety profile of microbiome modulation in ICI-treated patients requires careful consideration. FMT carries a risk of pathogen transmission, highlighted by a [FDA safety alert (2019)](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medical-product-safety-information/fecal-microbiota-transplantation-safety-communication-risk-serious-adverse-reactions-due) documenting transmission of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)–producing *E. coli* from donor to two immunocompromised recipients, one of whom died. The FDA mandated enhanced donor screening for multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs), including ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), and MRSA. These risks are particularly relevant for cancer patients, who may be immunocompromised from prior chemotherapy or the tumor itself.

A key trade-off exists between microbiome-enhanced ICI efficacy and increased irAE risk. By augmenting systemic immune activation, microbiome interventions may potentiate immune-related adverse events, including colitis, hepatitis, and dermatitis. This is especially pertinent for ICI colitis, where the same microbiome shifts that enhance antitumor immunity could theoretically exacerbate autoimmune-like toxicities. Probiotic use in immunocompromised or critically ill patients carries a risk of bacterial translocation and fungemia, particularly with *Lactobacillus* and *Saccharomyces* strains. Rigorous donor screening, strain-level characterization, and careful patient selection are essential to mitigate these risks.

**Regulatory Status**

Currently, no FMT product is FDA-approved for cancer immunotherapy enhancement. The only FDA-approved FMT products—[Rebyota](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/fecal-microbiota-transplantation-fmt) (fecal microbiota live-jslm) and Vowst (fecal microbiota spores)—are indicated solely for recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection. Investigational FMT use in oncology requires an Investigational New Drug (IND) application. Defined consortia such as SER-109 (approved for recurrent CDI) and VE303 are being explored as standardized alternatives but have not yet been evaluated as ICI adjuncts. Comprehensive regulatory and safety considerations for FMT as a modality are discussed in S6.2.

## Radiotherapy and Gut Microbiota

### Radiation-Induced Dysbiosis and Radiation Enteritis

Pelvic and abdominal radiotherapy is a cornerstone of treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer, typically delivered as neoadjuvant chemoradiation with 5-FU or capecitabine. However, the intestinal epithelium is among the most radiosensitive tissues in the body, and radiation inevitably damages the gut microbiota and mucosal architecture, producing radiation enteritis (RE) — a major dose-limiting toxicity that affects both acute treatment tolerance and long-term quality of life.

**Pathogenesis: From Radiation Injury to Dysbiosis**

Radiation induces double-strand DNA breaks in rapidly proliferating intestinal crypt stem cells, particularly the leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 5–positive (LGR5⁺) stem cells at the crypt base that sustain the crypt-villus axis ([Yang et al., 2023, *Frontiers in Pharmacology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2023.1116558/full)). The resulting cell cycle arrest (via p53/p21 activation), depletion of villus epithelial cells, and disruption of tight junctions compromise the intestinal mucosal barrier, permitting bacterial translocation into the systemic circulation and triggering release of inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS) ([Yang et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2023.1116558/full)). Approximately 40% of patients develop significant intestinal lesions at radiation doses of 10–30 Gy, rising sharply to 90% at doses exceeding 30 Gy ([Yang et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2023.1116558/full)). Radiation enteritis progresses through five overlapping stages: initial injury, major damage response (inflammation and apoptosis), signal amplification, ulceration, and healing ([Yang et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2023.1116558/full)).

**Radiation-Induced Microbiota Shifts**

Ionizing radiation directly and indirectly remodels gut microbial communities. A consistent finding across studies is the depletion of beneficial, mutualistic taxa and the enrichment of pro-inflammatory pathobionts. Specifically, radiation exposure decreases *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* spp. — key commensal probiotics — while concurrently increasing potentially harmful organisms such as *Fusobacterium* and *Proteus* ([Yang et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2023.1116558/full)). At the phylum level, pelvic radiotherapy has been shown to cause a ~10% decrease in *Firmicutes* and a ~3% increase in *Fusobacterium* ([Nam et al., 2021, *Radiotherapy and the Gut Microbiome: Facts and Fiction*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13014-020-01735-9)). The most consistently reported compositional changes across cytotoxic therapies — including radiotherapy — are increases in *Bacteroides* and *Enterobacteriaceae* and decreases in *Bifidobacterium*, *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, and *Clostridium* cluster XIVa (butyrate-producing clostridia) ([Nam et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13014-020-01735-9)). These shifts are functionally consequential: loss of butyrate producers reduces colonocyte fuel supply and anti-inflammatory signaling, while expansion of proteolytic and mucin-degrading taxa further erodes barrier integrity.

**The MARS Trial: Landmark Clinical Evidence**

The Microbiota and Radiotherapy-Induced Gastrointestinal Side-Effects (MARS) study, published by Reis Ferreira et al. in *Clinical Cancer Research* (2019), remains the largest clinical investigation of the microbiome in both acute and late radiation enteropathy ([Reis Ferreira et al., 2019, *Clin Cancer Res*](https://aacrjournals.org/clincancerres/article/25/21/6487/82157/Microbiota-and-Radiotherapy-Induced); [PubMed 31345839](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31345839/)). The MARS study enrolled 32 patients in an acute-toxicity cohort with longitudinal fecal sampling and 103 patients in a late-toxicity cohort. Key findings included:

- A microbiota characterized by comparatively **reduced bacterial diversity over time** was associated with a significantly higher incidence of acute GI toxicity.
- A trend toward higher **pre-radiotherapy diversity** was observed in patients who remained symptom-free (P = 0.09).
- A consistent (though nonsignificant) association between **low bacterial diversity and late radiation enteropathy** was also observed.
- An association of **higher relative abundance of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) producers** with both acute and late toxicity was identified — suggesting that SCFA-producing taxa may be depleted as a consequence of radiation, and their loss may contribute to mucosal vulnerability ([Reis Ferreira et al., 2019](https://aacrjournals.org/clincancerres/article/25/21/6487/82157/Microbiota-and-Radiotherapy-Induced); [Iacovacci et al., 2024, *PMC11314862*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11314862/)).

The MARS study established that a modified microbiota is associated with radiation enteropathy and that key homeostatic intestinal mucosa functions are compromised, providing the first large-scale clinical evidence linking microbiome alterations to radiation-induced intestinal injury.

**Baseline Microbiome as a Predictive Biomarker for Radiation Toxicity**

Building on the MARS findings, the MicroLearner observational cohort study (Iacovacci et al., 2024) demonstrated that **pre-radiotherapy baseline microbiome composition can predict the risk of developing acute GI toxicity** ([Iacovacci et al., 2024, *PMC11314862*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11314862/)). In 136 prostate cancer patients (discovery cohort) and 79 patients (validation cohort), the investigators identified a microbial signature — defined by the relative abundance of *Faecalibacterium*, *Bacteroides*, *Parabacteroides*, *Alistipes*, *Prevotella*, and *Phascolarctobacterium* — that effectively stratified patients into high-risk (60% toxicity rate) and low-risk groups using a clinical decision tree validated in both internal and external cohorts. The high-risk microbiota signature was enriched for genera adapted to oxidative stress and phosphate scarcity, reflecting a host environment characterized by elevated ROS levels that predispose to radiation-induced tissue damage ([Iacovacci et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11314862/)). While this study was conducted in prostate cancer patients, its implications for rectal cancer — where the rectum and adjacent bowel receive high-dose radiation — are directly relevant.

**Bidirectional Microbiota–Radiotherapy Interaction (HP6)**

A critical concept is the **bidirectional relationship** between gut microbiota and radiotherapy, articulated by Li et al. (2022, *PMC9824372*): radiation alters the microbiome (promoting dysbiosis, diversity loss, and pathobiont expansion), and the altered microbiome in turn modulates radiation sensitivity and tissue damage ([Li et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9824372/); [Nam et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13014-020-01735-9)). This bidirectional interaction operates through several mechanisms:

1. **Barrier disruption and bacterial translocation**: Radiation-damaged tight junctions allow luminal bacteria and endotoxins to cross the epithelial barrier, activating systemic inflammatory cascades (TLR4/NF-κB signaling) that amplify tissue injury ([Jian et al., 2021, *Frontiers in Oncology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8358303/); [Yang et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2023.1116558/full)).

2. **Loss of SCFA-mediated protection**: Butyrate-producing bacteria (*Faecalibacterium*, *Roseburia*, *Clostridium* cluster XIVa) are depleted by radiation. Butyrate normally serves as the primary energy source for colonocytes, maintains barrier integrity via tight junction protein expression, and exerts anti-inflammatory effects through HDAC inhibition and GPCR signaling. Its loss removes a key radioprotective mechanism ([Nam et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13014-020-01735-9); [Moraitis et al., 2023, *Trends Endocrinol Metab*](https://www.cell.com/trends/endocrinology-metabolism/fulltext/S1043-2760(23)00108-X)).

3. **Microbiome-driven modulation of radiosensitivity**: Preclinical evidence indicates that specific microbial metabolites can directly influence tumor response to radiation. For example, butyrate has been shown to enhance radiotherapy efficacy against CRC cells while simultaneously protecting normal mucosa, suggesting a dual radiosensitizing and radioprotective role ([Li et al., 2021, *PMC8300561*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8300561/); [Nam et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13014-020-01735-9)).

**Clinical Manifestations: Acute and Chronic Radiation Enteritis**

Radiation enteritis presents across a clinical spectrum. **Acute RE** manifests during or within 90 days of radiotherapy with diarrhea, mucositis, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and (in severe cases) hematochezia. Gastrointestinal adverse events occur in a substantial proportion of patients receiving pelvic radiotherapy, with incidence rates varying by radiation dose, fractionation, and concurrent chemotherapy ([Iacovacci et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11314862/); [Yang et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2023.1116558/full)). When 5-FU-based chemoradiation (e.g., capecitabine plus radiation) is used — the standard neoadjuvant regimen for locally advanced rectal cancer — the incidence of proctitis and diarrhea is further increased compared with radiation alone ([eviQ Cancer Treatments Online](https://www.eviq.org.au/medical-oncology/colorectal/rectal/84-rectal-locally-advanced-capecitabine-chemoradia)).

**Chronic radiation enteropathy** develops in a subset of patients, particularly those with severe or prolonged acute toxicity, and is characterized by intestinal fibrosis, stricture formation, malabsorption, and fistula development. The pathogenesis involves progressive submucosal and mural fibrosis driven by chronic inflammation, vascular injury, and dysregulated wound healing — processes that are themselves influenced by the persistent dysbiosis established during the acute phase ([Nam et al., 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13014-020-01735-9); [Yang et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2023.1116558/full)).

**Summary of Evidence Gaps and Limitations**

While the MARS trial and subsequent studies have established associative links between microbiome composition and radiation enteropathy, several limitations persist. Most clinical studies are observational and cannot establish causality. The MARS study itself noted that associations, while consistent, did not reach statistical significance for all endpoints (e.g., the pre-radiotherapy diversity–symptom association at P = 0.09). The MicroLearner predictive signature, though validated, was derived from prostate cancer patients and requires confirmation in rectal cancer cohorts receiving neoadjuvant chemoradiation. The bidirectional interaction framework (HP6) is supported primarily by preclinical models; definitive clinical evidence that microbiome modulation alters radiation outcomes in CRC patients remains to be established in prospective interventional trials.

### Microbiota as Radiosensitizer and Radioprotector

The gut microbiota plays a dual role in radiotherapy for colorectal cancer (CRC), functioning both as a **radiosensitizer** that enhances tumor cell killing and as a **radioprotector** that mitigates normal intestinal tissue damage. These opposing effects are mediated by distinct microbial taxa, metabolites, and host signaling pathways, and understanding this duality is essential for optimizing radiotherapy outcomes.

#### Microbiota as Radiosensitizer: Enhancing Tumor Response to Radiation

**The butyrate/OR51E1/RALB axis (HP3).** The most compelling preclinical evidence for microbiota-mediated radiosensitization in CRC comes from Dong et al. (2024), who demonstrated that the gut commensal *Roseburia intestinalis* sensitizes CRC cells to radiotherapy through a defined molecular axis ([Dong et al., *Cell Reports* 2024, PMID: 38412097](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38412097/)). In azoxymethane (AOM)/dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-induced CRC mouse models, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) potentiated the tumoricidal effects of radiation and concurrently reduced intestinal toxicity. FMT enriched *R. intestinalis* in the gastrointestinal tract, and oral gavage of *R. intestinalis* alone was sufficient to assemble at the CRC site and synthesize butyrate, sensitizing tumors to radiation in both primary and hepatic metastasis models.

The mechanism proceeds as follows: *R. intestinalis*-derived butyrate activates **OR51E1**, a G-protein-coupled receptor overexpressed in rectal cancer patients. OR51E1 activation facilitates radiogenic autophagy in CRC cells. OR51E1 shows a positive correlation with **RALB** (a Ras-related GTPase) in clinical rectal cancer tissues and CRC mouse models, and pharmacological blockage of the OR51E1/RALB signaling axis restrained butyrate-elicited autophagy in irradiated CRC cells. This study identifies the butyrate/OR51E1/RALB axis as a previously unrecognized mechanism by which a gut commensal bacterium promotes radiation-induced autophagy to accelerate CRC cell death, positioning *R. intestinalis* as a promising radiosensitizer in a preclinical setting ([Dong et al., *Cell Reports* 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38412097/)).

**Microbiota composition and neoadjuvant chemoradiation response.** Clinical evidence supports the relevance of baseline gut microbiota composition to radiotherapy outcomes. In a cohort of rectal cancer patients treated with concurrent chemoradiation (capecitabine + RT), patients with non-complete response (CR) showed relative enrichment of *Bacteroidales* (including *Bacteroidaceae*, *Rikenellaceae*, and *Bacteroides*) compared to those achieving CR, while *Duodenibacillus massiliensis* was associated with improved CR rates ([Reis et al., *Radiotherapy and Oncology* 2020, cited in Springer review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13014-020-01735-9)). Additionally, *Fusobacterium nucleatum* persistence in tumor tissue has been linked to increased risk of recurrence after preoperative treatment in locally advanced rectal cancer, suggesting that intratumoral *F. nucleatum* may promote radioresistance ([Serna et al., *Annals of Oncology* 2020](https://www.annalsofoncology.org/article/S0923-7534(20)39929-4/fulltext)). A broader analysis identified an intratumoral microbiome signature (*Pseudoxanthomonas-Streptomyces-Saccharopolyspora-Bacillus clausii*) highly predictive of long-term survivorship in CRC patients, further supporting the relevance of intratumoral microbiota to radiation outcomes ([Ciernik et al., cited in Springer review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13014-020-01735-9)).

#### Microbiota as Radioprotector: Reducing Normal Tissue Toxicity

**Probiotics for radiation enteritis prevention.** Probiotic supplementation represents the most clinically advanced microbiota-based radioprotective strategy. A systematic review of 13 studies on pelvic radiotherapy found that dysbiotic gut microbiome alterations — including decreased abundance of *Bifidobacterium*, *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, and *Clostridium* cluster XIVa with concurrent increases in *Bacteroides* and *Enterobacteriaceae* — were associated with radiation-induced diarrhea and enteritis ([Manolescu et al., *Frontiers in Oncology* 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2021.745262/full)).

A phase II clinical trial of *Bifidobacterium longum* subsp. *longum* BL21 enrolled 44 patients (29 gynecologic, 15 rectal cancer) receiving pelvic radiotherapy. BL21 was self-administered daily from day 1 of RT. Results showed a favorable safety profile with no serious adverse events attributed to BL21, lower rates of ≥grade 2 acute radiation enteritis (ARE) compared to historical controls, and reduced need for antidiarrheal medications. Microbiota analysis confirmed that BL21 abundance correlated with greater alpha diversity, increased beneficial bacteria, and decreased harmful bacteria ([Yang et al., *Frontiers in Nutrition* 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2025.1626169/full)).

Preclinical studies have further demonstrated that *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG (LGG) improves intestinal crypt survival and reduces radiation-induced apoptosis in the intestine, and that defined probiotic consortia protect against radiation-induced intestinal injury ([Xie et al., *International Journal of Radiation Oncology* 2024](https://www.redjournal.org/article/S0360-3016(24)00395-X/fulltext)). A meta-analysis of probiotic interventions for radiation-induced diarrhea confirmed a statistically significant reduction in diarrhea incidence, though effect sizes were modest and heterogeneity was notable ([Hamad et al., *Clinical Nutrition* 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261561413000502)). Ahrén et al. (2023) reported that two probiotic strains reduced multiple measures of radiation-induced toxicity in women treated for pelvic cancers ([Ahrén et al., *Advances in Radiation Oncology* 2023](https://www.advancesradonc.org/article/S2452-1094(22)00195-6/fulltext)).

**Butyrate and other SCFAs as radioprotective metabolites.** Sodium butyrate enemas have demonstrated clinical efficacy in the treatment of acute radiation-induced proctitis. In a crossover trial, topical sodium butyrate — but not saline — led to remission of symptoms in prostate cancer patients with radiation proctitis ([Hille et al., *International Journal of Radiation Oncology* 2000, PMID: 11072942](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11072942/)). A subsequent multicenter study confirmed that daily sodium butyrate enemas (NABUREN) were effective in treating acute radiation proctitis, with preliminary evidence suggesting superior efficacy compared to standard therapies ([Vernia et al., *Digestive Diseases and Sciences* 2008, PMID: 19107351](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19107351/)). More recently, microencapsulated sodium butyrate at 80 mL/day showed symptom reduction in acute radiation proctitis patients ([MDPI *Journal of Clinical Medicine* 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/14/13/4783)). These findings establish butyrate as a clinically validated radioprotective agent for rectal mucosal injury.

Preclinical work by Fan's group demonstrated that butyrate attenuates post-radiation intestinal injury and inflammation, reverses dysbiosis, and enhances gut integrity ([Yi et al., *Frontiers in Immunology* 2023, cited in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10201781/)). Prebiotic fibers — including inulin, galactooligosaccharides (GOS), fructooligosaccharides (FOS), and resistant starch — support the growth of butyrate-producing bacteria (*Roseburia*, *Faecalibacterium*) and enhance mucosal barrier integrity, though direct clinical evidence for prebiotic fiber supplementation in radiation enteritis remains limited compared to probiotic interventions ([Tao et al., *Nature Communications Biology* 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-026-10263-3); [Ma et al., *Clinical and Translational Medicine* 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12483842/)).

#### The Dual and Context-Dependent Role of Butyrate

A critical nuance is that butyrate exerts **context-dependent, opposing effects** on radiotherapy outcomes. While *R. intestinalis*-derived butyrate sensitizes CRC cells to radiation via the OR51E1/RALB/autophagy axis (Dong et al. 2024), Yang et al. (2021) demonstrated that gut microbiota-derived butyrate simultaneously **suppresses local type I interferon (IFN-I)** production in the tumor microenvironment by inhibiting STING activation in dendritic cells, thereby **impairing** the antitumor effects of ionizing radiation ([Yang et al., *Journal of Experimental Medicine* 2021, 218(3):e20201915](https://rupress.org/jem/article/218/3/e20201915/211721/Suppression-of-local-type-I-interferon-by-gut)). This dual role has profound implications: butyrate may enhance direct tumor cell killing through autophagy while simultaneously dampening the immune-mediated antitumor response that depends on STING–IFN-I signaling.

The STING–cGAS pathway activation and subsequent IFN-I release are essential for the **abscopal effect** — the regression of unirradiated distant metastases following localized radiotherapy combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Vanpouille-Box et al. identified strong IFN-I activation after RT as a prerequisite for abscopal effect induction, and Formenti et al. found elevated IFN-I blood levels after RT as a predictor of abscopal responses in patients treated with anti-CTLA4 ([Felchle et al., *Immunity* 2024, PMC10945164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10945164/)). By suppressing STING, butyrate may paradoxically limit the abscopal effect while enhancing direct radiosensitivity.

#### Gut Microbiota and the Abscopal Effect

The relationship between gut microbiota and the abscopal effect has been explored in preclinical models with nuanced findings. Felchle et al. (2024) used a bilateral MC38 tumor model (radioimmunotherapy with anti-CTLA4 or anti-PD1) and found that broad-spectrum antibiotic-mediated intestinal decontamination had **no significant effect** on abscopal tumor regression. Furthermore, oral administration of the microbiota-derived metabolites desaminotyrosine (DAT) and indole-3-carboxaldehyde (ICA) — both previously shown to enhance IFN-I activation in inflammatory disease models — failed to boost the abscopal effect. ICA even appeared to reduce outcomes after radioimmunotherapy with anti-CTLA4 ([Felchle et al., *Immunity* 2024, PMC10945164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10945164/)).

These findings suggest that the relationship between gut microbiota and abscopal responses is more complex than initially anticipated. Uribe-Herranz et al. found improved tumor regression after RT in mice treated with vancomycin, and Shiao et al. identified a negative impact of the intestinal fungome on RT outcomes ([Felchle et al. 2024, citing prior work](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10945164/)). Zhou et al. (2023) reported that methylglyoxal from gut microbes boosts radiosensitivity through abscopal effect modeling, indicating that specific microbial metabolites — but not all — may enhance systemic antitumor immunity ([Zhou et al., *Journal for ImmunoTherapy of Cancer* 2023](https://jitc.bmj.com/content/11/11/e007840)). Tonneau et al. (2021) proposed that the microbiome may trigger the immune system to observe a more systemic abscopal effect, though clinical evidence remains limited ([Tonneau et al., *Radiotherapy and Oncology* 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S016781402030880X)).

#### Implications for Neoadjuvant Chemoradiation in Locally Advanced Rectal Cancer

Neoadjuvant chemoradiation (5-FU-based or capecitabine with pelvic RT) for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) represents the clinical setting where microbiota-radiotherapy interactions are most relevant. Radiation-induced dysbiosis — depletion of *Bifidobacterium*, *Lactobacillus*, and butyrate producers with enrichment of *Fusobacterium*, *Proteus*, and pro-inflammatory pathobionts — contributes to both acute toxicity (radiation enteritis, proctitis) and potentially to tumor response modulation ([Lam et al., *Frontiers in Pharmacology* 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2023.1116558/full)). The bidirectional interaction between gut microbiota and radiotherapy — radiation alters the microbiome, and the altered microbiome in turn modulates radiation sensitivity and tissue damage — creates a feedback loop that may amplify or attenuate treatment outcomes ([Li et al., *Frontiers in Pharmacology* 2022, PMC9824372](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9824372/)). Preclinical evidence that *R. intestinalis* simultaneously sensitizes CRC to radiation and alleviates intestinal toxicity (Dong et al. 2024) suggests that targeted microbiome modulation could achieve the dual goal of enhancing efficacy while reducing toxicity, though clinical translation remains to be validated.

## Future Outlook: Probiotics/Prebiotics and Fecal Microbiota Transplantation

### Probiotics, Prebiotics, and Synbiotics as Adjunctive Therapy

The concept of supplementing probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics alongside conventional CRC treatment has attracted growing interest as a strategy to mitigate treatment toxicity, improve surgical recovery, and potentially enhance antitumor immunity. However, the evidence base remains heterogeneous, with most clinical data derived from small randomized controlled trials (RCTs) using diverse strains, doses, and outcome measures.

#### Systematic Review Evidence

Two major systematic reviews anchor the current evidence. **Moreira et al. 2024** conducted a PRISMA-compliant systematic review of 24 RCTs examining prebiotic, probiotic, or synbiotic supplementation in CRC patients undergoing treatment ([Moreira et al., *Frontiers in Oncology*, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). The review found that supplementation improved surgical outcomes — reducing the incidence of infectious and non-infectious postoperative complications, accelerating return to normal gut function, shortening hospital length of stay, and decreasing antibiotic usage. It also alleviated chemotherapy- and radiotherapy-related side effects, primarily diarrhea. However, the authors emphasized that evidence on the optimal strains, dosage, and duration of intervention remains scarce, and called for larger, less heterogeneous RCTs to confirm these benefits ([Moreira et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)).

**Kan, Yang et al. 2024** published a systematic review and meta-analysis in *Journal of Digestive Diseases* (Wiley) focusing on the efficacy and safety of probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics for CRC and precancerous lesion prevention in high-risk populations ([Kan et al., *J Dig Dis*, 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1751-2980.13247)). The meta-analysis found that probiotic supplementation significantly reduced adenoma incidence, although no significant benefit was observed for CRC incidence itself. The authors concluded that probiotics may have beneficial effects in CRC prevention but that more RCTs with larger sample sizes are warranted. A separate meta-analysis of 18 RCTs examining specifically chemotherapy-related diarrhea in CRC patients found that probiotics reduced the risk of grade 3–4 diarrhea, with *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG among the most studied strains (Yang et al., *Medicine*, 2025; [PMC12237357](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12237357/), post-cutoff).

#### Strains with the Strongest Evidence

**Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG)** is the most extensively studied probiotic in the CRC treatment context. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial by Österlund et al. (2007) demonstrated that LGG supplementation was well tolerated and reduced the frequency of severe diarrhea and abdominal discomfort in CRC patients receiving 5-FU-based chemotherapy ([Österlund et al., *World J Gastroenterol*, 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2360429/)). LGG's proposed mechanisms include competitive exclusion of pathogenic bacteria through adhesion to intestinal mucus, enhancement of tight-junction protein expression, and reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines ([Nature, *Oncogene*, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)).

**Bifidobacterium** species have the most CRC-specific evidence among probiotic genera. *B. breve* and *B. infantis* activate intestinal dendritic cells via toll-like receptor signaling, promoting Treg expression and reducing Th17-mediated inflammation ([Nature, *Oncogene*, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)). *Bifidobacterium longum* and *B. adolescentis* have been associated with enhanced anti-PD-1 immunotherapy response: *B. bifidum* boosted anti-PD-L1 efficacy in a melanoma mouse model by enhancing antitumor immunity ([Pei et al., *Frontiers in Immunology*, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10987355/)), and *B. catenulatum* was shown to suppress colorectal tumorigenesis through acetate production, improving anti-PD-1 efficacy via CD8+ T-cell activation in MSS CRC models (Li et al., *Gut*, 2026, post-cutoff). Notably, a 2025 study in *Cancer Cell* identified *Clostridium butyricum* — a spore-forming, butyrate-producing probiotic — as a tumor-resident bacterium that potentiates the antitumor effect of anti-PD-1 in orthotopic CRC allografts (Cell, 2025, post-cutoff; [PubMed 40780216](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40780216/)). This builds on earlier findings in renal cell carcinoma, where *C. butyricum* supplementation improved anti-PD-1 response ([Spencer et al., *Nature Medicine*, 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-022-01723-4)).

**Saccharomyces boulardii**, a non-pathogenic yeast, has demonstrated efficacy in reducing irinotecan-induced intestinal mucositis and diarrhea, likely through its anti-inflammatory properties and inhibition of pathogenic bacterial overgrowth ([López-Gómez et al., *PMC*, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10607965/); [Kaufman et al., 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/29933935.2024.2379475)).

Multi-strain formulations such as **VSL#3** (now marketed as Visbiome), containing eight bacterial strains including *Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium*, and *Streptococcus* species, have shown promise in delaying the transition from inflammation to dysplasia and cancer in preclinical CRC models ([Appleyard et al., *Cancer Research*, 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3233787/)). VSL#3 is also under investigation for preventing immune checkpoint inhibitor–induced colitis (NCT06508034, [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06508034)).

#### Prebiotic and Synbiotic Evidence

Prebiotic fibers — including **inulin**, galactooligosaccharides (GOS), fructooligosaccharides (FOS), and resistant starch — serve as substrates for beneficial bacterial fermentation, promoting SCFA production (particularly butyrate) and lowering colonic pH to inhibit pathogen growth. Inulin supplementation has been associated with reduced CRC risk in preclinical models, with inulin fermentation increasing colonic butyrate concentrations and reducing tumor formation ([PMC11231048](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11231048/)). Combined GOS and inulin treatment showed synergistic inhibition of aberrant crypt foci (ACF) in rodent CRC models, outperforming either prebiotic alone ([MDPI *Nutrients*, 2016](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/8/8/465)).

**Synbiotics** — combining prebiotics with probiotics such as GOS + *Bifidobacterium* or inulin + *Lactobacillus* — aim to enhance probiotic survival and colonization through targeted nutritional support. The Moreira et al. 2024 review did not find definitive evidence that synbiotics outperform probiotics alone, but the combination approach is mechanistically rational and remains an active area of investigation ([Moreira et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)). A prior meta-analysis by Veziant et al. (2022) found that probiotic/synbiotic supplementation reduced infectious complications after CRC surgery, with synbiotics showing a trend toward greater benefit than single-strain probiotics ([Veziant et al., *Nutrients*, 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/15/3066)).

#### Proposed Mechanisms

Probiotics and related interventions are proposed to improve CRC treatment outcomes through several mechanisms ([Nature, *Oncogene*, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)):

1. **Competitive exclusion of pathogens**: Probiotics compete for nutrients and adhesion sites, produce bacteriocins, and lower luminal pH through lactic and acetic acid production, inhibiting colonization by pathogens such as *C. difficile*, *F. nucleatum*, and pks⁺ *E. coli*.
2. **Enhancement of barrier function**: Probiotics increase mucin production, upregulate tight-junction proteins (e.g., claudins, occludin), and promote epithelial restitution, reducing bacterial translocation and systemic inflammation — particularly relevant during chemotherapy-induced mucositis and radiation enteritis.
3. **Immune modulation**: Strain-specific immunomodulation includes activation of dendritic cells, reduction of Th17-mediated inflammation, increase in Treg expression, and polarization of macrophages toward the M2 (anti-inflammatory) phenotype. Systemically, certain strains enhance Th17 and reduce Treg expression to boost antitumor immunity.
4. **SCFA production**: Butyrate-producing probiotics and prebiotic fiber fermentation generate butyrate, propionate, and acetate — which serve as colonocyte energy sources, inhibit histone deacetylases (HDACs) to induce tumor cell apoptosis, and maintain mucosal integrity.
5. **Carcinogen binding**: Some probiotic strains can bind dietary carcinogens and mutagenic compounds, potentially reducing their bioavailability.

#### Safety Concerns

A critical limitation of probiotic therapy in CRC patients is the risk of **bacterial translocation** — particularly in immunocompromised individuals receiving chemotherapy, with mucosal barrier disruption, or with central venous catheters. Documented cases of probiotic-associated bacteremia and fungemia (including *Lactobacillus* and *Saccharomyces* species) have been reported in critically ill and immunocompromised patients ([Liong et al., *Food Chem Toxicol*, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18366533/); [Kothari et al., *Biomed Pharmacother*, 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0753332218345657)). The risk is amplified in patients with neutropenia, severe mucositis, or intestinal barrier compromise — all common during CRC chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Additionally, horizontal transfer of antimicrobial resistance genes from probiotic strains to resident gut microbiota is a theoretical concern requiring careful strain selection and surveillance ([Nature, *Oncogene*, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41388-020-1341-1)).

#### Postbiotics and Engineered Biotherapeutics: Next-Generation Approaches

**Postbiotics** — defined as preparations of inanimate microorganisms or their components (including SCFAs, tryptophan metabolites, bile acids, vitamins, enzymes, and bacterial cell wall components) — offer a potentially safer alternative to live probiotics, with greater chemical stability and no risk of translocation or uncontrolled bacterial growth ([Salminen et al., *Frontiers in Microbiology*, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2024.1360225/full)). Butyrate, the most studied postbiotic in CRC, exerts antineoplastic effects through HDAC inhibition, induction of colonocyte apoptosis, and maintenance of mucosal barrier integrity. **Indole-3-propionic acid** (IPA), a tryptophan metabolite produced by commensal bacteria, has shown chemopreventive properties in CRC models, with fiber intake associated with increased colonic IPA concentrations and reduced tumor formation ([Fang et al., *PMC*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036377/)). Other postbiotic candidates include propionic acid (HDAC inhibitor promoting CRC apoptosis), indole-3-lactic acid, and microbial exopolysaccharides ([Salminen et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2024.1360225/full)). Postbiotics avoid the biosafety risks of live bacterial administration while retaining immunomodulatory and anticarcinogenic properties.

**Engineered biotherapeutics** represent a further frontier. Synthetic bacterial consortia and engineered strains — including modified *E. coli* Nissle and *Pediococcus* species designed for targeted drug delivery to CRC tumors — have demonstrated preclinical efficacy in suppressing tumor growth and restoring healthy gut microbiota ([PMC8157686](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157686/); [Nature Communications, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-15508-1)). Defined microbial consortia such as SER-109 (spore-based), RBX2660, and VE303 offer standardized, reproducible alternatives to whole-stool FMT with improved safety profiles. **Bacteriophage therapy** targeting CRC-associated pathobionts such as *F. nucleatum* is in preclinical development, offering strain-specific depletion without disrupting the broader commensal community. These approaches remain early-stage, with no completed clinical trials in CRC as of September 2024.

#### Key Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

The field faces several translational challenges: (1) **strain-specificity** — efficacy is highly strain-dependent, making cross-study comparisons difficult; (2) **lack of standardization** — variability in formulations, doses, and delivery routes across trials; (3) **small sample sizes** — most RCTs enrolled fewer than 100 patients, limiting statistical power; (4) **regulatory ambiguity** — probiotics are regulated as dietary supplements rather than pharmaceuticals in most jurisdictions, with no FDA-approved probiotic specifically indicated for CRC treatment; (5) **confounding by diet, antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors, and comorbidities** — all of which independently shape the gut microbiome; and (6) **the persistent gap between preclinical promise and clinical validation** — while mechanistic studies demonstrate compelling antitumor and anti-toxicity effects, robust phase III evidence confirming improved oncologic endpoints remains absent ([Moreira et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full); [Kan et al., 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1751-2980.13247)). Until these gaps are addressed, probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics should be regarded as promising but still investigational adjunctive modalities in CRC care.

### Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) in CRC: Current Evidence and Future Potential

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) — the transfer of processed stool from a healthy donor to a recipient's gastrointestinal tract — represents the most direct strategy for reshaping the entire gut microbial community. In CRC, the rationale is compelling: because dysbiosis drives treatment resistance across chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy (as detailed in S3–S5), a wholesale microbial community replacement could theoretically overcome these barriers simultaneously. However, FMT remains an investigational modality in oncology, with no CRC-specific regulatory approval and a clinical evidence base that is still maturing.

#### Clinical Evidence: From Melanoma to CRC

The strongest clinical evidence for FMT as a cancer treatment adjunct comes from melanoma immunotherapy trials, which provide proof-of-concept applicable to CRC. **Baruch et al. (2021, *Science*)** conducted a phase I trial in which FMT from two donors who had achieved complete response (CR) on anti-PD-1 therapy was administered to 10 patients with metastatic melanoma refractory to PD-1 blockade, followed by reinduction of anti-PD-1. Three of ten patients responded — two partial responses and one complete response — and FMT was shown to be safe and feasible, with microbiome analysis demonstrating successful engraftment of donor-derived taxa and favorable shifts in immune cell infiltration ([Baruch et al. 2021, *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33303685/)). This built on foundational work by **Routy et al. (2018, *Science*)**, who demonstrated that gut microbiome composition modulates anti-PD-1 efficacy in melanoma and that antibiotic exposure abrogated clinical benefit ([Routy et al. 2018, *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29097494/)).

The **FMT-LUMINate trial** (NCT04951583), a multicenter, open-label, phase 2 study, assessed healthy-donor FMT plus anti-PD-1 (pembrolizumab) in 40 patients with advanced NSCLC or melanoma. In the melanoma cohort, the overall response rate (ORR) was 75% (15/20), and FMT was deemed safe by an independent data and safety monitoring committee with no grade ≥3 FMT-related adverse events ([Duttagupta et al. 2026, *Nature Medicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41606121/); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04951583](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04951583)). Although this trial did not enroll CRC patients, the results establish that FMT can convert immunotherapy non-responders to responders — a finding directly relevant to the challenge of MSS CRC resistance to checkpoint inhibitors.

At **MD Anderson Cancer Center**, Yinghong Wang, MD, has pioneered FMT applications in cancer patients, including a phase II trial (NCT04038619) studying FMT for immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-associated colitis — the first case series demonstrating that FMT could successfully treat refractory ICI colitis ([Wang et al. 2018, *J Immunother Cancer*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30420754/)). MD Anderson also launched a trial (NCT04951583-related) of FMT plus anti-PD-1 re-introduction in patients with anti-PD-1-refractory solid tumors, including CRC ([MD Anderson Clinical Trials](https://www.mdanderson.org/patients-family/diagnosis-treatment/clinical-trials/clinical-trials-index/clinical-trials-detail.ID2020-0186.html)).

**CRC-specific evidence is largely preclinical.** **Su et al. (2024)** demonstrated that FMT alone, sodium selenite alone, and particularly the combination of FMT with sodium selenite significantly inhibited CRC development in a murine model. The combined treatment increased the abundance of beneficial bacteria, restored microbial diversity, and suppressed tumor growth more effectively than either intervention alone ([Su et al. 2024, *Biochemical and Biophysical Research Communications*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39213702/)). A separate phase 1 trial, **PERFORM**, investigated capecitabine combined with FMT in CRC patients, finding that the combination prevented CRC progression through correction of microbial dysbiosis and enhancement of anti-tumor immunity ([Fernandes et al. 2026, *Nature Medicine*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-04183-8)). These early results are encouraging but require validation in larger, randomized trials.

#### Safety Concerns and Regulatory Status

FMT carries inherent risks that are amplified in cancer patients, who are often immunocompromised from chemotherapy, corticosteroids, or the tumor itself. In June 2019, the **FDA issued a safety communication** reporting that two immunocompromised adults who received investigational FMT developed invasive infections caused by extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing *Escherichia coli*, resulting in one death. The donor stool had not been tested for multi-drug resistant organisms (MDROs) prior to use ([FDA Safety Communication, June 2019](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medical-product-safety-information/fecal-microbiota-transplantation-safety-communication-risk-serious-adverse-reactions-due)). The FDA subsequently mandated that all IND holders implement MDRO screening (including ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, VRE, CRE, and MRSA) and exclude high-risk donors (health care workers, recently hospitalized individuals, medical tourists) by July 15, 2019. A follow-up safety alert in March 2020 reinforced these requirements ([FDA Safety Alert, March 2020](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/safety-availability-biologics/safety-alert-regarding-use-fecal-microbiota-transplantation-and-risk-serious-adverse-events-likely)). These events underscore the particular vulnerability of oncology patients and the need for rigorous donor screening beyond standard protocols.

Currently, the only FDA-approved FMT products are indicated for recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI), not for cancer. **Rebyota** (fecal microbiota, live-jslm) became the first FDA-approved live biotherapeutic product in November 2022 for prevention of recurrent CDI, administered as a single-dose rectal enema ([FDA Press Release, November 2022](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-fecal-microbiota-product-prevention-recurrence-clostridioides)). **Vowst** (fecal microbiota spores), an oral capsule formulation, followed in April 2023 for the same indication ([FDA Press Release, April 2023](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-orally-administered-fecal-microbiota-product-prevention-recurrence-clostridioides)). Both products are derived from screened donor stool but are pathogen-reduced spore preparations, not whole-community transplants. No FMT product has received FDA approval as an adjunct to cancer therapy, and all oncologic FMT use requires an IND application.

#### Delivery Methods and Standardization Challenges

FMT can be delivered via colonoscopy (direct infusion into the cecum or ascending colon), oral capsules (lyophilized or frozen), or nasoduodenal/nasojejunal tube. Each route has trade-offs: colonoscopy allows direct mucosal assessment and targeted delivery but is invasive and resource-intensive; capsules are non-invasive and scalable but may have variable engraftment efficiency; nasoduodenal tubes are less invasive than colonoscopy but carry aspiration risk and patient discomfort. No consensus exists on the optimal delivery route for oncologic FMT, and comparative data in CRC are lacking.

Standardization remains a critical barrier. Donor screening protocols vary across stool banks, and there is no universally accepted definition of a "super-donor" — though evidence from Baruch et al. suggests that donors with favorable immunotherapy outcomes may produce more efficacious FMT. Preparation methods (anaerobic processing, cryopreservation, filtration) affect viability and composition. The absence of standardized potency assays, dose definitions, and quality control metrics complicates reproducibility and regulatory oversight.

#### Defined Microbial Consortia and Next-Generation Alternatives

To address the safety and standardization limitations of whole-stool FMT, several **defined microbial consortia** have been developed:

- **SER-109** (now Vowst): A purified spore consortium derived from healthy donor stool, FDA-approved for recurrent CDI. Its defined composition eliminates the risk of transmitting unknown pathogens but may lack the full community diversity needed for oncologic indications.
- **RBX2660** (now Rebyota): A broad-spectrum live biotherapeutic containing a diverse bacterial community from screened donors, also approved for recurrent CDI. It represents a middle ground between whole-stool FMT and highly defined consortia ([Wang et al. 2024, *PMC11117328*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11117328/)).
- **VE303**: An investigational 8-strain defined consortium designed for recurrent CDI, with the potential to become a first-in-class defined therapeutic. Its fully characterized composition offers the highest level of standardization and safety monitoring ([Vedanta Biosciences, as reviewed in PMC11117328](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11117328/)).

For CRC specifically, defined consortia enriched in taxa with demonstrated anti-tumor activity — such as *Akkermansia muciniphila*, *Roseburia intestinalis*, and *Bifidobacterium* spp. — could theoretically replicate the beneficial effects of FMT without the infection risk. However, no defined consortium has yet entered clinical trials for CRC treatment enhancement.

#### Bacteriophage Therapy Targeting CRC-Associated Pathobionts

An emerging complementary strategy is **bacteriophage therapy** targeting specific CRC-promoting bacteria. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that *Fusobacterium nucleatum*-specific bacteriophages can selectively eliminate this oncobacterium without disrupting the broader microbiome. **Kabwe et al. (2025)** showed that the FNU1 phage limited *F. nucleatum*'s pro-tumorigenic effects in colon cancer and gastric cancer cell models ([Kabwe et al. 2025, *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1721411/full)). A separate study by **Lam et al. (2025)** identified a novel bacteriophage with the potential to inhibit *F. nucleatum* in CRC ([Lam et al. 2025, *Journal of Microbiology*](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-6382/14/1/45)). A comprehensive review by **Kowsarkhizi et al. (2025)** concluded that a combination approach — integrating antibiotics, probiotics, bacteriophage therapy, and immune-based interventions — may be necessary to effectively target *F. nucleatum* given its ability to modulate the tumor microenvironment ([Kowsarkhizi et al. 2025, *PMC12522427*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12522427/)). While still preclinical, phage therapy represents a precision alternative to the blunt instrument of whole-stool FMT.

#### Systematic Review Evidence: Wekking et al. 2025

The most comprehensive assessment of FMT in cancer treatment comes from **Wekking et al. (2025, *Cancer Treatment Reviews*)**, a systematic review evaluating FMT's impact on cancer treatment outcomes and treatment-related toxicity. The review concluded that FMT appears to be a **safe and feasible** adjunctive strategy during both first-line and later-line cancer therapy, with potential for managing treatment-related colitis and enhancing treatment response. The authors noted that FMT may modulate the gut ecosystem to enhance treatment response and reduce toxicity, but emphasized that evidence remains heterogeneous, with most data coming from melanoma and lung cancer rather than CRC ([Wekking et al. 2025, *Cancer Treatment Reviews*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41061376/); [ScienceDirect full text](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305737225001471)). This review provides the strongest current evidence that FMT's benefits may extend across tumor types, but also highlights the urgent need for CRC-specific randomized trials.

#### Summary Assessment

FMT holds genuine promise as a future therapeutic modality in CRC, supported by proof-of-concept data in melanoma immunotherapy, preclinical evidence in CRC models, and early-phase clinical signals. However, several barriers must be overcome before clinical translation: (1) the near-total absence of randomized CRC-specific FMT trials; (2) safety risks from MDRO transmission in immunocompromised patients, as underscored by the FDA alerts; (3) the lack of any FDA-approved oncologic FMT indication; (4) standardization challenges in donor selection, preparation, and delivery; and (5) the unresolved question of whether whole-stool FMT offers advantages over defined consortia for specific oncologic indications. Defined microbial consortia and phage therapy represent next-generation approaches that may ultimately prove safer and more reproducible than whole-stool FMT, but they require their own clinical validation. For now, FMT in CRC remains an investigational strategy best pursued within the framework of clinical trials.

**Conflicting Evidence Notes:**

**(a) *F. nucleatum* paradox in immunotherapy.** Two seemingly contradictory findings must be reconciled: Jiang et al. 2023 demonstrated that Fn-derived succinic acid induces anti-PD-1 resistance via SUCNR1-mediated T-cell suppression in metastatic CRC patients ([*Cell Host & Microbe* 31(5):781–797](https://www.cell.com/cell-host-microbe/fulltext/S1931-3128(23)00161-0)), while Wang et al. 2024 showed that Fn paradoxically sensitizes MSS CRC to anti-PD-1, with FMT from Fn-high donors enhancing anti-PD-1 response ([*Cancer Cell*, Sep 2024](https://www.cell.com/cancer-cell/fulltext/S1535-6108%2824%2900318-0)). This discrepancy may reflect differences in: (i) tumor context — endogenous Fn colonization vs. FMT-mediated introduction; (ii) Fn abundance thresholds — low vs. high Fn load may have divergent immune effects; (iii) MSS vs. MSI-H status; and (iv) the specific immune mechanisms engaged — succinic acid/SUCNR1 suppression vs. Fn-mediated immune activation via FadA/Fap2 or formate/AhR pathways. Both findings are valid and context-dependent; Fn's role in ICI response is not unidirectional.

**(b) Anastomotic leak independent of microbiota shifts.** The 2024 *Nature Scientific Reports* study by Boatman et al. found that AL occurs independently of microbiota shifts associated with surgical bowel preparation (Sci Rep 14:21711; [link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-72520-x)). This challenges the prevailing hypothesis that microbiome changes from MBP directly cause AL and suggests AL is multifactorial — involving surgical technique, patient comorbidities, and local tissue factors. However, this does not negate the broader evidence that dysbiosis (independent of MBP) is associated with AL risk, as demonstrated by Hajjar et al. 2024 ([*Clin Cancer Res* 30(3):616](https://aacrjournals.org/clincancerres/article/30/3/616/733547/Modulating-Gut-Microbiota-Prevents-Anastomotic)), who showed that modulating gut microbiota prevents AL and reduces local cancer cell dissemination in mouse models. The two findings are complementary: MBP-induced dysbiosis may not be the primary driver of AL, but the baseline microbial community composition remains a relevant factor in anastomotic healing.

### Future Directions: Precision Microbiome Medicine in CRC

The preceding sections established that gut microbiota composition modulates efficacy and toxicity across surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy in CRC. Translating these mechanistic insights into clinically actionable strategies requires advancing six interdependent frontiers: microbiome-based biomarkers, multi-omics integration, engineered biotherapeutics, spatial tumor microbiome mapping, artificial intelligence (AI)/machine learning (ML) prediction, and regulatory/clinical trial infrastructure. Each faces distinct challenges that collectively determine whether microbiome medicine will become a standard component of CRC care.

#### Microbiome-Based Biomarkers for Treatment Selection

The most immediate clinical opportunity lies in using microbial signatures to predict treatment response. *Fusobacterium nucleatum* (Fn) is the most extensively studied candidate. In a cohort of 94 advanced CRC patients receiving 5-FU-based adjuvant chemotherapy, high Fn abundance correlated with chemoresistance and recurrence (47.8% recurrence rate), establishing Fn as a potential predictive biomarker for chemotherapy failure ([Rubinstein et al. 2019, J Exp Clin Cancer Res](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6327560/)). More recently, Wang et al. 2024 demonstrated that Fn can paradoxically sensitize MSS CRC to anti-PD-1 therapy, suggesting its presence may also serve as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response in this typically resistant subtype ([Wang et al. 2024, Cancer Cell](https://www.cell.com/cancer-cell/fulltext/S1535-6108%2824%2900318-0)). However, this stands in tension with Jiang et al. 2023, who showed that Fn-derived succinic acid induces anti-PD-1 resistance via SUCNR1-mediated T-cell suppression in metastatic CRC patients ([Jiang et al. 2023, Cell Host & Microbe](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1931312823001610)). This Fn paradox—where the same bacterium both sensitizes and resists immunotherapy depending on tumor context, Fn abundance, and MSS vs. MSI-H status—remains unresolved and complicates its deployment as a standalone biomarker.

Beyond Fn, *Akkermansia muciniphila* has emerged as a candidate predictive marker for ICI response through the inosine-CD73-IFN-γ axis ([Mager et al. 2020, Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abc3421)), and *Roseburia intestinalis* abundance has been linked to anti-PD-1 efficacy in CRC via butyrate-mediated CD8+ T-cell activation ([Kang et al. 2023, PMC10579466](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579466/)). At the intratumoral level, a study profiling tumors from curative surgery found intratumoral Fusobacterium in approximately 21% of tumors, with Fn-positive tumors associated with higher recurrence risk ([ESMO Open, 2026](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1138809); post-cutoff). Multi-cohort metagenomic analyses have identified cross-cohort microbial signatures—including *F. nucleatum*, *Bacteroides fragilis*, and *Parvimonas micra*—that distinguish CRC from healthy controls across populations ([Wirbel et al. 2019, Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41579-023-00973-4); [Thomas et al. 2019, Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41579-023-00973-4)), but validation as treatment-selection biomarkers in prospective trials remains incomplete.

#### Multi-Omics Integration for Precision Microbiome Profiling

Single-modality microbiome analysis captures only a fraction of the host-microbe interaction landscape. Integrating shotgun metagenomics with metatranscriptomics, metabolomics, proteomics, and host transcriptomics can link microbial community structure to functional activity and treatment-relevant metabolite production. Gao et al. 2022 demonstrated this approach by integrating gut microbial signatures with serum metabolites across CRC cohorts, revealing associations between specific microbial taxa and metabolite profiles that were invisible to 16S rRNA sequencing alone ([Gao et al. 2022, Gastroenterology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41579-023-00973-4)). Liu et al. 2022 extended integration to the fungal kingdom, showing that bacterial-fungal interactions serve as biomarkers across cohorts ([Liu et al. 2022, Nature Microbiology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41579-023-00973-4)).

More recently, Jayakrishnan et al. 2024 applied multi-omics machine learning to host-microbiome interactions in early-onset CRC, demonstrating that integrating microbiome data with host genomic and clinical variables improves prediction of cancer risk beyond any single modality ([Jayakrishnan et al. 2024, npj Precision Oncology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41698-024-00647-1)). An integrative multi-omics analysis of 274 CRC patients similarly showed that gut microbiota composition correlates with host transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles in ways that predict clinical outcomes ([Wang et al. 2025, PMC12255678](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12255678/); post-cutoff). Zhang et al. 2024 reviewed state-of-the-art systems biology approaches for integrating multi-omics data in microbiome-host interaction research, emphasizing the need for computational frameworks that can handle the high dimensionality and compositional nature of microbiome data ([Zhang et al. 2024, Cell Reports Medicine](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-medicine/fulltext/S2666-3791(24)00468-3)).

Despite this progress, the field lacks standardized multi-omics pipelines. The choice between 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing (lower cost, limited taxonomic resolution) and shotgun metagenomics (higher resolution, greater cost and computational burden) introduces heterogeneity that complicates cross-study comparison. A 2025 analysis highlighted that microbiome confounders and quantitative profiling challenges actively undermine the identification of reliable microbial targets in CRC development ([Nature Medicine, 2024](https://www.worldgastroenterology.org/publications/news-you-can-use/microbiome-signatures-for-colorectal-cancer-crc--dealing-with-confounding-variables-to-find-the-real-message)).

#### Engineered Biotherapeutics and Defined Consortia

Next-generation microbiome therapeutics are moving beyond whole-stool FMT toward precisely defined interventions. Three categories are in active preclinical or clinical development:

1. **Synthetic bacterial consortia**: Zhou et al. 2024 demonstrated that oral administration of a synthetic bacterial consortium ameliorated AOM/DSS-induced CRC in mice, establishing feasibility for rationally designed microbial communities ([Zhou et al. 2024, Nature Biomedical Engineering](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41522-024-00479-8)). An 11-strain consortium (Gazzaniga et al., JCI) has been explored in the context of ICI enhancement, though it remains preclinical. Wang et al. 2025 engineered a synthetic microbial community that alleviated Fn-infected CRC via ecological control, demonstrating that targeted displacement of pathobionts is achievable ([Wang et al. 2025, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13045-025-01766-3); post-cutoff).

2. **Engineered bacterial therapeutics**: Siguenza et al. 2024 reviewed the emerging use of engineered bacteria—including modified *E. coli* Nissle strains—to detect early CRC development, monitor the adenoma-to-carcinoma transition, and deliver therapeutic payloads ([Siguenza et al. 2024, Trends in Cancer](https://www.cell.com/trends/cancer/abstract/S2405-8033%2824%2900074-8)). Engineered bacteria have also been combined with radiotherapy to alleviate intratumoral hypoxia and increase radiosensitivity ([Chang et al. 2025, Frontiers in Microbiology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2025.1545334/full); post-cutoff).

3. **Bacteriophage therapy**: Kabwe et al. 2025 isolated bacteriophage FNU1, which specifically targets *F. nucleatum* and negates its induced cell growth, migration, and chemotherapy resistance in gastrointestinal cancer cells ([Kabwe et al. 2025, Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1721411/full); post-cutoff). Lam et al. 2025 similarly identified a novel bacteriophage with inhibitory potential against Fn ([Lam et al. 2025, Antibiotics](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-6382/14/1/45); post-cutoff). These approaches offer the advantage of selectively eliminating pathobionts without disrupting commensal communities, but all evidence remains preclinical.

Among defined consortia, SER-109 (purified Firmicutes spores, Seres Therapeutics) and RBX2660 (Rebyota, Ferring) have achieved FDA approval—but only for recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection, not for oncology indications. VE303 (Vedanta Biosciences), an 8-strain *Clostridia* consortium, has completed Phase 2 trials for rCDI prevention ([Louie et al. 2023, NEJM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10105904/)). No defined consortium has yet entered oncology-specific clinical trials for CRC, representing a critical translational gap.

#### Spatial Microbiome Mapping of Tumors

The recognition that bacteria reside within tumor tissue—not merely in the luminal microbiome—has opened a new dimension of investigation. Nejman et al. 2020 profiled intratumoral microbiota across 33 cancer types, revealing that each tumor type harbors a distinct intracellular bacterial composition, with bacteria present in both cancer and immune cells ([Nejman et al. 2020, Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32467638/)). Galeano Niño et al. 2022 extended this by showing that intratumoral microbiota localize to spatially distinct "microniches" within tumors, characterized by unique physiological and immunological states, and that these spatial patterns influence tumor cell heterogeneity ([Galeano Niño et al. 2022, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-05435-0)).

Technological advances are enabling increasingly granular spatial resolution. MERFISH (multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization) enables single-cell imaging of up to 1,000 genes and thousands of RNA species simultaneously ([PMC11660798, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11660798/); post-cutoff). Imaging mass cytometry (e.g., Hyperion system) has been applied to examine 52 stage III CRC tumors, mapping immune cell populations and microbial markers within the tumor microenvironment. NanoString's GeoMx platform has been used for deep spatial characterization of the CRC tumor microenvironment. These technologies will be essential for determining whether intratumoral bacterial localization—rather than bulk luminal abundance—is the relevant metric for treatment prediction.

#### AI and Machine Learning for Microbiome–Treatment Outcome Prediction

AI/ML approaches are increasingly applied to predict CRC risk and treatment response from microbiome data. Teixeira et al. 2024 reviewed ML methods for cancer microbiome studies, noting that support vector machines (SVMs) have been used to predict CRC survival time, prognosis, and drug responses from microbiome and gene expression data ([Teixeira et al. 2024, npj Precision Oncology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41698-024-00617-7)). Novielli et al. 2024 emphasized the importance of explainable AI for microbiome data, as the high dimensionality and compositional nature of metagenomic data require interpretable models that can identify biologically meaningful features rather than black-box correlations ([Novielli et al. 2024, Frontiers in Microbiology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2024.1348974/full)).

Specific tools are emerging: CRCpred, an AI-ML tool, predicts healthy vs. CRC status from gut microbiome relative abundance profiles ([Pateriya et al. 2025, Computers in Biology and Medicine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010482525009436); post-cutoff). Tsai et al. 2025 developed a gut microbiome-based ML model for early CRC detection at the adenoma stage ([Tsai et al. 2025, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13099-025-00750-z); post-cutoff). Most ambitiously, AI frameworks that integrate microbiome data with host genomics and clinical variables have been developed to predict how gut microbes influence CRC drug response and to identify potentially effective drug combinations ([Inside Precision Medicine, 2026](https://www.insideprecisionmedicine.com/topics/oncology/microbiome-aware-ai-uncovers-new-drug-combinations-for-colorectal-cancer/); post-cutoff). However, no AI/ML model trained on microbiome data has yet been prospectively validated for treatment selection in CRC, and the risk of overfitting given the high feature-to-sample ratio remains a fundamental challenge.

#### Regulatory and Clinical Trial Design Challenges

The regulatory landscape for microbiome therapeutics in oncology is nascent. The FDA established the Live Biotherapeutic Products (LBP) category in 2016, providing a pathway for defined microbial products regulated as biologics rather than foods or supplements. Two LBPs—Rebyota (RBX2660, Ferring) and Vowst (SER-109, Seres Therapeutics)—have received FDA approval, both for prevention of recurrent *C. difficile* infection ([Frontiers in Microbiomes, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiomes/articles/10.3389/frmbi.2024.1441290/full)). No microbiome therapeutic has been approved as an adjunct to cancer treatment.

A 2024 review by the Microbiome Therapeutics Innovation Group (MTIG) identified critical gaps: the FDA's key LBP guidance document dates to 2016 and has not been updated; analytical methods for microbial identification, potency, and bioburden are not standardized; and GMP expectations for manufacturing are incomplete and not harmonized across the FDA and EMA ([Frontiers in Microbiomes, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiomes/articles/10.3389/frmbi.2024.1441290/full)). The FDA recommends at least two complementary methods for microbial identification and active ingredient assessment, but specific methods are not mandated, leading to variability across developers. Probiotics occupy an ambiguous regulatory space—they are generally considered foods or supplements, lack a universally accepted regulatory definition, and require an IND if used to treat, prevent, or cure disease. This ambiguity has led to safety concerns, including FDA warning statements about probiotic use in immunocompromised patients ([Frontiers in Microbiomes, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiomes/articles/10.3389/frmbi.2024.1441290/full)).

Clinical trial design for microbiome interventions faces unique challenges: donor selection and screening for FMT, patient enrollment criteria, antibiotic pretreatment protocols, implantation procedures, and the lack of standardized endpoints. A 2025 Cell review noted that the intrinsic complexity of the gut microbiome makes clinical trial design and reporting particularly challenging, potentially preventing regulatory approval of microbiome therapeutics ([Cell, 2025](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(25)00448-9); post-cutoff). The NCI/NIH convened a Microbiome Intervention Clinical Research Workshop in 2025 to address harmonization, standardization, and collaboration needs ([NCI, 2025](https://events.cancer.gov/archive/nci-microbiome-clinical-research); post-cutoff).

Several ongoing trials are exploring microbiome interventions in CRC: NCT07509398 (FMT combined with first-line therapy), NCT04729322 (FMT and re-introduction of anti-PD-1), NCT05251389 (FMT to convert immunotherapy response), and NCT04038619 (FMT for ICI-induced colitis) ([ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04729322)). However, no completed randomized trial has yet demonstrated that microbiome modulation improves CRC survival or treatment response in a phase 3 setting.

#### Top Three Knowledge Gaps

Three gaps, if resolved, would most accelerate clinical translation:

**1. Causal vs. associative evidence.** The overwhelming majority of microbiome–CRC treatment studies are observational, demonstrating correlations between bacterial abundance and treatment outcomes without establishing causality. Mendelian randomization approaches have begun to explore causal relationships between gut microbiome composition and CRC risk ([Willems et al. 2023, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-31840-0)), and gnotobiotic mouse models with defined microbial communities have provided mechanistic evidence for specific taxa (e.g., Fn-driven chemoresistance via BIRC3). However, the field lacks systematic application of Koch's postulates–equivalent frameworks to establish that specific bacteria cause treatment resistance rather than merely marking it. Resolving this gap requires prospective interventional trials in which microbiome composition is deliberately modified and treatment outcomes are measured against appropriate controls.

**2. Standardization of microbiome analysis methods.** Heterogeneity in study designs, sample collection, DNA extraction, sequencing platforms (16S rRNA vs. shotgun metagenomics), bioinformatic pipelines, and statistical methods severely limits cross-study comparability and meta-analytic synthesis. A 2024 analysis demonstrated that microbiome confounders—including diet, medication use (antibiotics, PPIs), and comorbidities—actively undermine the identification of reliable microbial targets in CRC ([Nature Medicine, 2024](https://www.worldgastroenterology.org/publications/news-you-can-use/microbiome-signatures-for-colorectal-cancer-crc--dealing-with-confounding-variables-to-find-the-real-message)). The NCI/NIH workshop and MTIG initiatives represent initial steps, but field-wide adoption of standardized protocols—including validated reference materials, reporting standards, and quality control benchmarks—is essential before microbiome biomarkers can enter clinical practice.

**3. Predominance of preclinical over clinical evidence.** Nearly all mechanistic insights into microbiota–treatment interactions—including Fn-mediated BIRC3 upregulation, succinic acid/SUCNR1-induced ICI resistance, *Roseburia* butyrate/CD8+ T-cell activation, and engineered bacterial consortia—derive from cell line or mouse models. While these models have been invaluable for hypothesis generation, the complexity of the human gut microbiome, host immune system, and tumor microenvironment cannot be fully recapitulated in mice. The small number of published RCTs of probiotics/synbiotics in CRC surgery ([Moreira et al. 2024, Frontiers in Oncology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1395966/full)) and the early-phase FMT trials in ICI-refractory melanoma represent the vanguard of clinical translation, but no microbiome-targeted intervention has achieved phase 3 success in an oncology indication. Bridging this gap requires sustained investment in well-designed, adequately powered clinical trials with microbiome-specific endpoints, standardized interventions, and rigorous safety monitoring.

In summary, precision microbiome medicine in CRC stands at a juncture where mechanistic understanding has outpaced clinical validation. The path forward demands coordinated advances across biomarker validation, multi-omics integration, engineered therapeutics, spatial biology, AI-driven prediction, and regulatory science—underpinned by a commitment to resolving the causal, standardization, and translational evidence gaps that currently separate bench discoveries from bedside application.
