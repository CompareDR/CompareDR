# Gut Microbiota and Colorectal Cancer Treatment: Evidence Through September 2024

## Scope and key conclusions

This synthesis covers research that was publicly available through September 30, 2024, emphasizing primary human studies and mechanistic experiments that directly inform colorectal or rectal cancer treatment. Studies published online before the cutoff are included even when their print issue appeared later; for example, the rectal-cancer microbiome study by Yang and colleagues was available electronically in July 2024, although its print issue was October 2024. [1]

The literature supports a bidirectional relationship between CRC therapy and the gut microbiota. Surgery, cytotoxic chemotherapy, pelvic irradiation, and combined-modality therapy can reshape the intestinal ecosystem; conversely, particular microbes and microbial functions can alter drug metabolism, epithelial injury and repair, inflammation, antitumor immunity, and ultimately treatment response. The clearest causal examples include microbial β-glucuronidase reactivation of irinotecan metabolites, Fusobacterium nucleatum-driven resistance to 5-FU/oxaliplatin, microbiota-dependent oxaliplatin-induced reactive oxygen species, and bacteria that alter anastomotic healing. [2]

The evidence is nevertheless not equivalent across treatments. For surgery and chemotherapy, there are human CRC trials plus strong mechanistic experiments. For radiotherapy, several prospective rectal-cancer cohorts identify response- and toxicity-associated microbiota, but much of the causal evidence remains experimental. For immunotherapy, compelling microbiome biology exists, yet CRC-specific human datasets were still relatively small by September 2024, especially when compared with melanoma. [3]

A particularly important lesson is that there is no universally “good” or “bad” bacterium. The effect depends on therapeutic context, strain, microbial function, tumor biology, and the surrounding community. F. nucleatum, for example, promotes resistance to 5-FU/oxaliplatin and impaired radiotherapy efficacy in experimental CRC, yet another CRC study found that it can activate STING, increase PD-L1 expression, and make tumors more responsive to PD-L1 blockade. Likewise, Bacteroides vulgatus was associated with less severe diarrhea during rectal nCRT in one small human study but was mechanistically linked to nCRT resistance through microbial nucleotide production in another. [4]

## Cross-cutting mechanisms that explain the table

### Microbial drug metabolism can change toxicity without changing the prescribed dose

Irinotecan is the clearest example of pharmacomicrobiomics in CRC. Host metabolism glucuronidates the active metabolite SN-38 to SN-38G for biliary excretion, but bacterial β-glucuronidases in the intestinal lumen can remove the glucuronide and regenerate SN-38 at the mucosal surface. Wallace and colleagues demonstrated that selectively inhibiting bacterial β-glucuronidases protected mice from irinotecan intestinal toxicity while avoiding wholesale bacterial killing. [13]

This mechanism is important conceptually because it means two patients receiving the same systemic chemotherapy exposure can have different local intestinal exposures depending on microbial enzyme activity. It also argues that the eventual goal need not be broad antibiotics: highly selective inhibitors of a microbial enzyme may preserve beneficial community functions while removing a specific toxic metabolic reaction. [13]

### Microbes can alter tumor-cell stress responses and DNA repair

F. nucleatum represents direct microbe-to-cancer-cell signaling. In CRC, the organism activates a TLR4–MYD88 pathway, alters specific microRNAs, and increases autophagy machinery including ULK1 and ATG7; this allows CRC cells to survive otherwise lethal exposure to oxaliplatin or 5-FU. The human component of the study is particularly important because high tumor F. nucleatum was associated with recurrence after chemotherapy rather than the mechanism being inferred solely from cultured cells. [11]

A conceptually different resistance mechanism emerged for rectal nCRT: B. vulgatus and microbial pathways for nucleotide biosynthesis can increase the pool of nucleosides available to tumor cells, facilitating repair of therapy-induced DNA damage and attenuating response to chemoradiation. [22] This moves the field beyond “which species is present?” toward the potentially more reproducible question of which biochemical capacity is present?

### Microbiota shape treatment-induced immunity

Oxaliplatin is cytotoxic, but its optimal activity is not purely tumor-cell autonomous. Experimental work showed that commensals prime tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells for the reactive-oxygen-species response required for full platinum efficacy; antibiotic-treated and germ-free animals had diminished responses. [10]

Checkpoint blockade adds another layer because microorganisms can alter innate sensing, antigen presentation and T-cell activation. CRC-specific experiments demonstrate microbiome dependence of PD-1 blockade, while F. nucleatum illustrates the context problem exceptionally well: the bacterium that is associated with chemotherapy resistance can activate STING and PD-L1 in a way that creates a vulnerability to PD-L1 blockade. [18]

This is why a simplistic strategy of “eradicate all CRC-associated bacteria” could backfire. The therapeutically relevant variable may be a microbe × drug interaction, not whether a microbe is globally classified as oncogenic or beneficial. [34]

### Barrier repair and microbial virulence are central after surgery

Anastomotic healing provides unusually direct evidence that intestinal bacteria can change a hard clinical endpoint. The CRC patient-to-mouse FMT study by Hajjar et al. showed that microbiota from patients destined to develop an anastomotic leak transmitted a poor-healing phenotype to mice; A. onderdonkii worsened healing whereas P. goldsteinii improved it and reduced inflammatory signaling. [5]

Complementary work identified a specific virulence mechanism: E. faecalis strains at anastomoses can express collagen-degrading activity and activate host MMP-9 through the bacterial genes gelE and sprE, undermining the collagen matrix on which anastomotic integrity depends. [35] The subsequent 111-patient prospective study strengthened the human relevance by finding that leakage was associated with collagenase/protease-producing Enterococcus in anastomotic-margin microbiota and that mucosal samples were more informative than fecal samples. [36]

The latter point has broad implications: stool may not faithfully report the microbiota actually interacting with the tumor, irradiated mucosa or surgical anastomosis. Biomarker studies based only on stool could therefore miss treatment-relevant organisms residing in tissue or mucosal biofilms. [36]

### Microbiota influence treatment toxicity as well as efficacy

The microbiome-response question and the microbiome-toxicity question are partly independent. In the 22-patient rectal nCRT pilot, taxa that distinguished responders from nonresponders were not identical to taxa distinguishing severe from mild/no diarrhea. Responders were enriched for Shuttleworthia, whereas low-toxicity patients showed enrichment of organisms including Bifidobacterium, Butyricicoccus and multiple Bacteroides taxa. [37]

This distinction matters clinically. An “optimal” treatment-associated community would ideally improve the therapeutic ratio—increasing tumor killing while preserving mucosal integrity—rather than simply maximizing one endpoint. The conflicting B. vulgatus observations illustrate the challenge: it was more abundant among patients with mild/no diarrhea in one cohort but was implicated mechanistically in resistance to nCRT in another study. [38]

## Future outlook for microbiome-directed treatment

### Probiotics and prebiotics

By the September 2024 cutoff, probiotics had the most mature CRC-specific evidence as supportive interventions, particularly around surgery and gastrointestinal toxicity, rather than as independent anticancer therapies. In the 100-patient CRC surgery RCT, perioperative probiotics strengthened epithelial-barrier measures and reduced bacterial translocation and infectious complications. [7] In the 73-patient synbiotic trial, preoperative supplementation reduced IL-6 and CRP and was associated with markedly fewer postoperative infections. [8]

The chemotherapy evidence demonstrates why formulation-specific validation is essential. L. rhamnosus GG showed encouraging effects on severe diarrhea and abdominal discomfort during 5-FU-based therapy in earlier CRC work, but a much larger 2023 randomized phase III study found no overall preventive benefit from a BB-12 + LGG combination for irinotecan diarrhea. [39] These are not necessarily contradictory: irinotecan diarrhea has a distinctive β-glucuronidase-mediated mechanism, different products produce different metabolic effects, and simply adding strains does not guarantee suppression of the relevant enzyme. [40]

For prebiotics, the rationale is to feed selected resident organisms or metabolic networks—especially carbohydrate fermentation and SCFA production—rather than introduce live microbes. However, through September 2024, CRC treatment evidence was much less developed for isolated prebiotics than for probiotics or combined synbiotics; much of the human evidence used mixtures in which the independent contribution of the prebiotic component could not be separated. [8]

The most promising evolution is therefore likely to be away from generic over-the-counter “probiotics” toward functionally defined interventions: strains selected for a desired metabolic activity, defined microbial consortia, substrates matched to those organisms, microbial metabolites/postbiotics, or inhibitors of specific microbial enzymes such as β-glucuronidase. The β-glucuronidase work provides particularly strong proof that targeting microbial function can be more precise than changing community composition indiscriminately. [13]

### Fecal microbiota transplantation

FMT is substantially more powerful—and less controlled—than supplementation with one or a few strains because it transfers an ecological community with bacteria, bacteriophages, microbial genes and metabolites. By 2024, proof-of-concept had progressed beyond animal studies into cancer patients. A 2024 study in 13 patients with anti-PD-1-refractory advanced solid tumors found that responder-derived FMT plus renewed PD-1 blockade induced durable microbiota changes and clinical benefit in a subset, with gastrointestinal cancers appearing particularly promising. [29]

Most relevant to CRC, RENMIN-215 tested FMT together with the anti-PD-1 antibody tislelizumab and the VEGFR inhibitor fruquintinib in refractory microsatellite-stable metastatic CRC in an open-label phase II design. The study demonstrated feasibility and antitumor activity, making it an important early indication that microbiome manipulation might help overcome an immunologically resistant CRC phenotype. [30] Because all three components were administered together and there was no randomized FMT-free comparison arm, however, the amount of benefit attributable specifically to FMT remained unresolved. [30]

FMT may also have a toxicity-repair role independent of tumor response. A small clinical study in chronic radiation enteritis reported temporary improvement in intestinal symptoms and mucosal injury after FMT, supporting the hypothesis that restoring ecosystem function after radiation injury could complement conventional supportive care. [31]

Safety is the major counterweight. DeFilipp et al. documented transmission of an extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing E. coli through FMT to immunocompromised recipients; both developed bacteremia and one died. [33] That experience is particularly pertinent to CRC because many candidates for microbiome therapy are receiving chemotherapy or combination systemic therapy and may have compromised mucosal barriers or immune function. It argues for intensive pathogen and antimicrobial-resistance screening, standardized manufacturing, and clinical-trial-level monitoring rather than unsupervised FMT use. [33]

## What was clinically actionable by the cutoff

As of September 2024, the microbiome was best regarded as an important modifier and investigational therapeutic target, not a routine CRC treatment-selection assay. Human studies had demonstrated associations between baseline microbiota and surgical complications, chemoradiation response, treatment diarrhea and immunotherapy response, but no stool microbiome signature had reached sufficiently standardized prospective validation to determine routine choices among surgery, FOLFOX/CAPOX, irinotecan, radiotherapy or checkpoint blockade. [41]

Several implications nevertheless had strong biological support:

Avoid assuming that maximum microbial depletion is desirable. Broad microbiota disruption can remove organisms needed for optimal immune or chemotherapy responses, whereas selective targeting of a harmful organism or enzyme can preserve beneficial functions. The oxaliplatin and irinotecan experiments demonstrate these two sides particularly clearly. [42]

Separate efficacy biomarkers from toxicity biomarkers. In rectal nCRT, the microbial signature associated with pathologic response was different from that associated with diarrhea. A future treatment algorithm may therefore require at least two microbial dimensions: probability of tumor response and probability of toxicity. [37]

Measure function, not only taxonomy. β-glucuronidase activity, nucleotide biosynthesis, SCFA-related pathways, collagenase/protease activity and immune-stimulatory pathways can be more mechanistically informative than genus-level abundance. Different strains of the same species can also possess different virulence or metabolic properties. [43]

Consider the relevant anatomical compartment. Stool, mucosal biopsies, tumor tissue and even oral microbiota can provide different information. The prospective anastomotic-leak study found surgical-margin microbiota more informative than fecal microbiota, while experimental radiotherapy studies implicated migration of oral F. nucleatum into CRC lesions. [44]

Do not infer therapy from a single organism. F. nucleatum is the clearest cautionary example: it can promote fluoropyrimidine/oxaliplatin resistance and radiotherapy resistance yet enhance susceptibility to PD-L1 blockade under particular immune conditions. [45] A clinically useful microbiome test will likely have to integrate microbial genes/functions, tumor genotype and immune phenotype rather than issue a binary result based on one species.

## Evidence gaps and research priorities

The central unresolved question is causality in humans. Small prospective cohorts can identify taxa associated with response or toxicity, but diet, antibiotics, geography, tumor location, prior surgery, bowel preparation, medications and treatment itself all alter microbiota. The 22-patient nCRT study is a good example: it generated biologically plausible response and diarrhea signatures, but its size and associative design preclude treating those organisms as validated causal biomarkers. [37] Mechanistic follow-up such as the B. vulgatus nucleotide study is therefore essential. [22]

A second issue is reproducibility at strain and functional-gene level. Taxonomic labels often hide substantial functional heterogeneity. The anastomotic-leak work showed that what matters about Enterococcus is not merely abundance but possession/expression of collagenase/protease behavior capable of degrading the healing matrix. [46] Similarly, irinotecan toxicity is more directly linked to bacterial β-glucuronidase activity than to the total abundance of any broad taxonomic group. [13]

Third, future trials need to treat the microbiome as a longitudinal variable. A pretreatment stool sample may predict baseline susceptibility, but chemotherapy, radiation, diet changes, antibiotics and surgery can rapidly move the ecosystem into a different state. Response prediction therefore may ultimately require repeated measurements coupled to metabolomics and microbial functional profiling rather than a single baseline 16S profile. The prospective rectal-cancer and perioperative studies already show treatment-related microbial or host–microbial changes across the therapeutic course. [47]

Fourth, microbiome interventions should be tested against explicit mechanistic targets. For irinotecan, this might mean lowering luminal β-glucuronidase activity; for anastomotic healing, suppressing collagenolytic organisms while preserving anti-inflammatory species; for nCRT, reducing nucleotide-producing resistance pathways; and for checkpoint therapy, creating a community that enhances antigen presentation and cytotoxic T-cell function. Each strategy is grounded in a different mechanism and therefore calls for a different intervention rather than a generic goal of “increasing diversity.” [48]

Finally, FMT requires randomized CRC-specific confirmation and much greater standardization. RENMIN-215 and the 2024 anti-PD-1/FMT study established important clinical proof of concept, but combination designs cannot determine whether the transplanted microbiome itself causes the apparent benefit. [32] Future trials need defined donor criteria, metagenomic and metabolomic confirmation of engraftment, pathogen/resistance-gene screening, treatment-specific mechanistic endpoints and an FMT-free control arm. The documented transmission of drug-resistant bacteremia demonstrates that safety must be treated as a core design requirement, particularly in immunocompromised oncology populations. [33]

Taken together, the evidence available by September 2024 supports a shift from viewing the gut microbiota as a passive consequence of CRC treatment to viewing it as a modifiable component of treatment pharmacology, immunity, tissue repair and toxicity. The strongest near-term opportunities are likely to be mechanistically precise—microbial enzyme inhibition, rationally defined strains/consortia, targeted nutritional substrates and carefully controlled FMT—rather than nonspecific attempts to label whole microbiomes as simply “healthy” or “dysbiotic.” [49]
