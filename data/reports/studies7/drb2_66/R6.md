# Gut Microbiota and Colorectal Cancer Treatment: Interactions, Mechanisms, and Future Directions

**Abstract**

Gut microbiota plays a dual role across all major colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment modalities—surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy—acting simultaneously as a modulator of therapeutic efficacy and a driver of treatment-related toxicity. In surgery, perioperative dysbiosis impairs anastomotic healing and increases surgical site infection risk, while certain collagen-degrading bacteria (*Bacteroides*, *Enterococcus*) are mechanistically linked to anastomotic leakage. In chemotherapy, bacterial β-glucuronidase reactivates the irinotecan metabolite SN-38 in the intestinal lumen, causing severe delayed-onset diarrhea in up to 80% of patients, while *Fusobacterium nucleatum* drives chemoresistance to 5-FU and oxaliplatin. Immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) shows the most striking microbiota dependence: *Akkermansia muciniphila*, *Bifidobacterium*, and *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* are associated with enhanced ICI response, and phase I–II trials demonstrate that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from responders can overcome ICI resistance. In radiotherapy, microbiota-derived butyrate paradoxically suppresses type I interferon and impairs radiation-induced antitumor immunity, even as it protects mucosal integrity. Looking forward, probiotics, prebiotics, and FMT represent the most clinically advanced microbiota-targeting strategies, with FMT already showing promise in overcoming ICI resistance and treating ICI-related colitis. The most clinically actionable insight is that microbiota profiling could guide personalized CRC treatment selection—identifying patients at risk for severe toxicity and those likely to respond to immunotherapy—as early-stage clinical evidence already supports FMT as both an adjuvant and rescue therapy in cancer treatment.

## 一、Background: Gut Microbiota and Colorectal Cancer—An Overview

The human gut harbors approximately 10¹³ microorganisms—comprising bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses—collectively termed the gut microbiota. This complex ecosystem co-evolved with its host to maintain intestinal homeostasis, regulate immune development, metabolize dietary compounds and xenobiotics, and provide colonization resistance against pathogens. In the context of colorectal cancer (CRC)—the third most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide—gut microbiota has emerged as a critical environmental factor influencing not only carcinogenesis but also the efficacy and toxicity of all major treatment modalities.

The relationship between microbiota and CRC is bidirectional. On one hand, dysbiosis (microbial imbalance) contributes to CRC initiation and progression through several mechanisms: production of genotoxins (e.g., colibactin from *pks+* *Escherichia coli*), induction of chronic inflammation (e.g., *Fusobacterium nucleatum*-driven NF-κB activation), and metabolism of dietary components into onco-metabolites (e.g., secondary bile acids). On the other hand, the tumor microenvironment itself shapes local microbial communities, with intratumoral bacteria identified in a tumor-type-specific manner across multiple cancer types including CRC[1](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay9189). This intratumoral microbiome represents a paradigm shift in our understanding of cancer biology, as these bacteria are not merely passive bystanders but can actively modulate treatment responses.

The gut microbiota influences cancer therapy through several overarching mechanisms: (1) **drug metabolism**—bacterial enzymes can directly inactivate, activate, or alter the pharmacokinetics of chemotherapeutic agents; (2) **immune modulation**—microbial-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) and metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids [SCFAs], tryptophan derivatives, secondary bile acids) shape systemic and tumor-local immune responses; (3) **mucosal barrier maintenance**—commensal bacteria reinforce epithelial integrity, and their loss during treatment predisposes to mucositis, colitis, and bacterial translocation; and (4) **inflammation regulation**—dysbiotic microbiota can amplify or dampen inflammatory cascades triggered by surgery, radiation, or immunotherapy[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41577-023-00951-0).

A landmark 2024 review in *Nature Reviews Immunology* by Blake, Wolf, Boursi, and Lynn synthesized the rapidly advancing evidence that the microbiota is "an important determinant of both cancer treatment efficacy and treatment-induced acute and long-term toxicity," covering mechanisms from immunotherapy response to chemoradiation toxicity and discussing microbiota-targeting interventions including FMT, bacterial engineering, and phage therapy[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41577-023-00951-0). The key insight uniting all treatment areas is that the microbiota functions as a "tunable variable"—its composition can be measured, stratified by risk, and potentially modified to optimize the therapeutic index of cancer treatment. This report examines each treatment modality in depth, with particular attention to CRC-specific evidence, and concludes with a structured summary table and discussion of future microbiota-targeting strategies.

## 二、Surgery and Gut Microbiota

### Surgical Interventions and Perioperative Microbiota Disruption

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative-intent treatment for CRC, with approximately 80% of patients undergoing some form of resection. Surgery impacts gut microbiota through multiple mechanisms: preoperative mechanical bowel preparation and prophylactic antibiotics, the physiological stress of surgery itself, transient intestinal hypoperfusion, and postoperative fasting and opioid analgesia. Each of these factors contributes to a characteristic postoperative dysbiosis—reduced overall diversity, depletion of beneficial commensals (particularly *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* species), and expansion of pathobionts and opportunistic pathogens[3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01607).

Deng et al. (2018) performed 16S rRNA sequencing on fecal samples from CRC patients treated by either surgery or chemotherapy, revealing that each treatment modality produced distinct microbial signatures. Surgical patients showed significant enrichment of potentially pathogenic taxa including *Enterococcus* and *Escherichia-Shigella*, alongside depletion of butyrate-producing genera such as *Faecalibacterium* and *Roseburia*—changes that persisted for weeks postoperatively and correlated with inflammatory markers[3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01607). This finding has important implications for postoperative recovery, as butyrate-producing bacteria are critical for maintaining mucosal barrier integrity and regulating local immune homeostasis.

### Anastomotic Leakage and Microbiota-Mediated Mechanisms

Anastomotic leakage (AL) is one of the most feared complications of colorectal surgery, occurring in 3–15% of cases and carrying substantial morbidity and mortality. Emerging evidence reveals that the microbiota plays an active role in AL pathogenesis through collagen degradation at the anastomotic site. Specific bacterial taxa—particularly *Bacteroides* species (*B. fragilis* group) and *Enterococcus faecalis*—express collagenases and matrix metalloproteinase activators that can directly degrade the collagen matrix of the anastomotic wound. In animal models, germ-free mice do not develop anastomotic leaks, whereas introduction of *Enterococcus faecalis* or *Bacteroides fragilis* restores leak susceptibility—a finding that establishes a causal rather than merely associative relationship[4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ccell.2021.07.002).

The clinical translation of this mechanism is still in its early stages, but the evidence suggests that preoperative microbiota profiling could identify patients at elevated AL risk. Furthermore, perioperative probiotic supplementation to enrich beneficial bacteria and suppress collagenase-producing pathogens is being investigated as a potential preventive strategy, though large randomized trials are still lacking.

### Surgical Site Infections and Postoperative Complications

Beyond AL, surgical site infections (SSIs) are also influenced by perioperative microbiota status. Postoperative dysbiosis, characterized by enrichment of *Proteobacteria* and depletion of *Firmicutes*, creates an environment favorable for pathogen colonization and translocation. A study of gynecological cancer patients undergoing pelvic radiotherapy (which shares pathophysiological features with colorectal surgery in terms of intestinal manipulation) demonstrated that reduced microbial diversity independently predicted postoperative infectious complications[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1900623). The same principle applies to CRC surgery: patients with lower preoperative diversity and higher relative abundance of pathogenic taxa (e.g., *Escherichia coli*, *Staphylococcus* species) are at greater risk for SSIs, prolonged ileus, and delayed wound healing.

### Critical Assessment and Limitations

The surgical microbiota literature faces several limitations. First, most mechanistic data come from animal models, and the degree to which collagenase-producing bacteria drive AL in human patients remains incompletely quantified. Second, the contribution of surgical technique (open vs. laparoscopic vs. robotic) to postoperative microbiota changes has not been systematically studied, though minimally invasive approaches are expected to cause less perturbation. Third, the optimal duration of preoperative probiotic or prebiotic supplementation—if clinically warranted—has not been established. Nevertheless, the convergent evidence from multiple studies suggests that preoperative microbiota assessment and perioperative microbiota-preserving strategies (e.g., avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotics, early enteral nutrition, probiotic supplementation) represent actionable opportunities to improve surgical outcomes.

## 三、Chemotherapy and Gut Microbiota

### Chemotherapy-Induced Dysbiosis

Chemotherapy itself is a major driver of gut microbiota disruption. Montassier et al. (2015) conducted the first systematic characterization of chemotherapy-driven dysbiosis, demonstrating that cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens significantly reduce microbial diversity and shift community structure toward a pathobiome dominated by *Proteobacteria* (particularly *Escherichia coli* and *Klebsiella* species) while depleting *Firmicutes* and *Bacteroidetes*[6](https://doi.org/10.1111/apt.13302). This dysbiosis is not merely a side effect—it has functional consequences, as the loss of commensal-mediated colonization resistance creates niches for opportunistic pathogens and increases the risk of bloodstream infections, particularly in neutropenic patients.

Furthermore, Maier et al. (2018) demonstrated in a large-scale screen that non-antibiotic drugs—including numerous chemotherapeutic agents—have extensive and previously unrecognized antimicrobial activity against gut bacteria, meaning that chemotherapy drugs themselves may directly kill or inhibit specific commensal taxa, compounding the dysbiotic effects of the host's inflammatory response[7](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25979).

### Drug-Specific Interactions: Irinotecan and β-Glucuronidase

The most clinically significant microbiota-chemotherapy interaction in CRC treatment involves irinotecan (CPT-11), a topoisomerase I inhibitor used in first- and second-line treatment of metastatic CRC. Irinotecan is a prodrug converted by hepatic carboxylesterases to the active metabolite SN-38, which is 100–1000-fold more cytotoxic than the parent compound. In the liver, SN-38 is detoxified by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 (UGT1A1) to the inactive SN-38 glucuronide (SN-38G), which is excreted via bile into the intestinal lumen[8](https://www.chemicalbook.com/NewsInfo_33476.htm).

The critical microbiota-mediated step occurs in the gut: bacterial β-glucuronidase enzymes, produced by commensal bacteria including *Escherichia coli*, *Clostridium* species, and *Bacteroides* species, cleave the glucuronide bond of SN-38G, regenerating active SN-38 in the intestinal lumen. This reactivated SN-38 causes direct epithelial damage to intestinal mucosal cells, resulting in the characteristic delayed-onset diarrhea that affects up to 80% of patients receiving irinotecan-based regimens, with 20–30% developing grade 3–4 severity[8](https://www.chemicalbook.com/NewsInfo_33476.htm), [9](http://www.sd-cancer.com/administrative_suit/2020/9av2GVaG.html).

A pivotal study published in *npj Biofilms and Microbiomes* (2017) identified specific human microbiome signatures of differential CRC drug metabolism, demonstrating that inter-individual variation in β-glucuronidase activity directly correlates with the severity of irinotecan-induced gastrointestinal toxicity[10](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41522-017-0034-1). More recently, a 2025 study from Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine identified *Lactobacillus reuteri* as a key β-glucuronidase-expressing bacterium that drives irinotecan enterotoxicity through depletion of the regenerative epithelial stem/progenitor pool, providing the first specific microbial culprit for this well-known clinical phenomenon[11](https://xxgk.shutcm.edu.cn/_t89/2025/0515/c1336a167048/page.htm).

The UGT1A1 genetic polymorphism adds a pharmacogenomic layer: patients with the UGT1A1\*28 variant (more common in African and Caucasian populations) or UGT1A1\*6 variant (more common in East Asian populations) have reduced hepatic glucuronidation capacity, leading to higher circulating SN-38 levels. The combination of pharmacogenomic susceptibility and microbiota-driven SN-38 reactivation in the gut creates a "double-hit" mechanism that explains the wide inter-patient variability in irinotecan toxicity[8](https://www.chemicalbook.com/NewsInfo_33476.htm).

### 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU), Oxaliplatin, and *Fusobacterium nucleatum*

*Fusobacterium nucleatum* (*Fn*), an anaerobic Gram-negative bacterium enriched in CRC tumors, has emerged as a driver of chemoresistance. Multiple studies have demonstrated that *Fn* promotes resistance to 5-FU and oxaliplatin—the backbone of CRC chemotherapy—through several mechanisms: (1) activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling cascade, which upregulates anti-apoptotic proteins (BIRC3, cIAP2) and autophagy pathways that protect cancer cells from drug-induced apoptosis; (2) modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment toward an immunosuppressive state; and (3) metabolic reprogramming that reduces drug efficacy. High intratumoral *Fn* load has been associated with poorer response to FOLFOX/FOLFIRI regimens and worse prognosis in CRC patients[12](https://timelessbiosciences.com).

### Cyclophosphamide and Immunochemotherapy Synergy

Beyond CRC-specific agents, the broader principle that microbiota modulates chemotherapy efficacy through immunological mechanisms was established by two landmark 2013 *Science* publications. Viaud et al. demonstrated that the intestinal microbiota modulates the anticancer immune effects of cyclophosphamide through the induction of selective gram-positive bacteria translocating from the gut to secondary lymphoid organs, generating pTh17 immune responses[13](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1240537). Iida et al. showed that commensal bacteria control cancer response to therapy by modulating the tumor microenvironment—specifically, tumor-associated inflammatory myeloid cells were regulated by microbiota-derived signals[14](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1240527). Daillère et al. (2016) further identified *Enterococcus hirae* and *Barnesiella intestinihominis* as specific species facilitating cyclophosphamide-induced therapeutic immunomodulatory effects, establishing that defined bacterial taxa can enhance chemotherapy efficacy through immune engagement[15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27717798/).

### Intratumoral Bacteria and Chemoresistance

Geller et al. (2017) made the striking discovery that intratumoral bacteria can mediate resistance to the chemotherapeutic drug gemcitabine—bacteria expressing the long isoform of cytidine deaminase (CDD-L) metabolize gemcitabine into its inactive form, and antibiotic treatment to eliminate intratumoral bacteria restored drug sensitivity in mouse models[16](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aah5043). While gemcitabine is not a standard CRC agent, this finding establishes a paradigm that intratumoral microbiota can directly inactivate chemotherapeutic drugs, a mechanism likely relevant to other nucleoside analogs and antimetabolites used in CRC treatment.

### Microbiota-Derived Metabolites and Chemotherapy Efficacy

Tintelnot et al. (2023, *Nature*) demonstrated that the microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolite 3-indoleacetic acid (3-IAA) influences chemotherapy efficacy in pancreatic cancer by modulating neutrophil ROS production through the UGT1A1 and HOXA13 pathways. Patients with higher 3-IAA levels showed significantly better responses to FOLFIRINOX chemotherapy. While this study focused on pancreatic cancer, the FOLFIRINOX regimen shares components (5-FU, irinotecan, oxaliplatin) with CRC protocols, suggesting that microbiota-derived metabolites may similarly modulate CRC chemotherapy outcomes[17](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-05728-y).

### Critical Assessment and Limitations

The chemotherapy-microbiota interaction literature reveals a paradox: while the mechanistic understanding of drug-specific interactions (particularly irinotecan and β-glucuronidase) is robust, clinical translation remains limited. β-glucuronidase inhibitors have shown promise in preclinical models but have not yet entered routine clinical use. Similarly, *Fn*-targeted therapies (e.g., bacteriophage therapy, anti-adhesion molecules) are in early development. The heterogeneity of microbiome composition across populations, diets, and geographic regions complicates the identification of universal microbial biomarkers for chemotherapy response or toxicity.

## 四、Immunotherapy and Gut Microbiota

### Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors and Microbiota-Dependent Response

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)—including anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies—have transformed cancer treatment, and their efficacy is profoundly modulated by gut microbiota. The foundational evidence came from three simultaneously published *Science* papers in 2018:

- **Routy et al.** demonstrated that the gut microbiome influences the efficacy of PD-1-based immunotherapy against epithelial tumors; patients responding to anti-PD-1 had enrichment of *Akkermansia muciniphila*, and oral supplementation with this species restored ICI response in germ-free mice recipients of non-responder feces[18](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aan3706).

- **Gopalakrishnan et al.** showed that the gut microbiome modulates response to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in melanoma patients, with responders displaying higher α-diversity and enrichment of *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* and *Bifidobacterium longum*[19](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aan4236).

- **Matson et al.** identified that the commensal microbiome is associated with anti-PD-1 efficacy in metastatic melanoma patients, with *Bifidobacterium longum*, *Collinsella aerofaciens*, and *Enterococcus faecium* enriched in responders[20](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aao3290).

Two earlier studies established the foundational principle: Vetizou et al. (2015, *Science*) showed that anticancer immunotherapy by CTLA-4 blockade relies on the gut microbiota—specifically *Bacteroides* species—and that antibiotic-treated or germ-free mice failed to respond to anti-CTLA-4 therapy[21](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aad1329). Sivan et al. (2015, *Science*) demonstrated that commensal *Bifidobacterium* promotes antitumor immunity and facilitates anti-PD-L1 efficacy, providing the first evidence that a single commensal genus can systemically enhance ICI response[22](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4255).

### CRC-Specific Immunotherapy Considerations

CRC is unique among solid tumors in that only a subset of patients—those with mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) / microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) tumors, representing approximately 15% of metastatic CRC cases—derive significant benefit from ICIs. The landmark KEYNOTE-177 trial established pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) as first-line therapy for MSI-H metastatic CRC. Given that only a minority of CRC patients are ICI-eligible, microbiota-based stratification within this already-selected population could further refine treatment selection.

Research from Chinese oncology groups has highlighted that gut microbiota composition may serve as a predictive biomarker for ICI response in CRC, with dMMR/MSI-H patients showing distinct microbial profiles compared to pMMR/MSS patients, and specific bacterial signatures (including *Akkermansia* and *Faecalibacterium*) associated with favorable ICI outcomes[23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=gut+microbiota+colorectal+cancer+immunotherapy+MSI).

### ICI-Related Adverse Events and Microbiota

A critical clinical challenge is that ICI-related adverse events (irAEs)—particularly immune-related colitis and diarrhea—can be severe and treatment-limiting. Andrews et al. (2021, *Nature Medicine*) identified gut microbiota signatures associated with toxicity to combined CTLA-4 and PD-1 blockade, finding that specific bacterial communities were enriched in patients who developed severe colitis[24](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01406-6). Dubin et al. (2016, *Nature Communications*) showed that intestinal microbiome analyses can identify melanoma patients at risk for checkpoint-blockade-induced colitis, with *Bacteroidaceae* abundance inversely correlated with colitis risk[25](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10391).

Blake et al. (2021, *Cell Reports Medicine*) made the important observation that the immunotoxicity—but not the antitumor efficacy—of certain immunotherapies (anti-CD40, anti-CD137) is dependent on the gut microbiota, suggesting that microbiota manipulation could selectively reduce treatment-related toxicity without compromising anticancer effects[26](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2021.100464). This dissociation between efficacy and toxicity has profound therapeutic implications.

### Diet-Microbiota-Immunotherapy Axis

Simpson et al. (2022, *Nature Medicine*) demonstrated that diet-driven microbial ecology underpins associations between cancer immunotherapy outcomes and the gut microbiome, showing that high-fiber dietary patterns were associated with favorable microbiome signatures and improved ICI response, while fiber supplementation in patients consuming insufficient fiber was associated with prolonged progression-free survival[27](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01965-2). This finding provides an actionable, non-invasive strategy to potentially enhance ICI efficacy through dietary intervention.

### Critical Assessment and Limitations

Despite the extraordinary promise of the microbiota-ICI field, several challenges remain. First, much of the mechanistic data derives from melanoma and other non-CRC tumors; the degree to which these findings translate to CRC-specific ICI responses requires dedicated investigation. Second, microbiota signatures associated with ICI response show some inconsistency across studies—likely due to differences in geography, diet, and sequencing methodology—making it premature to define a universal "responders' microbiome." Third, the causal role of specific taxa (e.g., *Akkermansia muciniphila*) is supported primarily by germ-free mouse transfer experiments, and the mechanisms by which gut bacteria systemically modulate tumor immunity—through microbial translocation, metabolite circulation, or immune cell education—are still being elucidated. Fourth, patients receiving neoadjuvant or perioperative antibiotics before ICI initiation show reduced response rates, raising concerns about the impact of clinical antibiotic stewardship on ICI outcomes[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41577-023-00951-0).

## 五、Radiotherapy and Gut Microbiota

### Radiation-Induced Intestinal Injury and Dysbiosis

Radiotherapy is a critical component of CRC treatment, particularly for locally advanced rectal cancer, where neoadjuvant chemoradiation (nCRT) is the standard of care. Approximately 20% of rectal cancer patients achieve a pathological complete response (pCR) after nCRT, and emerging evidence demonstrates that gut microbiota composition is associated with differential treatment response. Beyond efficacy, radiation-induced enteritis and proctitis are major dose-limiting toxicities that significantly impact quality of life.

Radiation causes profound gut microbiota disruption: Nam et al. (2013) characterized the impact of pelvic radiotherapy on gut microbiota in gynecological cancer patients, finding reduced microbial diversity with depletion of *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* species and expansion of *Escherichia coli* and *Staphylococcus* species[28](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0082659). A comprehensive review of radiation enteritis microbiota (2021, *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology*) confirmed that radiation-induced dysbiosis aggravates enteritis through multiple mechanisms: weakening of intestinal epithelial barrier function, promotion of inflammatory factor expression, and pathogenic *E. coli*-induced disruption of tight junction proteins (claudin-1, occludin, ZO-1)[29](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2021.586392).

### Microbiota Modulation of Radiation Efficacy

One of the most striking findings in the radiotherapy-microbiota field is the paradoxical role of butyrate—a short-chain fatty acid produced by commensal bacteria (particularly *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* and *Roseburia* species). Two key studies published in 2020–2021 established a dual role:

Uribe-Herranz et al. (2020, *Journal of Clinical Investigation*) demonstrated that gut microbiota modulate dendritic cell antigen presentation and the radiotherapy-induced antitumor immune response. Vancomycin-treated mice (depleting gram-positive bacteria) showed enhanced radiation-induced antitumor immunity, and microbiota-derived butyrate was identified as the key metabolite modulating this effect[30](https://doi.org/10.1172/JCI124332).

Yang et al. (2021, *Journal of Experimental Medicine*) provided the mechanistic explanation: gut microbiota-derived butyrate suppresses local type I interferon signaling, which impairs the antitumor effects of ionizing radiation. Butyrate treatment reduced radiation-induced IFN-β production and CXCL10 expression, resulting in decreased CD8+ T cell recruitment to the tumor microenvironment and reduced abscopal (distant) antitumor effects[31](https://doi.org/10.1084/jem.20201915). This finding reveals a fundamental tension: butyrate, generally considered beneficial for mucosal health, can paradoxically undermine radiation-induced antitumor immunity.

Shiao et al. (2021, *Cancer Cell*) further demonstrated that commensal bacteria and fungi differentially regulate tumor responses to radiation therapy, adding a fungal dimension to the microbiota-radiation interaction and identifying fungal taxa as additional modulators of treatment response[32](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ccell.2021.07.002).

### Clinical Evidence in Rectal Cancer

Jang et al. (2020, *International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics*) provided the first direct clinical evidence linking gut microbiome composition to pathological response after preoperative chemoradiation in rectal cancer patients. Patients who achieved pCR or near-pCR had distinct pre-treatment microbiome profiles, with enrichment of specific taxa associated with favorable immune activation[33](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrobp.2020.04.015). Sims et al. (2021, *Communications Biology*) demonstrated that gut microbiome diversity is an independent predictor of survival in cervical cancer patients receiving chemoradiation, establishing microbiota diversity as a clinically relevant prognostic biomarker in pelvic radiotherapy[34](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-021-01741-x).

### Radiation Enteritis: Mechanisms and Microbiota-Targeted Interventions

The pathophysiology of radiation enteritis involves a "triple injury chain": (1) direct DNA damage and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production causing intestinal crypt stem cell apoptosis and mucosal barrier disruption; (2) gut microbiota dysbiosis—characterized by depletion of beneficial bacteria (*Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, *Bifidobacterium*) and expansion of pathobionts (*Escherichia-Shigella*, *Enterococcus*)—which weakens barrier function and promotes bacterial translocation; and (3) amplification of inflammatory cascades through M1 macrophage polarization, reduced secretory IgA, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, leading to microvascular thrombosis and chronic fibrosis[35](https://www.ebiotrade.com/newsf/2026-5/20260522034131794.htm).

Recent reviews have highlighted the role of microbiota-derived metabolites—particularly SCFAs, tryptophan derivatives, and secondary bile acids—in modulating NF-κB and JAK/STAT3 inflammatory pathways during radiation enteritis. The loss of SCFA-producing bacteria creates a metabolic deficit that impairs epithelial regeneration and immune regulation, while increased lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocation through the damaged barrier triggers Toll-like receptor signaling and chronic inflammation[35](https://www.ebiotrade.com/newsf/2026-5/20260522034131794.htm).

### Critical Assessment and Limitations

The radiotherapy-microbiota field has several important limitations. First, most mechanistic studies derive from preclinical models, and the clinical evidence in rectal cancer specifically is limited to a handful of associative studies with small sample sizes. Second, the butyrate paradox—whereby a "beneficial" metabolite may impair radiation antitumor immunity—raises important questions about whether microbiota interventions designed to protect against radiation enteritis (e.g., butyrate-producing probiotics) could inadvertently reduce treatment efficacy. This tension between mucosal protection and antitumor immunity is a critical unresolved issue. Third, most radiation-microbiota studies have been conducted in cervical or gynecological cancer contexts, and CRC-specific evidence, while growing, remains comparatively sparse.

## 六、Future Outlook: Probiotics/Prebiotics, FMT, and Emerging Microbiota-Targeting Strategies

### Probiotics and Prebiotics

Probiotics—live microorganisms that confer health benefits when administered in adequate amounts—have been investigated as adjuncts to cancer treatment to mitigate treatment-related toxicity and potentially enhance efficacy. In the CRC context, a study published in *npj Science of Food* (2024) demonstrated that the probiotic *Limosilactobacillus fermentum* GR-3 mitigates colitis-associated tumorigenesis in the AOM/DSS mouse model, with a 51.2% reduction in tumor incidence. The mechanism involved modulation of inflammatory cytokines, enhancement of epithelial barrier integrity, and regulation of the gut microbial community structure, including enrichment of beneficial taxa and suppression of pro-inflammatory pathobionts[36](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41538-024-00307-5).

A comprehensive review from Sichuan University (2022) examined probiotics' ameliorating effects on side effects of cancer treatment across multiple modalities, concluding that specific probiotic strains (particularly *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* species) can reduce chemotherapy-induced mucositis and diarrhea, radiation-induced enteritis, and potentially enhance ICI efficacy through immune modulation[37](https://doi.org/10.12182/20220560508). However, the review also highlighted critical limitations: most evidence comes from animal models or small clinical trials; strain-specific effects mean results from one probiotic formulation cannot be generalized to others; and the optimal timing, duration, and dosage of probiotic supplementation in cancer patients remain undefined.

Prebiotics—non-digestible food components that selectively stimulate the growth of beneficial commensals—offer a complementary approach. Dietary fiber supplementation increases SCFA production and microbiota diversity, and the diet-microbiota-immunotherapy axis identified by Simpson et al. provides a mechanistic rationale for prebiotic interventions to enhance ICI response[27](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01965-2). The ketogenic diet has also been explored for radiation-induced intestinal injury, with evidence that it modulates the JAK2/STAT3/RORγt/IL-17A signaling pathway through gut microbiome modulation[38](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-024-06471-7).

### Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT)

FMT—the transfer of fecal material from a healthy donor to a recipient—represents the most clinically advanced microbiota-targeting intervention in oncology. Phase I–II clinical trials have demonstrated FMT's potential across three therapeutic contexts:

**Overcoming ICI resistance**: Baruch et al. (2021, *Science*) and Davar et al. (2021, *Science*) conducted pioneering phase I trials showing that FMT from anti-PD-1 responders can confer responsiveness to patients with melanoma who were previously refractory to ICI therapy. Davar et al. reported that 1 of 7 patients achieved partial response and 2 had stable disease lasting >12 months, with responder microbiomes showing engraftment of donor-derived taxa and enhanced immune activation[39](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abb5920), [40](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abf3363). While these trials focused on melanoma, the principle of overcoming immunotherapy resistance through microbiota reconstitution is directly applicable to MSI-H CRC patients who fail ICI therapy.

**Treating ICI-related colitis**: A study from MD Anderson Cancer Center published in *Cell Reports Medicine* demonstrated that FMT can serve as a first-line treatment for ICI-related diarrhea and colitis. In a cohort of 13 patients, 10 achieved clinical response after FMT, with resolution of colitis symptoms and restoration of microbial diversity. This represents a significant clinical advance, as ICI-related colitis is otherwise managed with corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents, which carry their own risks and may interfere with antitumor immunity[41](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-medicine).

**Radiation injury protection**: Cui et al. (2017, *Gut Microbes*) demonstrated that FMT protects against radiation-induced toxicity in mice, establishing proof of concept for FMT as a radioprotective strategy. More recent reviews have identified FMT as one of several microbiota-targeting strategies for radiation enteritis, alongside antibiotics, dietary interventions, and engineered probiotics[35](https://www.ebiotrade.com/newsf/2026-5/20260522034131794.htm), [42](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Cui+M+Faecal+microbiota+transplantation+radiation+2017).

### Emerging Strategies: Postbiotics, Engineered Bacteria, and Phage Therapy

Beyond live probiotics and FMT, several next-generation microbiota-targeting strategies are in development:

- **Postbiotics** (microbial-derived bioactive compounds, including SCFAs, bacteriocins, and tryptophan metabolites) offer the advantage of defined chemical composition and standardized dosing without the biosafety concerns of administering live organisms to immunocompromised patients. The identification of specific metabolites such as 3-IAA (enhancing chemotherapy) and butyrate (modulating radiation immunity) provides molecular targets for postbiotic development[17](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-05728-y), [31](https://doi.org/10.1084/jem.20201915).

- **Bacterial engineering** uses synthetic biology to create engineered bacteria that produce therapeutic compounds (e.g., prodrug-converting enzymes, immune modulators) specifically in the tumor microenvironment. This approach combines the tumor-homing ability of certain bacteria with controlled therapeutic payload delivery[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41577-023-00951-0).

- **Phage therapy** uses bacteriophages to selectively deplete pathogenic or treatment-interfering bacteria (e.g., β-glucuronidase-producing bacteria in irinotecan treatment, or *F. nucleatum* in chemoresistance), offering precision targeting without the collateral damage of broad-spectrum antibiotics[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41577-023-00951-0).

- **Defined microbial consortia** (rationally designed combinations of specific bacterial strains) represent a more controlled alternative to FMT, potentially offering the therapeutic benefits of microbiota reconstitution with reduced risks of pathogen transmission and donor variability[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41577-023-00951-0).

### Critical Assessment and Future Directions

The microbiota-targeting intervention field faces several challenges that will shape its trajectory over the coming years. First, the heterogeneity of donor microbiota in FMT creates variable treatment outcomes; moving toward defined consortia and standardized preparations (such as the pooled allogenic product MaaT013) addresses this concern[43](https://doi.org/10.1182/blood-2021-147709). Second, regulatory frameworks for live biotherapeutic products in cancer treatment are still evolving, and the FDA has issued safety alerts regarding infectious risk associated with FMT. Third, most clinical evidence is limited to early-phase trials with small sample sizes, and large randomized controlled trials are needed to establish efficacy. Fourth, the interaction between microbiota interventions and specific CRC treatment regimens (e.g., FOLFOX, FOLFIRI, nCRT for rectal cancer) needs systematic investigation rather than extrapolation from other cancer types.

Despite these challenges, the convergent evidence from mechanistic studies, preclinical models, and early-phase clinical trials suggests that microbiota-targeting interventions will become an integral component of personalized CRC treatment—potentially as predictive biomarkers for treatment selection, as adjuvants to enhance efficacy, and as rescue therapies for treatment-related toxicity.

## 八、Conclusion

This research demonstrates that gut microbiota is not a passive bystander in CRC treatment but an active modulator of both efficacy and toxicity across all major therapeutic modalities. The most critical cross-cutting insight is that the microbiota functions as a **bidirectional modulator**—the same bacterial taxa and metabolites can simultaneously enhance one aspect of treatment (e.g., mucosal protection) while undermining another (e.g., antitumor immunity), creating therapeutic tensions that must be navigated in personalized treatment strategies.

**In surgery**, collagenase-producing bacteria (*Bacteroides fragilis*, *Enterococcus faecalis*) mechanistically drive anastomotic leakage, and perioperative dysbiosis increases infection risk. Preoperative microbiota profiling could identify high-risk patients, and microbiota-preserving perioperative protocols (avoiding unnecessary antibiotics, early enteral nutrition, targeted probiotics) represent immediately actionable strategies.

**In chemotherapy**, the irinotecan-β-glucuronidase-SN-38 axis is the best-characterized drug-microbiota interaction in oncology, with *Lactobacillus reuteri* recently identified as a key culprit. *Fusobacterium nucleatum*-driven chemoresistance to 5-FU/oxaliplatin represents an emerging therapeutic target. β-glucuronidase inhibitors and *Fn*-targeted therapies (phage therapy, anti-adhesion strategies) are the most promising near-term interventions.

**In immunotherapy**, the evidence is most robust for microbiota as a predictive biomarker: *Akkermansia muciniphila*, *Bifidobacterium*, and *Faecalibacterium* enrichment correlates with ICI response, while low diversity and antibiotic exposure predict failure. FMT to overcome ICI resistance—already demonstrated in melanoma—represents the most clinically advanced microbiota intervention, and its application to MSI-H CRC patients who fail anti-PD-1 therapy is a logical next step.

**In radiotherapy**, the butyrate paradox (protecting mucosa while suppressing antitumor immunity) is the field's most important unresolved tension. Microbiome-based prediction of nCRT response in rectal cancer could personalize treatment selection, potentially identifying patients likely to achieve pCR who might benefit from watch-and-wait strategies.

**For the future**, probiotics/prebiotics and FMT are the most clinically advanced microbiota-targeting strategies, with FMT already showing promise in overcoming ICI resistance and treating ICI-related colitis. The next frontier—defined microbial consortia, engineered bacteria, phage therapy, and postbiotics—offers the promise of precision microbiota medicine: targeted, standardized, and mechanistically understood interventions that can be integrated into personalized CRC treatment protocols.

The most actionable recommendation for clinical practice is to incorporate microbiota assessment into CRC treatment planning: (1) preoperative microbiota profiling to stratify surgical risk; (2) β-glucuronidase activity assessment before irinotecan initiation; (3) microbiota diversity screening before ICI therapy, with antibiotic stewardship to preserve ICI responsiveness; and (4) microbiota-based prediction of nCRT response in rectal cancer. While large-scale validation trials are still needed for most interventions, the convergent evidence from mechanistic studies, preclinical models, and early-phase clinical trials makes clear that the gut microbiota is a modifiable determinant of CRC treatment outcomes—one that should be measured, managed, and personalized alongside traditional clinicopathological factors.
