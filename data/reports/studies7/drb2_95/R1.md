# Hydroforming Technology: Material Selection and Common Manufacturing Defects

Per your constraint, the specified Chinchanikar et al. review and the listed URLs were excluded from the evidence base; none of the analysis below relies on or quotes that source.

This report treats tube hydroforming (THF) as the main reference process because it dominates the automotive examples, while also drawing on sheet/fluid-cell and hydromechanical hydroforming where they provide useful evidence on springback, surface quality, and difficult-to-form alloys. In tube hydroforming, the most important coupled variables are internal pressure, axial end feeding, friction/lubrication, preform geometry, and—for low-formability alloys—temperature. Experimental work on DP600 tubes, for example, shows that end feeding can delay burst/fracture and materially increase corner-filling capability. [1]

## Materials commonly used in hydroforming

### High-strength steel

In automotive hydroforming literature, the most relevant subset of “high-strength steel” is advanced high-strength steel (AHSS), particularly dual-phase grades such as DP600, DP800, DP980/DP1000 and, in some studies, TRIP steels. Dual-phase steel combines a relatively ductile ferritic matrix with martensitic islands, giving it a high initial work-hardening rate and a favorable strength–ductility combination compared with conventional steels at a similar yield-strength level. [2]

| Aspect | Analysis |
| --- | --- |
| Material characteristics | HSS/AHSS offers high tensile strength and good structural efficiency, making thin-walled hydroformed sections particularly attractive for crash-load paths. DP steels retain useful strain hardening even at high strength levels, which helps distribute deformation before localized necking. [2] |
| Forming challenges | The major trade-off is the reduction in available formability as strength rises. Hydroforming therefore has a relatively narrow permissible window between too little pressure/too much end feed, which promotes buckling or wrinkling, and too much pressure/too little material feed, which promotes thinning and burst. Experiments on DP600 show that properly controlled end feeding delays fracture and permits greater corner expansion. [3] |
| Dimensional challenge | High-strength grades are particularly sensitive to springback. Hydroforming research on high-strength steel shows that forming pressure, die geometry and friction influence residual stress and subsequent elastic recovery; industry experience likewise identifies low formability and high springback as key AHSS hydroforming challenges. [4] |
| Main applications | Typical applications are load-bearing or crash-management structures: roof rails, A/B-pillar structures, frame rails, crossmembers, engine cradles/subframes and similar hollow structural members. AHSS hydroforming guidance specifically documents hydroformed automotive roof-rail applications. [5] |

Concrete automotive example — 2013 Ford Fusion. Schuler documents the use of DP1000 hydroformed roof rail and B-pillar structures on the 2013 Ford Fusion. The roof rail runs from the lower A-pillar region rearward toward the C-pillar. Schuler reported approximately 4.2 kg per vehicle of mass reduction compared with the alternative hot-stamped design, while exploiting DP1000's high strength for structural and impact-load requirements. [6]

The practical implication is that HSS/AHSS is generally the best of the four material families when high-volume automotive structural production, crash performance and cost discipline are the main requirements. The process-development effort should concentrate on pressure–feed synchronization, pre-bending history, springback compensation, and accurate forming-limit/fracture models rather than simply maximizing pressure. [7]

### High-strength aluminum alloys

“High-strength aluminum” needs a small qualification. Hydroforming development spans several families: readily extrudable 6xxx-series alloys are common for automotive tubular structures, while higher-strength 2xxx and 7xxx alloys are attractive where specific strength is paramount. The latter, particularly 7075 in high-strength conditions, pose substantially greater room-temperature forming difficulties. NRC research describes 7075 as attractive because of its high strength-to-weight performance but reports insufficient room-temperature ductility for demanding hydroformed geometries, motivating warm hydroforming. [8]

| Aspect | Analysis |
| --- | --- |
| Material characteristics | Aluminum provides a major mass advantage over steel and is well suited to hollow/extruded structural members. Depending on alloy and temper, it can combine corrosion resistance with useful structural strength; 7xxx alloys such as 7075 extend the strength-to-weight advantage but sacrifice cold formability. [8] |
| Forming challenges | The central problem is limited ductility in stronger tempers, which raises the risk of localized thinning and burst before the tube completely conforms to the die. NRC work on 7075 therefore uses elevated-temperature hydroforming to increase usable deformation. [8] |
| Material-flow challenge | Preform geometry and friction become critical for complex cross-sections. In an experimental 6063-T4 automotive crossmember, researchers optimized the preform specifically to avoid flash, severe thinning, wrinkling and fracture; the successfully formed component had a reported maximum thinning of about 14%. [9] |
| Springback challenge | Aluminum hydroformed parts also require explicit springback control. Experimental/FE work on 2024-T3 double-curvature hydroformed parts shows that elastic recovery must be included in final-shape prediction, while SAE research on 2024-T3 and 2524-T3 specifically examined forming-pressure effects on hydroforming springback. [10] |
| Main applications | Typical targets include automotive crossmembers, subframe/frame members, crash rails and other extruded hollow structures; higher-strength 2xxx-series sheet hydroforming is also relevant to aerospace parts requiring complex curvature. [11] |

Concrete automotive example — 6063-T4 crossmember. Cai, Wang and Yuan experimentally hydroformed a 960-mm-long 6063-T4 aluminum automotive crossmember, using a carefully designed preform to manage material distribution. The study obtained a sound final component with approximately 14% maximum local thinning and controlled dimensional deviation. [9]

It is important not to conflate that example with the very highest-strength aluminum grades: 6063-T4 is a formability-oriented 6xxx condition rather than a 7075-class very-high-strength alloy. The distinction illustrates the central material-selection compromise in aluminum hydroforming. Softer or intermediate tempers simplify forming; 7xxx-type high-strength alloys may require warm forming, tailored thermal fields, or a forming-plus-heat-treatment route to obtain both complex geometry and high final strength. NRC's 7075 work is a clear demonstration of this need for thermal assistance. [8]

### Magnesium alloys

For hydroforming, the representative wrought magnesium alloy is AZ31/AZ31B. Magnesium offers exceptional lightweighting potential, but its deformation behavior is fundamentally more difficult than steel or conventional automotive aluminum at room temperature. Research on fine-grained AZ31 tubes links poor room-temperature hydroformability to strong basal texture and the restricted deformation modes of the magnesium crystal structure. [12]

| Aspect | Analysis |
| --- | --- |
| Material characteristics | AZ31/AZ31B is attractive for thin-walled lightweight structures, but its plastic deformation is strongly texture-, direction-, temperature- and strain-rate-dependent. This anisotropy is much more consequential for forming-process design than in conventional low-strength steel. [13] |
| Forming challenges | Room-temperature hydroforming is difficult. Heating activates additional deformation mechanisms and greatly enlarges the viable forming window, which is why most successful AZ31 tube-hydroforming research uses warm rather than conventional cold hydroforming. [14] |
| Temperature challenge | More heat is not automatically better. Warm-hydroforming experiments on AZ31B found that total elongation could continue rising with temperature while uniform elongation and maximum expansion reached their best values at a lower intermediate temperature—around 175°C in that particular experimental system. Temperature therefore has to be optimized jointly with strain rate and loading path rather than selected solely to maximize ductility. [15] |
| Process-control challenge | The pressure/axial-feed trajectory remains critical, and non-uniform heating can be deliberately used to control where material flows. A 2021 AZ31B study established an experimental/FE process window for T-shaped warm hydroforming at 250°C and showed the importance of both loading path and temperature distribution. [16] |
| Main applications | The strongest evidence today is in technology demonstrators and lightweight structural nodes/tubes, rather than the large-scale automotive deployment seen with steel. Research geometries include large-expansion tubes and T-joints that are directly representative of the kinds of hollow nodes needed in lightweight vehicle or aerospace frames. [17] |

Concrete application example — AZ31 T-joint. Experimental work has demonstrated successful hydroforming of a T-shaped AZ31 magnesium-alloy tubular joint at about 250°C, with a tailored loading path. [18] The component should be interpreted as a process/structural demonstrator, not as evidence that a particular production automobile uses an AZ31 hydroformed T-joint. That distinction matters: compared with steel and aluminum, magnesium hydroforming remains much more dependent on warm-process development and thermal-process integration. [19]

For engineering selection, magnesium therefore makes most sense when the mass-reduction target justifies a more complicated thermal process. The forming cell must control not only pressure, feed and friction, but also local temperature history, because the usable process window changes sharply with temperature. [17]

### Titanium alloys

The most relevant titanium alloy is Ti-6Al-4V (Ti-64, Grade 5). Its attraction comes from its combination of high strength, low structural mass relative to steels, temperature capability and corrosion resistance, which explains its importance in aerospace structures and engines. Hydroforming is particularly interesting for titanium because it can consolidate geometrically complicated sheet or tube components, but the material presents serious forming and springback challenges. [20]

| Aspect | Analysis |
| --- | --- |
| Material characteristics | Ti-6Al-4V is an α+β titanium alloy with high structural strength and strong relevance to aerospace, including regions requiring elevated-temperature capability. [20] |
| Forming challenges | Room-temperature sheet formability is limited, making fracture/damage prediction important. A 2024 experimental/numerical study of 0.55-mm Ti-6Al-4V sheet explicitly evaluated burst pressure, thickness distribution, dimensional accuracy and damage during hydraulic bulging and hydroforming. [21] |
| Springback challenge | Titanium's elastic recovery is substantial enough to require dedicated tooling compensation. Work on aerospace Ti-6Al-4V hydroforming reports greater springback than aluminum in the investigated conditions and identifies material thickness and forming pressure as influential parameters. [22] |
| Process solution | Warm/high-pressure and assisted processes are therefore attractive. Research has demonstrated Ti-6Al-4V fluid-cell/high-pressure forming at moderate elevated temperatures for intricate aerospace-representative shapes, while newer work examines pulsating and electrically assisted approaches to improve formability. [23] |
| Main applications | Titanium hydroforming is most naturally aligned with aerospace airframe and engine sheet-metal components, where complex geometry and relatively low production volume justify sophisticated processing. Automotive work exists but is currently much more specialized and development-oriented. [24] |

Concrete aerospace example. Hydroforming/fluid-cell forming is used for aerospace sheet components, and a Ti-6Al-4V study specifically describes the process in the context of aerostructure and engine sheet-metal parts, including aircraft hot-region applications. [22] A separate high-pressure warm-forming study used Ti-6Al-4V aerospace demonstrators at approximately 270°C and 140 MPa, showing that intricate shapes could be formed at substantially lower temperature than conventional hot-forming routes studied by the authors. [25]

Concrete automotive development example. A 2025 research study investigated electrically assisted hydro-mechanical/gas forming of Ti-6Al-4V tubular automobile A-pillar components. This is useful evidence that titanium hydroforming can address automotive safety-body geometries, but it should be classified as an emerging process demonstration rather than an established high-volume production application. [26]

Taken together, the four materials occupy quite different positions:

| Material family | Relative hydroforming advantage | Dominant process difficulty | Most credible application domain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HSS/AHSS | High structural/crash strength with mature tube-forming routes | Limited formability, springback, pressure–feed window | High-volume automotive body/chassis structures [27] |
| High-strength Al | Lightweight hollow structures; excellent potential with extrusions | Local thinning/burst, springback; very-high-strength grades need thermal assistance | Automotive crossmembers/frame members; aerospace sheet parts [28] |
| Mg AZ31/AZ31B | Maximum lightweighting potential among these mainstream structural-metal candidates | Very poor cold formability; strong temperature/texture sensitivity | Warm-hydroformed lightweight structural demonstrators and specialized components [14] |
| Ti-6Al-4V | High specific strength and aerospace-relevant temperature/corrosion performance | Low formability, strong springback, demanding process conditions | Aerospace airframe/engine components; emerging high-end automotive structures [24] |

## Common defects, causes, and countermeasures

A useful way to understand hydroforming defects is as a process-window problem. Internal pressure generates tensile expansion and forces the workpiece against the die, while axial feed supplies additional material into the deformation zone. Too much compressive feeding relative to pressure tends toward buckling/wrinkling; too much tensile expansion without adequate material inflow tends toward thinning, necking and ultimately tearing/bursting. Friction shifts both boundaries because it determines how readily material can move from the feeding region into highly strained areas. [29]

| Defect | Main causes | Recommended solutions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface defects（表面缺陷） | Surface quality can deteriorate through two distinct mechanisms. First, plastic strain itself can roughen a free surface; an aluminum sheet-hydroforming experiment measured increased roughness after forming even on a surface not contacting a metallic tool. Second, on the die-contact side, friction and lubrication determine sliding behavior and material flow, so an unsuitable tribological condition increases the risk of localized surface damage as well as nonuniform deformation. [30] | Control lubrication as an engineered process variable, rather than simply applying more lubricant. Select lubricant behavior for the actual contact pressure and sliding velocity; maintain a smooth, undamaged die surface; prevent foreign particles from becoming trapped at the interface; and control pressure so that unnecessarily severe die contact/sliding is avoided. Experimental THF work shows lubricant viscosity materially changes friction, while differential-lubrication experiments show that strategically changing friction can redirect tube material flow. [31] |
| Springback（回弹） | Springback is the elastic recovery that occurs once hydraulic/tool loads are removed. Its magnitude depends on the material's constitutive behavior and on the residual-stress field generated during forming. In hydroforming studies, forming pressure, die geometry, blank-holder friction, sheet thickness and material model have all been shown to influence predicted or measured springback. High-strength steel and Ti-6Al-4V are particularly demanding cases. [32] | Use sufficient final calibration/coining pressure within the safe forming window so the part fully conforms to the die; compensate die/form-block geometry for known elastic recovery; and use validated FE models with appropriate anisotropy/hardening behavior rather than relying on nominal tensile properties alone. For difficult alloys, warm or dynamically assisted forming can also reduce the severity of elastic recovery in suitable processes. [33] |
| Wrinkling（起皱） | The fundamental cause is excessive compressive instability: axial feed is too large or arrives too early relative to stabilizing internal pressure. Yuan et al. showed that unreasonable combinations of internal pressure and axial feeding produce wrinkling/buckling or, at the opposite extreme, bursting. Wrinkles form when compressive axial loading reaches a critical condition, and their number/behavior also depends on geometry such as length-to-diameter ratio. [34] | Redesign the pressure–feed loading path: raise pressure sufficiently to stabilize the wall before imposing large compressive feed, then synchronize feed with expansion. For sheet hydroforming, optimize blank-holder restraint as well. Preforming and local friction control can redistribute material before final calibration. Importantly, an intermediate wrinkle is not always fatal: controlled “useful wrinkles” can serve as temporary material reservoirs if subsequent pressure removes them completely; uncontrolled/dead wrinkles remain defects. [35] |
| Thinning（变薄） | Thinning is caused by excessive local tensile stretching without enough material replenishment. The usual triggers are high pressure relative to axial feed/draw-in, strong local expansion, tight die radii or abrupt geometry changes, and excessive friction that prevents material from flowing into the critical region. A hydroforming study of a torsion-beam geometry found that increasing friction hindered inflow into corner regions, aggravated local thinning, and at sufficiently severe conditions led to rupture. [36] | Increase or retime axial feed so additional material reaches the expansion zone without crossing into the wrinkling regime; optimize the pressure ramp instead of applying maximum pressure early; reduce harmful die–tube friction with appropriate lubrication; use a preform that places material closer to the final geometry; and, where design freedom exists, enlarge tight radii or smooth abrupt section changes. The 6063-T4 automotive-crossmember study demonstrates how optimized preforming can control severe thinning and produce a sound part. [37] |
| Tearing / bursting（撕裂/爆裂） | This is the end-stage tensile failure: localized strain/thinning exceeds the material's forming or fracture limit. Typical causes are excessive internal pressure, insufficient axial material feed, local stress concentration at tight corners, a preform that forces too much expansion into one region, or intrinsically poor ductility at the selected temperature/temper. Analytical hydroforming work explicitly treats bursting together with thinning, wrinkling and pressure/feed process limits; Ti-6Al-4V studies similarly show why damage/fracture behavior must be modeled rather than inferred from geometry alone. [38] | Reduce or reshape the pressure ramp and add appropriately timed end feed; modify the preform to distribute strain more evenly; increase critical corner radii where possible; validate the process against a forming-limit or calibrated fracture/damage criterion; and improve intrinsic formability where necessary using warm hydroforming or other assisted loading. DP600 experiments demonstrate fracture delay through end feeding; warm AZ31 and 7075 studies demonstrate the value of temperature for alloys whose room-temperature hydroformability is inadequate. [39] |

The five defects are consequently not independent problems. The most important optimization is the balance among pressure, feeding, friction and geometry. Increasing axial feed to solve thinning can create wrinkles; increasing pressure to remove wrinkles and improve die filling can accelerate thinning or burst; increasing friction to restrain one region can starve a neighboring corner of material; and maximizing final calibration pressure to reduce springback may exceed the fracture limit if the wall has already become too thin. This trade-off is why hydroforming process design is normally framed around a feasible loading window rather than a single “optimal pressure.” [40]

For production engineering, the most robust control sequence is therefore: establish the material's forming/fracture limits → design the preform and radii → characterize realistic friction → optimize the pressure/end-feed history → verify minimum wall thickness and wrinkle stability → apply only the calibration pressure necessary for dimensional accuracy → unload and validate springback. Research on DP600, aluminum crossmembers, AZ31B warm forming and Ti-6Al-4V damage prediction all point toward this integrated material–geometry–loading-path approach rather than treating each defect after it appears on the production line. [41]
