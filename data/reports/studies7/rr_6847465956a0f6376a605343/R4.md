#### Hard Constraints

The venue search is bounded by six non-negotiable criteria:

1. **Proximity to Berkeley/UC campus** — within walking distance or a short drive of the UC Berkeley campus.
2. **Seating capacity ≥30**, with a target of 30–45 to allow headroom for tables, a buffet line, and a projector screen.
3. **Confirmed projector + screen** suitable for a slideshow — not merely a PA system or television.
4. **Availability for an end-of-year date** (late April–May timeframe).
5. **Venue cost** that leaves enough room within the $2,500 budget for catering 30 guests — the lower the venue cost, the more budget is available for food, AV add-ons, and incidentals.
6. **Permission for outside catering** — the user intends to hire a specific external caterer with allergen-control capabilities, so venues requiring in-house catering are only viable as combined venue+catering scenarios.

## Dietary Restriction Strategy & Caterer Selection

### Allergen Matrix & Unified Safe Menu Strategy

#### Guest Count Restriction Audit

Of the 30 attendees, **6 guests carry dietary restrictions** and **24 have no restrictions**. The six restricted guests break down as: 2 with egg allergies, 2 with peanut allergies, 1 with a combined dairy + tree-nut + shellfish allergy, and 1 vegetarian. (The vegetarian restriction is subsumed entirely by a vegan menu, so it adds no further ingredient constraints; it is counted here for completeness. The egg-allergen guests may overlap with no other restrictions, and the combined-allergy guest is treated as having all three restrictions simultaneously.) The strategic question is whether to prepare separate meal variants for each restriction profile — or to serve a single unified menu that is simultaneously safe for all six restricted guests and all 24 unrestricted guests. This subsection argues that only the unified approach is operationally robust at this scale.

#### The FDA Big-9 Allergen Framework

The FDA enforces allergen labeling for nine major food allergens, as codified by the Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act (FALCPA) of 2004 — which originally identified eight (milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans) — and the FASTER Act of 2021, which added **sesame** as the ninth major allergen effective **January 1, 2023** ([FDA, Food Allergies](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-labeling-nutrition/food-allergies); [FDA, FASTER Act: Sesame Is the Ninth Major Food Allergen](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergies/faster-act-sesame-ninth-major-food-allergen)). These nine allergens account for approximately 90% of all serious allergic reactions in the United States ([UC Berkeley Dining, Allergies](https://dining.berkeley.edu/nutrition/allergies/)). The nine are: **milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame**.

Importantly, the FDA currently **has not established a threshold level** for any allergen — meaning there is no quantity below which an allergen is considered safe for a sensitised individual ([FDA, Food Allergies](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-labeling-nutrition/food-allergies)). This underscores why the zero-tolerance approach embedded in the unified menu strategy is the appropriate standard.

#### Allergen-Avoidance Matrix: Mapping Each Restriction to Eliminated Ingredients

The table below translates each guest-level restriction into the ingredient categories that must be eliminated across the entire menu. The "Unified Safe Menu" column shows that a single menu — fully plant-based, peanut-free, and tree-nut-free — simultaneously satisfies every restriction.

| Restriction | Guests | Ingredients that must be eliminated | Does unified vegan + peanut-free + tree-nut-free satisfy? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egg allergy | 2 | Eggs and all egg derivatives: albumin, lysozyme, mayonnaise, meringue, ovalbumin, vitellin, surimi | **Yes** — vegan menus contain no egg |
| Peanut allergy | 2 | Peanuts and peanut-derived products: peanut butter, peanut oil (unrefined), peanut flour, satay sauce, peanut garnish | **Yes** — but must actively exclude; see peanut-is-a-legume analysis below |
| Dairy + tree nuts + shellfish allergy | 1 | Milk and milk derivatives (butter, cream, whey, casein, ghee, cheese); all tree nuts (almonds, cashews, pistachios, pine nuts, walnuts, etc.) and tree-nut derivatives; all crustacean shellfish (shrimp, crab, lobster) | **Yes** — vegan excludes dairy and shellfish; tree-nut exclusion covers tree nuts |
| Vegetarian | 1 | All meat, poultry, fish, and seafood | **Yes** — vegan is stricter than vegetarian; fully plant-based exceeds vegetarian requirements |
| **Unified safe menu** | **6 restricted + 24 unrestricted** | Menu must be: vegan (no meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, shellfish, gelatin), peanut-free, tree-nut-free | **All 30 guests eat the same food safely** |

Sources: [FDA, Food Allergies](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-labeling-nutrition/food-allergies); [Food Allergy Research & Education (FARE), Egg Allergy](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-essentials/common-allergens/egg); [Gordon Food Service, Egg Allergies: What Foodservice Operators Need to Know](https://gfs.com/en-us/ideas/egg-allergies-what-foodservice-operators-need-know/); [Allergy & Asthma Network, Safe Food Substitutions for Top 9 Food Allergens](https://allergyasthmanetwork.org/food-allergies/living-with-food-allergies/safe-food-substitutions/).

#### Why a Unified Vegan Menu Is the Only Robust Strategy

**1. Eliminating cross-contamination between meal variants.** If the caterer prepares separate modified meals — one egg-free plate, one dairy-free plate, one nut-free plate, one vegetarian plate — the risk of cross-contact between variants rises sharply. Each additional meal variant requires separate prep surfaces, separate fryers, separate utensils, and dedicated storage. FARE defines cross-contact as the accidental transfer of an allergen from one food to another, noting that even amounts "so small that they usually can't be seen" have caused allergic reactions ([FARE, Avoiding Cross-Contact](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/avoiding-cross-contact)). A single unified menu eliminates this vector entirely: there is no need for a caterer to maintain parallel production lines, because every dish must meet the same standard.

**2. Simplifying the allergen-control protocol.** With one menu, the caterer must manage a single allergen profile rather than juggling multiple profiles. This means one set of ingredient checks, one set of supplier确认s, one set of labeling decisions, and one cross-contact strategy. For a 30-person student banquet with a limited budget, operational simplicity directly reduces the probability of an error.

**3. Vegan automatically satisfies four of the five restrictions.** A 100% plant-based menu excludes all animal products — meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, and shellfish. This satisfies the egg-allergy, dairy+shellfish-allergy, and vegetarian restrictions simultaneously. The only restriction that a vegan menu does *not* automatically satisfy is the peanut-allergy, because peanuts are legumes, not animal products, and a vegan menu can still include peanuts. Therefore, the unified menu must explicitly extend from "vegan" to "vegan + peanut-free + tree-nut-free."

#### The Critical Peanut-Is-a-Legume Nuance

A common and dangerous misconception is that "tree-nut-free" labels or claims guarantee "peanut-free." **This is false.** Peanuts (*Arachis hypogaea*) are botanically legumes — related to beans, lentils, and soybeans — not tree nuts ([FARE, Egg Allergy](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-essentials/common-allergens/egg); [Allergy & Asthma Network, Safe Substitutions](https://allergyasthmanetwork.org/food-allergies/living-with-food-allergies/safe-food-substitutions/)). Conversely, tree nuts (almonds, walnuts, cashews, pistachios, pecans, etc.) grow on trees and are botanically unrelated to peanuts. However, a person with a peanut allergy may also be allergic to tree nuts — approximately 25–40% of individuals with peanut allergy are also allergic to one or more tree nuts — but the two allergens are distinct ([Allergy & Asthma Network, Safe Substitutions](https://allergyasthmanetwork.org/food-allergies/living-with-food-allergies/safe-food-substitutions/)).

This distinction matters operationally for two reasons:

- **Caterer "nut-free" claims must be verified.** A caterer who says "our menu is nut-free" may mean tree-nut-free only. The event host must explicitly confirm that the menu excludes **both** peanuts **and** tree nuts, and request written confirmation.
- **Peanut oil and peanut sauce are hidden sources.** Unrefined peanut oil retains allergenic proteins and can cause reactions; highly refined peanut oil is generally considered non-allergenic due to the refining process, but this should be confirmed with each guest's allergist ([Allergy & Asthma Network, Safe Substitutions](https://allergyasthmanetwork.org/food-allergies/living-with-food-allergies/safe-food-substitutions/)). Peanut sauce (satay) and peanut garnish in Asian cuisine are less obvious sources that must be screened out.

#### Residual Risks Under the Unified Vegan Strategy

Even with a vegan + peanut-free + tree-nut-free menu, the following residual risks require active management:

**1. Peanut as a legume.** A vegan menu could theoretically include peanuts or peanut-derived ingredients. The unified menu must explicitly exclude them. This requires confirming with the caterer that no peanut oil, peanut butter, peanut flour, satay sauce, or peanut garnish is used in any dish ([FARE, Egg Allergy](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-essentials/common-allergens/egg); [Allergy & Asthma Network, Safe Substitutions](https://allergyasthmanetwork.org/food-allergies/living-with-food-allergies/safe-food-substitutions/)).

**2. Hidden egg in breads, wraps, and baked goods.** Eggs function as a binder, leavening agent, and glaze in many baked and prepared foods that are not obviously "egg products." FARE and foodservice guidance identify the following hidden egg sources that a caterer must explicitly confirm are absent:

- **Pasta / macaroni:** Fresh or commercially made cooked pasta typically contains egg; dried boxed pasta is usually egg-free but must be verified ([Gordon Food Service, Egg Allergies](https://gfs.com/en-us/ideas/egg-allergies-what-foodservice-operators-need-know/); [FARE, Egg Allergy](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-essentials/common-allergens/egg)).
- **Baked goods and breads:** Many commercial breads are coated with an egg wash for sheen and browning; cakes, cookies, muffins, and pastries commonly contain egg ([Gordon Food Service, Egg Allergies](https://gfs.com/en-us/ideas/egg-allergies-what-foodservice-operators-need-know/)).
- **Mayonnaise:** Mayonnaise is an emulsion of egg yolks and oil; some vegan mayo substitutes exist but must be specified.
- **Some falafel recipes:** Traditional falafel is made from chickpeas or fava beans without egg, but some commercial recipes use egg as a binder. The caterer must confirm egg-free falafel ([The Mediterranean Dish, Authentic Falafel Recipe](https://www.themediterraneandish.com/how-to-make-falafel/)).
- **Some pita bread recipes:** Certain pita or flatbread recipes include egg; standard pita is typically egg-free but must be confirmed.
- **Surimi** (imitation crab/meat) contains egg white and must be excluded entirely.
- **Meatloaf and meatballs:** Often use egg as a binder; a vegan menu eliminates this risk, but any "vegetarian" meat substitute using egg white could be a hidden source.
- **Fried rice:** Some commercial fried rice contains scrambled egg; the caterer must confirm.
- **Marshmallows, meringue, nougat, and meringue powder** all derive from egg whites and must be excluded.

Source: [Gordon Food Service, Egg Allergies: What Foodservice Operators Need to Know](https://gfs.com/en-us/ideas/egg-allergies-what-foodservice-operators-need-know/); [FARE, Egg Allergy](https://www.foodallergy.org/living-food-allergy/food-essentials/common-allergens/egg); [UC Berkeley Dining, Allergies](https://dining.berkeley.edu/nutrition/allergies/).

**3. Hidden dairy in prepared and processed foods.** Dairy proteins (casein, whey) and milk derivatives appear in many foods that are not obviously "dairy." The caterer must verify exclusion of:

- **Butter, cream, cheese, sour cream, crème fraîche, and ghee.** Ghee (clarified butter) is widely used in Indian cuisine and is a significant hidden dairy source; it is not rendered non-allergenic by the clarification process.
- **Whey, casein, caseinate, and lactalbumin** — milk proteins that appear in processed foods including breads, crackers, cereals, sauces, and "natural flavor" ingredients.
- **Margarine** — many brands contain whey or milk solids.
- **Deli meats and sausages** — some use milk proteins as binders.
- **Non-imitation cheese** labeled "non-dairy" — some contain casein, a milk protein.
- **Chocolate** — even dark chocolate frequently contains milk or is produced on shared lines with milk chocolate.
- **"Natural flavors"** — this umbrella term can conceal dairy-derived ingredients; the caterer must confirm each sauce and seasoning.

Source: [Safe Plated, Hidden Sources of Dairy in Common Foods](https://www.safeplated.com/blog/hidden-sources-of-dairy-in-common-foods); [IngrediiCheck, Hidden Sources of Dairy](https://www.ingredii-check.app/blog/hidden-sources-of-dairy-a-checklist-for-milk-allergies).

**4. Tree-nut hidden sources in dressings, sauces, and garnishes.**

- **Pesto** traditionally contains pine nuts.
- **Cashew cream** is a common dairy-free sauce base in vegan cooking — but is a tree nut and must be excluded.
- **Nut garnishes** on salads, grain bowls, or desserts.
- **Nut oils** (e.g., walnut oil) in dressings.
- **Marzipan and nougat** contain nuts.
- **Sunflower seed butter** or **tahini** — seeds, not tree nuts, but flagged separately under sesame.

Source: [Allergy & Asthma Network, Safe Substitutions](https://allergyasthmanetwork.org/food-allergies/living-with-food-allergies/safe-food-substitutions/); [Safe Plated, Hidden Dairy Sources](https://www.safeplated.com/blog/hidden-sources-of-dairy-in-common-foods).

**5. Shared fryers with egg or seafood.** Cross-contact through shared cooking oil is a documented risk. The Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America confirms that "patients with allergies to fish and shellfish can have an allergic reaction after eating foods cooked in the same oil used to cook fish or shellfish" ([AAFA Community, Ask the Allergist: Shared Oil](https://community.aafa.org/db/ask-the-allergist/record/is-it-safe-for-me-to-eat-foods-fried-in-the-same-oil-as-my-allergens)). This is directly relevant to the guest with a shellfish allergy. The caterer must confirm that no shellfish or egg-battered items are fried in oil shared with the banquet's dishes. If the kitchen prepares egg-battered items (e.g., tempura, fried chicken) in a shared fryer, the proteins can transfer to the oil and subsequently to the banquet food. FARE's cross-contact guidance recommends that allergen-free meals be prepared with utensils and pans thoroughly washed with soap and water, and preferably on separate surfaces or with separate fryers ([FARE, Avoiding Cross-Contact](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/avoiding-cross-contact)).

#### Sesame: The Ninth Allergen and The Tahini Caveat

The FASTER Act added **sesame** as the ninth major U.S. allergen effective January 1, 2023 ([FDA, Food Allergies](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-labeling-nutrition/food-allergies)). None of the six restricted guests has a declared sesame allergy. However:

- **Tahini** (sesame paste) is a staple of Mediterranean and Middle Eastern hummus. If the selected caterer's cuisine leans Mediterranean, tahini may appear in hummus, baba ghanoush, dressings, and sauces.
- **Sesame seeds** are common garnishes on breads, bagels, salads, and Asian dishes.
- **Sesame oil** is a standard cooking oil in East Asian cuisines.

No guest has reported a sesame allergy, so sesame is not a hard exclusion. However, **sesame sensitivity can co-occur with other allergies**, and because the FDA only began mandatory sesame labeling in 2023, some products in circulation may still lack clear sesame labeling ([FDA, FASTER Act](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergies/faster-act-sesame-ninth-major-food-allergen)). The event host should: (a) ask all six restricted guests whether they also avoid sesame; (b) request sesame ingredient confirmation from the caterer as a precautionary measure; and (c) label any sesame-containing dish at the buffet so self-service is transparent. If any guest confirms sesame sensitivity, tahini and sesame-containing dishes must be replaced or excluded, which narrows the cuisine options (e.g., a Mediterranean falafel-and-hummus platter would need hummus reformulated without tahini).

#### Cross-Contact Control Protocol

Even with a fully unified vegan + peanut-free + tree-nut-free menu, the following cross-contact controls must be in place during preparation, transport, and service:

| Control Layer | Specific Measure | Responsible Party |

| **Pre-preparation** | Caterer confirms no shared fryers with egg-battered or seafood items; dedicated or thoroughly washed prep surfaces | Caterer |

| **Buffet serving** | Separate serving utensils per dish; shared scoops prohibited; host verifies utensil placement before doors open | Host + Caterer |

| **Cross-contact response protocol** | If a server accidentally transfers a utensil between dishes, the affected dish is covered, removed, and replaced — "the entire dish must be discarded and remade, as even trace amounts of an allergen can make a meal unsafe" per Konstantinou et al. (2025) ([NIH, Managing Food Allergies in Dining Establishments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12113666/)) | Host monitors |

#### Summary: The Unified Safe Menu Rule

The only robust strategy at this scale — and the standard recommended by food-allergy event-planning guidance — is to serve a **single unified menu that is simultaneously vegan, peanut-free, and tree-nut-free**. This rule ensures that every dish is safe for all six restricted guests and all 24 unrestricted guests, eliminating the cross-contamination vector inherent in preparing multiple meal variants. The menu must also **actively exclude sesame** if any guest confirms sesame sensitivity, and the caterer must provide **written allergen confirmation per dish** covering all FDA Big-9 allergens — milk, egg, fish, crustustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanut, wheat, soy, and sesame — as well as confirmation of **no shared fryers** with egg-battered or seafood items. This establishes the baseline from which specific caterer selection (S2.2) and menu itemization (S3.1) proceed.

### Allergen-Safety Protocol & Accommodation Matrix

This subsection defines the operational safety measures that protect every dietary group at the banquet. It covers the full chain of allergen safety: pre-event caterer communication, on-site labeling, cross-contact controls, guest-facing dietary cards, host verification, and emergency medical protocol. The guiding strategy — established in S2.1 and S3.1 — is a single unified menu that is fully plant-based (vegan), peanut-free, and tree-nut-free, so that all 30 guests eat the same food with no variant meals and no cross-contamination between different dish types. The protocols below enforce that strategy operationally.

---

#### 1. Pre-Event Written Allergen Brief to the Caterer

The single most important safety document is a **one-page written allergen brief** sent to the caterer no fewer than 14 days before the event (aligned with the T-14 timeline in S4.2). The brief should be signed by the event host and emailed to both the caterer's designated chef/manager and the sales contact, with a request for written confirmation of receipt and compliance.

**Critical context from [UC Berkeley Dining](https://dining.berkeley.edu/nutrition/allergies/):** UC Berkeley Dining explicitly states that "customers concerned with food allergens and gluten must be aware of these risks" and that "due to the volume of meals served… cross-contact may also occur despite our best efforts to prevent it." The same principle applies to any external caterer serving 30 guests — a verbal conversation is insufficient; the brief must be in writing to create a documented allergen-control agreement.

**The brief template should include the following sections:**

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| **Event details** | Date, venue address with loading/drop-off instructions, guest count (30), service start time |
| **Master allergen restriction** | "All food must be simultaneously: egg-free, dairy-free, peanut-free, tree-nut-free, shellfish-free, and vegetarian-compatible (fully plant-based). No variant meals — one unified buffet for all 30 guests." |
| **Guest-level restriction table** | List each restriction type and count: 2 × egg allergy, 2 × peanut allergy (peanut = legume, NOT tree nut), 1 × dairy + tree-nut + shellfish allergy, 1 × vegetarian. Note that the unified vegan + peanut-free + tree-nut-free menu covers all simultaneously (see S2.1 for the full restriction-to-ingredient mapping). |
| **Ingredient disclosure request** | "Provide a complete allergen matrix for every dish covering all FDA Big-9 allergens (per S2.1) plus the specific items: beans (no lard), rice (no chicken stock), tortillas (no egg wash), falafel (no egg binder if applicable), no shared fryers with seafood or egg-battered items." |
| **Cross-contamination controls** | "Dedicated prep surfaces and utensils for allergen-free preparation; no shared fryers; separate cooking equipment from any dairy/seafood/egg items; food transported in sealed, labeled containers." |
| **Labeling request** | "Provide ingredient cards per dish listing all Big-9 allergens present/absent; label all dishes at the buffet." |

As the [American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI)](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/event-planning-and-food-allergy-awareness) emphasizes, event planners are serving a "captive audience": food-allergic guests at a banquet cannot simply walk to another restaurant if they feel unsafe, unlike diners at a restaurant who can choose elsewhere. This asymmetry of control makes the written brief — and the caterer's written commitment to its terms — the primary protective mechanism.

**Caterer willingness:** Per the [Messina Catering guide on working with caterers to accommodate food allergies](https://messinascatering.com/working-with-caterer-accommodate-food-allergies/), "the best caterers will take precautions to avoid cross-contamination" and "food must be cooked with clean utensils, packed in separate containers, and never come in contact with an allerger." The host should request a written confirmation that the caterer has read and agreed to each bullet in the brief. If the caterer balks at signing or providing ingredient lists, that is a red flag — the host should consider the backup caterer identified in S2.2.

#### 2. On-Site Ingredient Cards and Color-Coded Buffet Labeling

Every dish at the buffet requires a physical ingredient card. Best practices are drawn from [UC Berkeley Dining's allergen labeling system](https://dining.berkeley.edu/nutrition/allergies/), which tracks and labels the Big-9 using icon-based symbols on menu signage, and from [Groups360's meeting planner guide](https://groups360.com/blog/food-allergy-friendly-events-a-meeting-planners-guide-to-ensure-food-safety/), which recommends labels that "include key ingredients, allergen warnings, and potential cross-contamination risks" with "font large enough to be read easily."

**On-site ingredient card format (one per dish, placed directly in front of each chafing dish/tray):**

Each card should list:

1. **Dish name** in bold, large font
2. **All FDA Big-9 allergens** present in the dish, using a "Contains: [allergens]" format per [FDA labeling convention](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-labeling-nutrition/food-allergies)
3. **"Free of"** line listing the allergens the dish does NOT contain (this is the inverse verification — it confirms what was deliberately excluded)
4. **Prep note** if applicable (e.g., "Prepared in dedicated allergen-free workspace," "Uses corn tortillas, not flour")

Under a unified vegan + peanut-free + tree-nut-free menu, **every dish card should read "Free of: milk, eggs, fish/crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts"** with varying notes on wheat, soy, and sesame depending on the specific dish.

**Color-coded placard system** (layered on top of ingredient cards):

| Color | Meaning | Role at This Banquet |
|---|---|---|
| **🟢 GREEN** (card background or tent) | **SAFE FOR ALL** — no egg, dairy, peanut, tree nut, shellfish, soy (if also soy-free), or sesame (if also sesame-free) in this dish | All dishes under the unified menu should carry a green label. This is the default. |
| **🟠 ORANGE** | **Contains dairy** | Reserved for any optional add-on items (e.g., cheese on the side for non-allergic guests) — NOT part of the unified safe menu |
| **🔵 BLUE** | **Contains sesame** (e.g., tahini in hummus) | If any dish contains sesame but is otherwise safe for all 5 restricted guests, it gets a blue card. No guest has a listed sesame allergy (per S2.1), but the host should verify with the two peanut-allergic guests whether they also have sesame sensitivity, as cross-reactivity is possible. |
| **🟡 YELLOW** | **Contains tree nuts/peanuts** | Reserved for any optional add-on items — NOT part of the unified safe menu |

Since the unified menu eliminates all top allergens by design, **the expected outcome is that every dish on the buffet carries a GREEN label.** The orange, blue, and yellow cards exist as a protocol for the rare case where an optional add-on (e.g., a cheese plate for unrestricted guests) is introduced — those items go at the **far end of the buffet line** and are clearly marked.

Per [Groups360's guidance](https://groups360.com/blog/food-allergy-friendly-events-a-meeting-planners-guide-to-ensure-food-safety/), QR codes can supplement (not replace) physical cards by linking to a detailed ingredient breakdown, but physical cards are mandatory because not all guests will have phones or feel comfortable scanning at a buffet.

#### 3. Cross-Contamination Minimization Protocol

Even with a unified menu that eliminates top allergens by design, cross-contact can still occur during **serving** if a guest or server accidentally transfers a contaminant between dishes. The protocol below is modeled on [FARE's (Food Allergy Research & Education) cross-contact prevention guidance](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/avoiding-cross-contact) and [Unilever Food Solutions' professional cross-contamination protocols](https://www.unileverfoodsolutions.us/chef-inspiration/allergen-101/cross-contamination.html).

**Before the event (caterer-side):**
- Confirm with the caterer in writing that allergen-free food is prepared using **dedicated utensils, cutting boards, and pans** — or, at minimum, that all equipment has been cleaned with hot, soapy water before allergen-free prep ([FARE guidance](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/avoiding-cross-contact)).
- Confirm **no shared fryers** with egg-battered items, meat, or seafood. If the caterer's kitchen also prepares items containing dairy, eggs, or shellfish on other orders, the allergen-free dishes must be cooked first or on separate equipment.
- Food must be transported in **sealed, labeled containers** — not open trays that could be exposed to airborne allergens or shared surfaces during transport.

**During serving (event-side):**
- **Separate serving utensils per dish** — no shared scoops, tongs, or ladles between dishes. Each chafing dish or tray has its own dedicated utensil, placed to the side of (not inside) the food to prevent accidental double-dipping.
- **Buffet covered when not serving** — foil or chafing-dish lids kept on dishes between serving waves to prevent airborne contamination and guest accidental contact.
- **Optional allergen-containing add-ons at the far end** of the buffet line — if the host opts to provide cheese or nut-containing items for unrestricted guests, those items are placed at the **end of the line** (never at the beginning) so that restricted guests do not pass them or risk drips/splashes onto safe dishes. This follows the [Food Allergy Canada](https://foodallergycanada.ca/professional-resources/foodservice/foodservice/tips-for-caterers/) recommendation to "keep allergen-safe foods together" and separate from allergen-containing items.

**If cross-contamination is discovered during the event:**
- **Default protocol: immediate dish removal.** If a server or guest reports that a utensil was cross-dipped (e.g., someone used the dairy-containing cheese scoop in the guacamole), the affected dish is immediately removed from the buffet and replaced with a backup store-bought item that meets the unified allergen profile (e.g., pre-packaged guacamole or salsa from a sealed container with a clean ingredient label).
- The host designates **one person** to oversee this decision — no debate at the buffet line. Better to over-remove than to leave a potentially contaminated dish in service.
- If the contamination is widespread (e.g., a server systematically mixed utensils across all dishes), the host should switch to a **plated-service fallback**: pre-serve individual plates from the kitchen using the caterer's backup ingredients.

The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/event-planning-and-food-allergy-awareness) specifically warns that cross-contamination disasters often occur because of "taking a spoon used to cream soup and stirring milk-free soup" or "chopping nuts and a salad on the same block" — scenarios that are easily visible at a buffet and must be actively guarded against.

#### 4. Dietary Accommodation Cards for Restricted Guests

Each of the 6 guests with dietary restrictions receives a **printed dietary card** at seating (placed at their table setting or handed at check-in). The card serves as their personal "safe to eat" reference, so they do not need to memorize the menu or nervously interrogate servers at the buffet line. This practice follows the [Thrive Meetings recommendation](https://thrivemeetings.com/2015/08/ingredients-needed-for-safe-food-event/) to "provide one personalized card for each person with a summary of their needs on it to reduce confusion with wait staff and culinary team" — rather than forcing guests to carry multiple generic cards or self-advocate repeatedly.

**Dietary card format (4" × 6" cardstock):**

> **DIETARY ACCOMMODATION CARD — [Guest Name]**
>
> **Restrictions:** [e.g., Egg allergy (anaphylaxis risk)]
>
> **You may safely eat ALL dishes at this banquet.** The entire buffet is vegan, peanut-free, tree-nut-free, egg-free, dairy-free, and shellfish-free by design.
>
> **Please note:** [Any dish-specific flag, e.g., "The hummus contains tahini (sesame). No sesame allergy on file, but please be aware if you have any sesame sensitivity."]
>
> **Emergency medication:** [e.g., "I carry an EpiPen in my bag." / "Host-supplied EpiPen is at the host table."]
>
> **If you have any questions at the buffet, please ask [Host Name] — they have the full ingredient matrix.**

**Card distribution logic:**

| Guest Type | # Guests | Card Restriction Field | Special Note on Card |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egg allergy | 2 | "Egg allergy — severe" | All dishes egg-free; see S2.1 for hidden-egg sources to verify. |
| Peanut allergy | 2 | "Peanut allergy — severe" | All dishes peanut-free and tree-nut-free. Peanuts are legumes, not tree nuts. |
| Dairy + tree nuts + shellfish allergy | 1 | "Dairy + tree-nut + shellfish allergy — severe" | All dishes free of dairy, tree nuts, and shellfish; see S2.1 for hidden sources. |

For the **one guest with the combined dairy + tree-nut + shellfish allergy**: the unified vegan + peanut-free + tree-nut-free menu means this guest can eat **100% of the buffet with no modifications** and no special sub-menu. Because every dish is plant-based (no dairy, no meat, no shellfish), peanut-free, and tree-nut-free by design, there is no need for a separate "safe" plate or a restricted sub-menu. #### 6. EpiPen and Emergency Medical Protocol

**EpiPen access and carriage:**
- Every guest with a known severe allergy (the 2 egg-allergic, 2 peanut-allergic, and the 1 dairy+tree-nut+shellfish-allergic guests) should carry their **own epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen)** on their person at all times. This is standard practice: UC Berkeley Dining explicitly states that "[c]ustomers with life-threatening food allergies in need to use an epinephrine injector should carry their own at all times" and that "UC Berkeley Dining staff are NOT trained to administer epinephrine and CANNOT provide or administer it" ([UC Berkeley Dining allergen policy](https://dining.berkeley.edu/nutrition/allergies/)). The same principle applies to the ban venue — no venue staff will administer epinephrine; guests must self-carry.
- The host should maintain **2 backup EpiPens** (separate from guest-carried devices) in a known, accessible location — e.g., at the host table near the entrance. These are for emergency use if a guest's own device is inaccessible (e.g., left in a car) or if a second dose is needed. The [FARE ("Heading to Camp") resource](https://www.foodallergy.org/resources/heading-camp) notes that organizations should have epinephrine on hand even when individuals are expected to carry their own, because a reaction can occur from an undiagnosed allergy.
- International guidelines recommend that individuals at risk of anaphylaxis carry **two doses** of epinephrine — "You Need 2" doses for anaphylaxis, as noted by the [Allergy & Asthma Network](https://allergyasthmanetwork.org/food-allergies/living-with-food-allergies/safe-food-substitutions/).

**Emergency responder designation:**
- One host member is designated as the **on-site emergency responder** for the full duration of the event. This person's responsibilities are: (1) know where all EpiPens are (guest-carried + host backup), (2) know the location of the nearest hospital, (3) be able to recognize anaphylaxis symptoms (hives, swelling, difficulty breathing, throat tightness, dizziness), (4) be authorized to call 911, and (5) brief the caterer's lead server on the plan before doors open.
- This designation is the host-side responsibility per the [Groups360 guide](https://groups360.com/blog/food-allergy-friendly-events-a-meeting-planners-guide-to-ensure-food-safety/): "work with staff to identify where medical aid is located, ensure EpiPens are accessible if needed, and establish a protocol for handling severe allergic reactions."

**Nearest hospital:**
- **Alta Bates Summit Medical Center — Alta Bates Campus Emergency Department**, 2450 Ashby Avenue, Berkeley, CA 94705. Open 24 hours, phone: (510) 204-4444. This is approximately 2 miles from downtown Berkeley venues (Sports Basement, Berkeley Public Library area). The [UC Berkeley University Health Services](https://uhs.berkeley.edu/medical/urgent-care/local-emergency-and-urgent-care-centers) lists this as the local emergency room for the campus area. The host should save this phone number and address in their phone and in the event-day logistics document.

**Emergency action plan (posted at host table):**
1. Recognize anaphylaxis symptoms
2. Use guest's own EpiPen (or host backup if needed) — lay person flat or in recovery position
3. Call 911 — state "anaphylaxis, food allergy, epinephrine administered"
4. Notify on-site emergency responder host
5. Do NOT give the person anything to eat or drink (risk of choking)
6. If no improvement after 5 minutes, administer second EpiPen if available
7. Meet paramedics at venue entrance and direct them to the guest

#### Verification Against Key Research Questions

**How should the host communicate allergy restrictions to the caterer in writing?**
Via the one-page written allergen brief (Section 1 above), sent by email to the caterer's chef/manager and sales contact at T-14 days, requesting written confirmation. The brief lists each restriction type, the master allergen-avoidance requirement (unified vegan + peanut-free + tree-nut-free), specific ingredient checks (no lard, no chicken stock, no egg wash), and cross-contamination controls (dedicated surfaces, no shared fryers). The [Messina Catering guide](https://messinascatering.com/working-with-caterer-accommodate-food-allergies/) recommends "be as transparent as possible with regard to labeling food and ingredients."

**Can the caterer provide a complete allergen matrix per dish covering all FDA Big-9 allergens (per S2.1)?**
Yes — the written brief requests exactly this. The caterer should provide an ingredient list per dish. Per the [Thrive Meetings analysis](https://thrivemeetings.com/2015/08/ingredients-needed-for-safe-food-event/), event planners should ask caterers "to label their items with the allergens they contain — at least the top 8, but preferably the EU list of 14." UC Berkeley Dining tracks all Big-9 allergens ([dining.berkeley.edu](https://dining.berkeley.edu/nutrition/allergies/)), and any caterer experienced with university catering should be able to provide equivalent documentation.

**Best practice for self-serve buffet labels?**
Color-coded placards + per-dish ingredient cards + printed menu tents at tables + QR code supplement (optional). The [Groups360 guide](https://groups360.com/blog/food-allergy-friendly-events-a-meeting-planners-guide-to-ensure-food-safety/) recommends "detailed and legible signage" that "includes key ingredients, and potential cross-contamination risks" and using symbols/icons for common allergens.

**What happens if cross-contamination is discovered?**
Immediate dish removal and replacement with a backup store-bought allergen-safe item (Section 3). The affected dish is not returned to service for the remainder of the event. The host designee makes this decision without debate.

**If a guest carries an EpiPen: what is the venue policy?**
Guests self-carry and self-administer. Venue staff are not trained to administer epinephrine (per [UC Berkeley Dining policy](https://dining.berkeley.edu/nutrition/allergies/), which applies broadly). The host maintains 2 backup EpiPens and designates one emergency responder.

**For the one guest with combined dairy + tree-nut + shellfish allergy: can they eat 100% of the unified menu?**
Yes. Confirmed in Section 4 and the attendance matrix. No special sub-menu is needed because the unified menu is simultaneously vegan (no dairy), tree-nut-free, and shellfish-free by design.

#### Food Safety Protocol

Per [USDA Food Safety guidelines](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-handling-take-out-foods):

- **Hot dishes**: Maintain at or above 140°F using chafing trays or insulated carriers (Comal delivers hot items in insulated carriers).
- **Cold dishes**: Maintain at or below 40°F using ice baths or cold packs.
- **2-Hour Rule**: Never leave food out of refrigeration for more than 2 hours. If ambient temperature exceeds 90°F, the limit is 1 hour. After this window, discard or refrigerate remaining food.
- **Danger Zone**: Bacteria grow rapidly between 40°F and 140°F. Minimize time food spends in this range.
- **Separate utensils**: Use a dedicated serving utensil for each dish; never cross-use between different food items.
- **Buffet coverage**: Cover dishes when not actively serving to prevent contamination by insects, airborne particles, or accidental contact.
