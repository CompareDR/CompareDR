# From Dodder to Virus: Documenting the Landscape of Natural Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants

## Intimate Connections: Gene Flow Through Parasitic Relationships

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT), the non-sexual movement of genetic material across species boundaries, has long been recognized as a dominant force in prokaryotic evolution [[1](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)]. However, its prevalence and significance in multicellular eukaryotes, particularly plants, are increasingly appreciated [[43](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7248198/)]. Among plants, parasitic species stand out as exceptional hotspots for HGT, primarily due to the intimate and sustained physical connection they establish with their hosts [[52](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstreams/2ab6cca1-30b0-4668-9356-db2bd78d7a11/download), [54](https://d-nb.info/1233426761/34)]. This connection is mediated by a specialized organ called the haustorium, which functions as a rootlike structure that invades the host's vascular tissues to extract water, nutrients, and photosynthates [[35](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-phyto-082718-100043), [72](https://www.zmescience.com/science/parasites-steal-genes-23072019/), [118](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haustorium)]. This direct cellular bridge creates a unique opportunity for the exchange of not only resources but also genetic information, blurring the lines between distinct organisms [[119](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7075074/)]. The frequency of HGT appears to increase with the intimacy of such interactions, making the plant-parasite interface a powerful model for studying inter-kingdom gene flow [[52](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstreams/2ab6cca1-30b0-4668-9356-db2bd78d7a11/download)].

The primary conduit for this genetic exchange is the haustorial connection [[72](https://www.zmescience.com/science/parasites-steal-genes-23072019/)]. Once established, the haustorium forms a continuous network of cytoplasmic bridges between the parasite and the host, allowing for the direct trafficking of molecules, including RNAs and potentially DNA fragments [[4](https://www.scienceintheclassroom.org/research-papers/getting-know-your-neighbors-parasitic-plants-traffic-rna-their-hosts), [98](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jipb.12041)]. This close association enhances the likelihood of horizontal gene transfer events [[54](https://d-nb.info/1233426761/34), [56](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32224234/), [57](https://su.diva-portal.org/smash/record.jsf?pid=diva2:1438878)]. While the precise molecular mechanisms governing the transfer of large DNA segments remain largely unknown, the physical continuity provided by the haustorium is considered the foundational prerequisite [[3](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.16022)]. Evidence suggests that this interface facilitates the cell-to-cell travel of whole organelles, representing a massive-scale form of genetic exchange that could theoretically include entire plastid or mitochondrial genomes [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7775762/)]. Such direct transport of cellular components would represent a significant pathway for HGT, although the frequency and stability of such integrations require further investigation [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7775762/)].

The types of genetic material documented to move via these parasitic interfaces are diverse. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is the most frequently reported class of transferred genetic material in parasitic systems [[51](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S136952661500059X)]. Studies have shown that plant mitochondrial genomes are remarkably fluid, having undergone rampant horizontal gene transfer events [[53](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article/info%3Adoi%2F10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1003265)]. These foreign genes can be integrated into the host mitochondrial genome and, in some cases, are even expressed, suggesting they have become functional parts of the organelle's genetic machinery [[58](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32929737/)]. For instance, analyses of parasitic plants and their hosts reveal clear footprints of mtDNA exchange [[74](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6023873/)]. Although the exact mechanisms driving these frequent mtDNA transfers are still being elucidated, they are thought to involve extensive recombination events within the mitochondrial nucleoid, where foreign DNA fragments can be incorporated into the host genome [[50](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/)]. Beyond mitochondria, transfers of nuclear DNA (nDNA) also occur, albeit less frequently than mtDNA exchanges [[51](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S136952661500059X)]. The acquisition of entire, functional nuclear genes represents a more profound evolutionary change, as these genes can directly alter the phenotype and physiology of the recipient organism [[71](https://www.biotechniques.com/plant-climate-science/parasitic-plants-stealing-host-genes-to-use-against-them/)]. Furthermore, evidence points to the widespread trafficking of RNA, specifically messenger RNA (mRNA), between parasitic plants like *Cuscuta* and their hosts [[4](https://www.scienceintheclassroom.org/research-papers/getting-know-your-neighbors-parasitic-plants-traffic-rna-their-hosts)]. This mRNA interchange raises the intriguing possibility that horizontally transferred RNA could serve as a template for reverse transcription into DNA, which might then integrate into the nuclear genome, providing a potential intermediate step in the HGT process [[5](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2015.00045/full), [9](https://d-nb.info/1161344187/34)]. Finally, selfish genetic elements, such as transposable elements (TEs), are also part of the HGT landscape. One study documented the acquisition of several TEs derived from grasses into the genome of the parasitic witchweed *Striga asiatica*, demonstrating that even mobile DNA segments can cross kingdom barriers [[8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982219309479)].

The genus *Cuscuta*, commonly known as dodders, serves as a premier model for studying HGT between parasites and hosts. As a stem parasite, *Cuscuta* wraps around its host, forming haustoria that penetrate the host's vascular system [[44](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/nph.17003), [72](https://www.zmescience.com/science/parasites-steal-genes-23072019/)]. Recent advances, including the sequencing of the high-quality reference genome of *Cuscuta australis* and the assembly of the *Cuscuta campestris* genome, have provided unprecedented insights into this phenomenon [[10](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-04721-8), [90](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCA_900332095.2/), [92](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/9184601)]. Analysis of *C. campestris* revealed 108 transcribed and likely functional HGT events originating from a wide array of host plants, indicating a history of broad host range colonization [[66](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/), [68](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/parasitic-plants-use-stolen-genes-make-them-better-parasites)]. These studies detected at least 52 separate instances of nonsexual DNA transfer from hosts to *Cuscuta*, underscoring the scale of this genetic exchange [[76](https://science.psu.edu/news/parasitic-plants-may-form-weapons-out-genes-stolen-hosts)]. Researchers have proposed that *Cuscuta* may use these stolen genes to enhance its parasitic success, for example, by interfering with host defense responses or better manipulating host nutrient transport pathways [[71](https://www.biotechniques.com/plant-climate-science/parasitic-plants-stealing-host-genes-to-use-against-them/), [73](https://www.earth.com/plants/parasitic-plants-steal-genetic-material/)]. A compelling piece of evidence for this comes from microRNAs (miRNAs) produced by *Cuscuta campestris*, which have been shown to target and silence host mRNAs, effectively using the host's own gene expression machinery against it [[45](https://sites.psu.edu/axtell/publications/)]. This demonstrates a sophisticated level of molecular mimicry and manipulation made possible by HGT. Interestingly, the *Cuscuta* genome itself contains genes of bacterial origin, specifically homologs of the *mikimopine synthase (mis)* gene from *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* [[67](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338391661_Parasitic_plant_dodder_Cuscuta_spp_A_new_natural_Agrobacterium-to-plant_horizontal_gene_transfer_species)]. This finding suggests that *Cuscuta*, while being a recipient of HGT, may also have acted as a vector, picking up bacterial genes from its environment and integrating them into its own genome [[69](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31902033/)].

Another major group of plant parasites, the root-infecting Striga and Orobanche species (witchweeds and broomrapes), also engages in HGT with its hosts. These parasites are devastating agricultural pests, causing annual crop losses estimated at US $53 million in Kenya alone and yield losses of up to 100% under severe infestation in sub-Saharan Africa [[36](https://scispace.com/pdf/striga-infestation-in-kenya-status-distribution-and-12qgtxuky3.pdf), [131](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11302897/)]. The availability of the *Striga asiatica* genome sequence provided direct evidence for HGT from host plants, including the acquisition of both protein-coding genes and retrotransposons [[42](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982219310103)]. This indicates a continuous gene flow from host to parasite. Comparative analysis of *Striga*, *Cuscuta*, and the unrelated holoparasitic plant *Rafflesia* revealed convergent patterns of HGT, suggesting that despite their independent evolutionary origins, these parasites have faced similar selective pressures and have converged on acquiring similar sets of genes from their hosts to optimize their parasitic lifestyle [[32](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ppp3.10370)]. The interaction is a dynamic co-evolutionary arms race; for example, the cereal plant sorghum secretes a molecule, LGS1, into the soil that acts as a germination stimulant for the parasitic plant *Striga hermonthica*, helping it locate a suitable host [[62](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214662820300669)]. While this is not an HGT event itself, it exemplifies the deep molecular interplay occurring at the host-parasite interface. The identification of quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with resistance to *Striga* in crops like maize highlights the ongoing struggle and the importance of understanding these molecular dialogues for developing resistant cultivars [[80](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12870-024-05590-8), [81](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7925482/), [83](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11411799/)]. The table below summarizes key findings of HGT involving parasitic plants.

| Organism | Donor/Acceptor | Genetic Material | Specific Genes/Elements | Functional Implication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

In summary, the intimate physical contact afforded by the haustorial connection is the fundamental driver of extensive HGT between parasitic plants and their hosts. This process involves the transfer of various genetic materials, including mitochondrial and nuclear DNA, as well as RNA. Well-studied genera like *Cuscuta* and *Striga* provide numerous examples of functional gene acquisitions that appear to enhance the parasite's ability to exploit its host. The discovery of these events challenges traditional views of plant evolution as strictly vertical and highlights the dynamic nature of plant genomes shaped by interactions with neighboring organisms.

## Bidirectional Exchange: The Impact of Fungal-Plant Genetic Interactions

While parasitism provides a direct conduit for genetic exchange, the relationship between fungi and plants, encompassing both pathogenic and symbiotic interactions, is another significant arena for horizontal gene transfer. Unlike the well-documented, ongoing HGT in parasitic systems, the evidence for fungal-plant gene flow suggests that these events are far rarer but profoundly impactful when they do occur [[132](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/), [133](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19584142/)]. Phylogenomic analyses indicate that while genetic exchange between angiosperms and fungi is exceedingly rare, it has occurred throughout evolutionary history, contributing to the adaptation and diversification of both kingdoms [[132](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/)]. These transfers are often ancient, having taken place over tens or hundreds of millions of years, making them difficult to detect without advanced genomic and phylogenetic tools [[133](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19584142/)]. The evidence reveals a pattern of bidirectional gene flow, where both fungi and plants have served as donors and recipients, showcasing a complex and dynamic history of genetic borrowing.

One of the most celebrated and well-characterized examples of fungus-to-plant HGT is the transfer of the *Fhb7* gene, which confers resistance to Fusarium Head Blight (FHB) in wheat (*Triticum aestivum*) [[149](https://plantae.org/horizontal-gene-transfer-of-fhb7-from-fungus-underlies-fusarium-head-blight-resistance-in-wheat-science/)]. This single gene was horizontally transferred from an ancestor of the head blight-causing fungus *Fusarium* to the ancestor of wheat [[149](https://plantae.org/horizontal-gene-transfer-of-fhb7-from-fungus-underlies-fusarium-head-blight-resistance-in-wheat-science/)]. The *Fhb7* gene encodes a glutathione S-transferase (GST) enzyme that plays a crucial role in detoxifying trichothecene mycotoxins, such as deoxynivalenol (DON), which are produced by *Fusarium* species [[152](https://europepmc.org/article/med/32273397), [153](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32273397/)]. By chemically modifying DON through de-epoxidation, the FHB7 protein neutralizes its toxicity, thereby protecting the wheat plant from disease [[150](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?params=/context/usdaarsfacpub/article/3346/&path_info=Wang_SCIENCE_2020_Horizontal_gene_USDA.pdf)]. The discovery of this gene, cloned from the related wild grass *Thinopyrum elongatum*, was a landmark finding, revealing how a plant can acquire a powerful defensive weapon directly from its fungal enemy [[151](https://graingenes.org/GG3/node/902)]. This HGT event has had a monumental impact on agriculture, as the *Fhb7* gene is now a cornerstone of modern breeding programs aimed at developing durable, chemical-free resistance to one of the world's most destructive cereal diseases [[149](https://plantae.org/horizontal-gene-transfer-of-fhb7-from-fungus-underlies-fusarium-head-blight-resistance-in-wheat-science/)]. The presence of this gene in the Triticeae tribe (wheat, barley, rye) is a testament to its strong selective advantage.

Conversely, there is compelling evidence that plants can also be the source of genes for fungi. A striking example comes from the interaction between the soil-borne fungus *Verticillium* and its host, the tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*). The fungus produces effector proteins that manipulate host physiology, and one such effector, Ave1, triggers a strong immune response in tomato plants carrying the corresponding resistance (R) gene, *Ve1* [[96](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1119623109)]. Phylogenetic analysis of the *Ave1* gene and its surrounding genomic region strongly suggests that *Ave1* was not present in the fungal lineage ancestrally but was instead acquired from a plant ancestor through horizontal transfer [[96](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1119623109)]. Remarkably, researchers demonstrated that transiently expressing Ave1 in tomato cells was sufficient to trigger immunity, confirming its function as a plant-derived immune elicitor [[96](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1119623109)]. This finding illustrates a fascinating reversal of roles: a pathogen has hijacked a host-derived molecule and repurposed it as a virulence factor to promote infection. This bidirectional capability—whereby fungi can both donate and receive genes from plants—is further supported by broader genomic surveys. Analyses of various fungal and plant genomes have identified multiple instances of inter-kingdom transfer. For example, one study identified seven genes of fungal origin that were transferred to plants, while another identified 19 genes of plant or bacterial origin that were transferred into the genome of the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus *Rhizophagus irregularis* [[38](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5982333/), [39](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2018.00701/full)]. Another study uncovered a horizontal transfer of a subtilisin protease gene from plants into an ancestor of the plant pathogenic fungus *Colletotrichum*, which causes anthracnose diseases [[154](http://fgblab.org/publications/)]. Additionally, phylogenetic analyses of fungal enzymes known as type III polyketide synthases (PKSs) have revealed multiple transfers from bacteria to fungi, highlighting the broader theme of metabolic gene exchange among microbes and plants [[112](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2019.03018/full)].

Despite the clear evidence for past HGT events, the precise mechanisms facilitating the transfer of large DNA segments between filamentous fungi and plants remain a subject of intense research. Unlike the haustorium of parasitic plants, no specific anatomical structure analogous to the haustorium has been identified in the fungal-plant interface that would allow for the direct cytoplasmic mixing required for HGT. It is hypothesized that transfer occurs during periods of intimate contact, such as in pathogenic infections or in beneficial symbioses like mycorrhizae, where fungal hyphae grow into or alongside plant cells [[128](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/9781118953297.refs)]. During such interactions, DNA released from dying fungal or plant cells could be taken up by the partner organism and integrated into its genome by error-prone DNA repair mechanisms. The rarity of these events suggests that such transfers are inefficient and likely require specific cellular conditions to overcome the formidable barriers of cell walls and membranes. The fact that many of the identified transfers are ancient implies that once a gene is successfully integrated and fixed in a population, it can persist for vast evolutionary timescales if it provides a selective advantage [[133](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19584142/)].

The functional consequences of these rare but significant HGT events are profound. The acquisition of the *Fhb7* gene provided wheat with a novel biochemical pathway for detoxifying a major class of fungal toxins, fundamentally altering its defense strategy and enabling it to thrive in environments contaminated with *Fusarium* [[149](https://plantae.org/horizontal-gene-transfer-of-fhb7-from-fungus-underlies-fusarium-head-blight-resistance-in-wheat-science/)]. Similarly, the acquisition of host-derived effector genes by pathogens like *Verticillium* and *Phytophthora* represents a sophisticated evolutionary tactic to manipulate host processes, suppress immunity, and establish infection [[16](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2012.00050/full), [96](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1119623109)]. Even in symbiotic relationships, gene transfer can play a critical role. For example, the ability of mycorrhizal fungi to transfer substantial amounts of nitrogen to their host plants is a key benefit of this mutualism [[128](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/9781118953297.refs)]. While the direct link to HGT in this context is not specified in the provided sources, the transfer of genes related to nutrient exchange or symbiotic signaling could be a plausible mechanism. In conclusion, the genetic relationship between fungi and plants is characterized by rare but consequential episodes of horizontal gene transfer. These events, though ancient and infrequent, have played a pivotal role in shaping the evolution of defense mechanisms, virulence strategies, and symbiotic capabilities in both kingdoms. The discovery of genes like *Fhb7* and *Ave1* provides concrete proof of this inter-kingdom genetic "theft," demonstrating that the evolutionary trajectory of plants and fungi is not solely written in their vertical lineages but has also been influenced by lateral exchanges across the tree of life.

## Nature's Genetic Engineer: Agrobacterium-Mediated Transfers in Plants

The bacterium *Agrobacterium*, particularly *A. tumefaciens*, is renowned in biotechnology as "nature's genetic engineer" due to its remarkable ability to transfer a segment of its own DNA, the T-DNA (Transferred DNA), from its tumor-inducing (Ti) plasmid into the genome of a host plant cell [[11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/hairy-root-disease), [75](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279862899_Agrobacterium_tumefaciens_Gene_Transfer_How_a_Plant_Pathogen_Hacks_the_Nuclei_of_Plant_and_Nonplant_Organisms)]. This natural process of inter-kingdom horizontal gene transfer has been harnessed for decades to create genetically modified plants. However, beyond its laboratory application, compelling evidence exists for the occurrence of *Agrobacterium*-to-plant HGT in nature, with some of the most significant examples predating human agriculture [[156](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321273377_Horizontal_Gene_Transfer_Contributes_to_Plant_Evolution_The_Case_of_Agrobacterium_T-DNAs)]. These natural transfer events have resulted in the stable integration of bacterial genes into the chromosomes of certain plant species, where they are actively expressed and have had profound effects on plant evolution and domestication.

The mechanism underlying *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation is a highly evolved biological process. When the bacterium infects a wound site on a plant, it recognizes specific plant-derived compounds, such as phenolics, which activate the Ti plasmid's virulence (Vir) genes [[75](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279862899_Agrobacterium_tumefaciens_Gene_Transfer_How_a_Plant_Pathogen_Hacks_the_Nuclei_of_Plant_and_Nonplant_Organisms)]. The Vir proteins then process the T-DNA region of the plasmid and assemble a protein-DNA complex that is transported through a pilus-like structure into the plant cell. Once inside the plant cytoplasm, the T-DNA is protected from degradation and trafficked to the nucleus, where it integrates randomly into the host chromosome [[106](https://www.iab.kit.edu/conference/img/Abstract_Book_XI_Int_Fung_Biol_Cong.pdf), [108](https://dokumen.pub/plant-physiology-theory-and-applications-2nbsped-1108486398-9781108486392.html)]. The T-DNA carries genes that encode enzymes for the biosynthesis of plant growth hormones (auxins and cytokinins) and a unique carbon source called opines, which the transformed *Agrobacterium* can metabolize but most other organisms cannot [[75](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279862899_Agrobacterium_tumefaciens_Gene_Transfer_How_a_Plant_Pathogen_Hacks_the_Nuclei_of_Plant_and_Nonplant_Organisms)]. This genetic takeover leads to uncontrolled cell division in the plant, resulting in the formation of a crown gall tumor, which provides a nutrient-rich environment for the bacteria [[11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/hairy-root-disease)].

The most striking natural example of this process is found in the cultivated hexaploid sweetpotato (*Ipomoea batatas*) [[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)]. Genomic analysis of sweetpotato has revealed two distinct regions containing DNA sequences derived from *Agrobacterium*. These regions, named Ib T-DNA1 and Ib T-DNA2, are considered ancient HGT events and represent a permanent feature of the sweetpotato genome [[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)]. Ib T-DNA1 is a partial inverted repeat containing several genes homologous to those responsible for auxin biosynthesis in lab-induced tumors, including *C-prot*, *Acs*, *iaaH*, and *iaaM* [[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)]. Ib T-DNA2 is a larger region (~11,187 bp) containing seven open reading frames homologous to rol (root locus) genes, such as *RolB/RolC*, which are known to affect cell division and differentiation [[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)]. The integration sites of these T-DNA regions are noteworthy. Ib T-DNA1 is inserted into an F-box gene on chromosome 3, while Ib T-DNA2 is located within the coding sequence of a mitochondrial substrate carrier family protein gene (*UcpB*) on chromosome 7, causing a local deletion of 893 base pairs [[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)]. This precise mapping demonstrates that the integration did not disrupt essential genes in a catastrophic manner but rather became a stable part of the genome.

The age of these HGT events has been estimated through phylogenetic analysis. The high nucleotide sequence identity (98.8%) between the two copies of Ib T-DNA1 in the sweetpotato cultivar Xu781 corresponds to a very low divergence rate, suggesting that the integration event occurred approximately 1.7 million years ago [[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)]. This timing is significant because it places the transfer well before the domestication of sweetpotato by humans, which began around 8,000 years ago [[13](https://laidbackgardener.blog/2017/02/16/the-sweet-potato-is-genetically-modified/)]. Further evidence supporting an ancient origin comes from the distribution of these T-DNA regions. They are not exclusive to the cultivated hexaploid species but are also found in its wild relatives within the *Ipomoea* series Batatas, including tetraploid *I. batatas* and *I. cordatotriloba* [[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)]. This confirms that the HGT event predated domestication and was subsequently inherited and selected for during cultivation [[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)].

Crucially, the integrated T-DNA sequences in sweetpotato are not silent relics; they are actively expressed within the plant [[13](https://laidbackgardener.blog/2017/02/16/the-sweet-potato-is-genetically-modified/)]. Based on this evidence, researchers have proposed a compelling hypothesis for the evolutionary impact of this HGT event. The introduction of bacterial genes that influence plant hormone pathways likely caused the storage roots of the ancestral wild *Ipomoea* species to become enlarged and starchy—a key trait that defines the modern sweetpotato [[13](https://laidbackgardener.blog/2017/02/16/the-sweet-potato-is-genetically-modified/)]. This morphological change would have made the roots more nutritious and palatable, attracting early human gatherers. As humans began to cultivate these plants, they inadvertently selected for individuals with the largest, sweetest tubers, leading to the dramatic domestication and global spread of sweetpotato [[13](https://laidbackgardener.blog/2017/02/16/the-sweet-potato-is-genetically-modified/)]. In this scenario, a natural HGT event from *Agrobacterium* provided the raw genetic variation that allowed a wild, inedible tuber to evolve into one of the world's most important staple crops. This represents a stunning example of how HGT can drive major evolutionary transitions and shape the course of human history.

The case of sweetpotato provides definitive proof of a natural, ancient HGT event from this bacterium to a plant lineage. The integration and expression of T-DNA genes have been directly linked to a key morphological trait—the enlarged, starchy root—that was central to the domestication of one of humanity's most vital food crops. This discovery transforms our view of *Agrobacterium* from a simple plant pathogen into a participant in the deep evolutionary history of flowering plants.

## Viral Integration and Vector Potential in Plant Genomes

Viruses, as obligate intracellular parasites, represent another significant source of genetic material that can enter and persist within plant genomes, contributing to the landscape of horizontal gene transfer. Unlike the direct cellular connections seen in parasitism or the targeted DNA injection by *Agrobacterium*, viral-mediated HGT operates through a different set of mechanisms, primarily centered on the integration of viral DNA into the host's nuclear genome. This process gives rise to Endogenous Viral Elements (EVEs), which are essentially viral fossils embedded within the host's chromosomes. These integrated sequences can be passed vertically to subsequent generations, becoming a permanent part of the plant's genetic heritage. Furthermore, the capacity of viruses to package and transport foreign genetic material raises the intriguing possibility that they could act as vectors for transferring host genes between different plant species, although direct evidence for this is still emerging.

The integration of viral genomes into host DNA is a well-documented phenomenon, particularly for certain families of plant viruses. The most prominent examples come from pararetroviruses, which have a DNA-based replication cycle that involves a reverse transcription step. This group includes the Badnaviruses and Caulimoviruses, members of the family *Caulimoviridae* [[27](https://bsppjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/mpp.13019), [88](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/recombinant)]. During a typical infection, these viruses produce an RNA intermediate that is reverse-transcribed into DNA, which can then integrate into the host chromosome [[28](https://www.b3nz.org.nz/projects/integrated-or-not-is-this-badnavirus-viable-and-what-is-the-risk/)]. While this integration is not part of their standard replication cycle, it is believed to occur when viral DNA intermediates gain access to the host nucleus and are mistakenly incorporated into the chromosomal DNA by the cell's own repair machinery [[28](https://www.b3nz.org.nz/projects/integrated-or-not-is-this-badnavirus-viable-and-what-is-the-risk/), [104](https://www.deutsche-botanische-gesellschaft.de/en/kategorie/promoting-young-researchers)]. Once integrated, these viral sequences are referred to as Endogenous Plant Pararetroviruses (EPRVs) [[65](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2898222/)]. These EPRVs are found in the genomes of a wide range of plant species, from banana to rice, and can exist in various forms, including complete or fragmented genomes [[24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4926197/), [87](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12147756/)]. Their presence is a testament to countless past infections where the viral DNA was not cleared but instead became a permanent resident of the host genome.

The functional consequences of these viral integrations are varied and can be either detrimental or beneficial to the host plant. On the negative side, integration can disrupt the function of host genes. For example, insertions of badnavirus-derived sequences have been found to disrupt orthologs of late blight resistance genes (*R1*) in the genome of cultivated potatoes, potentially compromising the plant's innate immunity [[25](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2021.683681/full)]. Such disruptions highlight the parasitic nature of these elements and their potential to cause deleterious mutations. However, over evolutionary time, some of these integrated viral sequences have been co-opted by the host for its own benefit, a process known as exaptation. There is growing evidence that EPRVs can be repurposed by the host plant to mount an antiviral defense. The expression of these integrated viral sequences can lead to the production of small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) that guide the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway to recognize and degrade RNA from related, currently active viruses [[115](https://theses.hal.science/tel-02969504v1/file/HOUE_Vincent_2019.pdf)]. This effectively turns a past viral infection into a form of adaptive immunity, providing the plant with resistance to future attacks by similar pathogens [[155](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.00408/full)]. This mechanism represents a sophisticated way for plants to leverage the genetic legacy of viruses for their own protection.

Beyond their role as passive passengers in the genome, viruses also possess the theoretical potential to act as vectors for the inter-species transfer of host chromosomal DNA. The principle is straightforward: if a virus infecting Plant A packages a fragment of Plant A's DNA along with its own viral genome, and this hybrid viral particle subsequently infects Plant B, it could deliver the Plant A DNA fragment into the genome of Plant B. This would constitute a true horizontal gene transfer event between two plant species, mediated by the virus. While this concept is plausible, direct, empirically verified evidence for such a process occurring naturally in plants has been challenging to obtain. However, recent experimental work has begun to document the initial steps of this hypothetical pathway. In one study investigating viral infection in sugar beet, researchers reported the generation of circular DNA hybrids composed of both viral and host chromosomal sequences [[78](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6293997/)]. This observation provides a snapshot of what could be the first stage of a virus-mediated transfer, where host DNA is excised and packaged into a viral capsid [[79](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329501532_Virus-mediated_export_of_chromosomal_DNA_in_plants)]. While this study did not demonstrate successful integration into a new host species, it offers crucial proof-of-concept that the necessary molecular events can occur.

The existence of EPRVs distributed across diverse plant lineages strongly suggests that viral-mediated integration is a recurring phenomenon in nature [[26](https://www.mdpi.com/2218-273X/13/7/1069)]. For example, badnaviral sequences are found distributed throughout the banana genome, indicating a long and complex association between the virus and its host [[27](https://bsppjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/mpp.13019)]. The integrated sequences of Banana Streak Virus (BSV), known as endogenous BSV (eBSV), are present in the polyploid genome of cultivated bananas (*Musa acuminata*) and can be reactivated under certain conditions, producing infectious virus particles [[97](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-019-0288-7)]. This shows that integrated viral genomes can retain their functionality over long evolutionary periods. The table below summarizes the key aspects of viral integration and its potential role in HGT.

In conclusion, viruses are a dual-nature force in the context of plant HGT. On one hand, they act as persistent guests, integrating their genomes into the host chromosome to form endogenous viral elements. These EVEs can have lasting negative effects through gene disruption or be turned into a powerful asset for the host's immune system through RNAi-mediated defense. On the other hand, viruses hold the potential to act as natural vectors, capable of shuttling genetic material between different plant species. While direct evidence for this inter-species gene transfer remains elusive, the documented ability of viruses to package host DNA provides a strong mechanistic basis for this phenomenon. Together, these roles underscore the significant contribution of viruses to the genetic diversity and evolutionary trajectory of plants.
