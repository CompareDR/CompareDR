# Horizontal Gene Transfer in the Plant World: Gene Exchange Through Parasitic and Symbiotic Relationships

**Abstract**

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)—the movement of genetic material between organisms outside of traditional vertical (parent-to-offspring) inheritance—has increasingly been recognized as a significant evolutionary force in plants. This report systematically compiles findings on HGT in the plant world, focusing on discoveries made before 2021, organized around four biological pathways: (1) gene exchange between parasitic plants and their hosts via haustorial connections, where *Cuscuta* species transfer mRNA bidirectionally with hosts and have acquired over 108 functional genes through historical HGT, while *Striga hermonthica* has incorporated nuclear genes from its sorghum host; (2) gene transfer between fungi and plants, which evidence suggests is predominantly unidirectional (fungi-to-plant), with notable cases including the *Fhb7* gene from a fungal endophyte conferring Fusarium head blight resistance in wheat, and a β-1,6-glucanase gene transferred from an *Epichloë*-like ancestor to cool-season grasses approximately 9–13 million years ago; (3) *Agrobacterium*-mediated T-DNA transfer to plants, exemplified by the sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) genome which naturally contains two distinct T-DNA insertions (IbT-DNA1 and IbT-DNA2) with functional bacterial genes; and (4) viral sequences integrated into plant genomes as endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs), found in banana, petunia, rice, and yam, though evidence for viruses acting as natural carriers facilitating gene transfer between different plant species remains limited. Together, these findings reveal that parasitic and symbiotic relationships create physical bridges that enable cross-species gene movement, fundamentally shaping plant evolution.

## Background and Basic Concepts

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT), also called lateral gene transfer (LGT), refers to the movement of genetic material between organisms in ways other than traditional vertical reproduction. In prokaryotes, HGT is well established as a major evolutionary mechanism mediated by transformation, conjugation, and transduction. In eukaryotes—and particularly in flowering plants (angiosperms)—the phenomenon was long considered exceedingly rare. However, accumulating genomic evidence over the past two decades has revealed that HGT has occurred more frequently in plants than previously appreciated, especially when mediated by the intimate physical contacts characteristic of parasitic, symbiotic, and pathogenic relationships.

Several key features define plant HGT:

- **Physical proximity is a prerequisite**: The close cell-to-cell contact established by parasitic haustoria, fungal hyphae growing intercellularly, bacterial infection structures, or viral replication within plant cells creates opportunities for DNA or RNA exchange that would not occur between free-living organisms.
- **Both nuclear and organellar genomes are involved**: While early discoveries focused on mitochondrial and chloroplast gene transfers, nuclear gene transfers—though rarer—have now been documented in multiple lineages.
- **Transferred genes can be functional**: Some horizontally acquired genes are expressed and subject to selection, suggesting they may confer adaptive advantages to the recipient.
- **Time scales vary enormously**: Some transfers occurred tens of millions of years ago and have been fixed across entire lineages; others are detectable in only a single species or genus, suggesting more recent events.

The concept of "natural transgenic organisms" (nGMOs) has emerged from this research, recognizing that some plants carry foreign DNA acquired through natural HGT processes rather than laboratory genetic engineering. Sweet potato is the most prominent example, but genomic surveys suggest the phenomenon may be far more widespread than initially recognized.

This report examines four pathways of HGT involving plants, each mediated by a different type of biological interaction: parasitic plant–host connections, fungal–plant symbiosis/pathogenesis, bacterial infection, and viral integration. For each pathway, specific species names, gene names, and mechanisms are discussed, with a primary focus on discoveries reported before 2021.

## 1. Between Parasitic Plants: Host–Parasite Gene Exchange

Parasitic plants develop specialized invasive organs called **haustoria** (singular: haustorium) that penetrate host tissues and establish vascular connections, allowing the parasite to draw water, nutrients, and—at the molecular level—genetic material from the host. This intimate physical bridge creates one of the most direct pathways for HGT in the plant kingdom.

### 1.1 *Cuscuta* (Dodder): mRNA and DNA Transfer via Haustoria

*Cuscuta* (commonly known as dodder) is a genus of stem-parasitic plants in the family Convolvulaceae. Lacking roots and leaves, *Cuscuta* species wrap thread-like stems around host plants and insert haustoria into host vascular tissue. Research published before 2021 revealed *Cuscuta* as a remarkable system for studying HGT.

**Bidirectional mRNA exchange:**

- In a landmark 2014 study published in *Science*, Kim et al. demonstrated that *Cuscuta pentagona* (five-angled dodder) exchanges mRNA molecules bidirectionally with its host plants, including *Arabidopsis thaliana* and tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*). [1](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1253111)
- The study showed that thousands of mRNA transcripts move between *Cuscuta* and its hosts in both directions—from host to parasite and from parasite to host.
- Approximately **half of the host transcriptome** could be detected in the parasite's tissues, indicating massive scale of exchange.
- This mRNA movement occurs through the haustorial connections and may allow the parasite to sense host physiology and manipulate host gene expression.

**Genomic DNA transfer and functional gene acquisition:**

- A 2019 study published in *Nature Plants* by Yang et al. identified **108 functional HGT events** in *Cuscuta* genomes, along with 42 host-derived transposon or pseudogene regions. [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-019-0458-0)
- These horizontally transferred genes include **Strictosidine synthase-like (SSL) genes**—involved in alkaloid biosynthesis.
- **Albumin 1** genes—storage protein genes—were also acquired.
- **hAT transposon** sequences—mobile genetic elements—were identified as transferred.
- **Leucine-rich repeat receptor-like kinase (LRR-RLK)** genes—involved in signaling—were among the acquired genes.
- Critically, the study demonstrated that these transfers involved **genomic DNA**, not reverse-transcribed RNA products, based on the presence of intron sequences that matched the donor species.
- **18 of these functional HGT events** were independently acquired by both *Cuscuta* and parasitic plants in the family Orobanchaceae (e.g., *Striga*, *Orobanche*), representing convergent evolution of gene acquisition through parasitism.

**MicroRNA-mediated host manipulation:**

- Shahid et al. (2018, *Nature*) showed that *Cuscuta* produces microRNAs (miRNAs) that can transit into host plants and target specific host mRNAs. [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature25027)
- These parasite-derived miRNAs can suppress host defense genes, facilitating the parasite's ability to extract resources.
- This represents a form of "horizontal transfer" of regulatory molecules, though it involves functional RNAs rather than stably inherited DNA.

### 1.2 *Striga*: Nuclear Gene Acquisition from Hosts

*Striga hermonthica* (purple witchweed) is a root-parasitic plant in the family Orobanchaceae that devastates cereal crops in sub-Saharan Africa. It parasitizes grasses (Poaceae), including sorghum (*Sorghum bicolor*), maize (*Zea mays*), and rice (*Oryza sativa*).

**Nuclear gene transfer from host to parasite:**

- In a 2010 study published in *Science*, Yoshida et al. revealed that the *Striga hermonthica* genome contains a gene (identified as **ShContig9483** and related sequences) that was acquired from its monocotyledonous host, sorghum. [4](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1187145)
- This gene showed high sequence similarity to sorghum nuclear genes and was absent from non-parasitic relatives of *Striga*, confirming its horizontal rather than vertical origin.
- The transferred gene is a **strictosidine synthase-like (SSL)** gene, involved in the biosynthesis of terpenoid indole alkaloids—compounds that may play roles in defense or host interaction.

**Implications:**

- The *Striga* case demonstrates that HGT is not limited to mRNA movement but extends to **stable integration of host nuclear genes** into the parasite genome.
- The independent acquisition of SSL genes in both *Cuscuta* (Convolvulaceae) and *Striga* (Orobanchaceae)—two distantly related parasitic plant lineages—suggests that these genes may confer selective advantages for parasitic lifestyles, possibly by enabling the synthesis of defense-related or host-manipulating compounds.

### 1.3 Transferable Material and Structural Mechanisms

The mechanisms by which parasitic plants exchange genes with hosts involve several layers:

- **Haustorial bridge**: The haustorium creates a direct cytoplasmic and vascular connection between parasite and host, allowing movement of macromolecules including mRNA, proteins, and potentially DNA.
- **mRNA movement**: Demonstrated in *Cuscuta*–host interactions; thousands of transcripts traverse the haustorial connection bidirectionally. This is a transient exchange (mRNA is not stably inherited unless reverse-transcribed and integrated).
- **Genomic DNA integration**: Documented in both *Cuscuta* (108 functional genes) and *Striga* (sorghum-derived genes). The mechanism of DNA transfer and integration is less clear but may involve cell death and DNA release at the haustorial interface, followed by uptake and integration into the parasite genome; transit through plasmodesmata or vascular connections; or possible involvement of transposable elements as vehicles for gene mobilization.
- **Organellar genome transfer**: Mitochondrial gene transfers between parasitic plants and hosts have also been documented. Davis and Xi (2015) reviewed evidence for HGT in parasitic plants and noted that organelle genomes—particularly mitochondria—are especially prone to exchange during parasitic interactions. [5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pbi.2015.01.008)
- ***Amborella trichopoda*** as a model for organelle HGT: Rice et al. (2013, *Science*) showed that the mitochondrial genome of *Amborella*, the basalmost living angiosperm, has acquired entire genomes from green algae, mosses, and other angiosperms through "mitochondrial fusion" events, possibly facilitated by epiphytic or parasitic contacts. [6](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1246275)

**Key summary for Section 1:**

| Parasitic Plant | Host | Material Transferred | Direction | Key Genes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Cuscuta pentagona* | *Arabidopsis*, tomato | mRNA (thousands of transcripts) | Bidirectional | Multiple host transcripts |
| *Cuscuta campestris* | Various dicots | Genomic DNA (108 genes) | Host to Parasite | SSL, Albumin 1, hAT, LRR-RLK |
| *Striga hermonthica* | Sorghum | Nuclear DNA | Host to Parasite | ShContig9483, SSL genes |
| *Cuscuta* spp. | Various hosts | miRNA | Parasite to Host | Defense-targeting miRNAs |

## 2. Between Fungi and Plants: Unidirectional or Bidirectional?

Fungi and plants have coexisted for hundreds of millions of years, engaging in mutualistic symbioses (e.g., mycorrhizae, endophytic associations) and antagonistic pathogenesis (e.g., fungal diseases). The persistent intimate contact between fungal hyphae and plant cells raises the question of whether gene transfer occurs and in which direction.

### 2.1 Fungi to Plants: Disease Resistance and Cell Wall–Degradation Genes

Several well-documented cases of gene transfer from fungi to plants have been reported, all involving genes that appear to confer adaptive advantages:

**The *Fhb7* gene: Fungal endophyte to wheat**

- In a high-profile 2020 study published in *Science*, Wang et al. reported that the **Fhb7** gene, which confers broad-spectrum resistance to Fusarium head blight (FHB) in wheat, originated from a fungal endophyte. [7](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba5435)
- *Fhb7* was identified in *Thinopyrum elongatum* (tall wheatgrass), a wild relative of wheat, and was shown to encode a **glutathione S-transferase (GST)** enzyme.
- Phylogenetic analysis indicated that *Fhb7* was horizontally transferred from an **endophytic fungus** of the genus *Epichloë* (or a closely related lineage) to the ancestor of *Thinopyrum*.
- The GST enzyme detoxifies trichothecene mycotoxins produced by *Fusarium* pathogens, explaining the broad-spectrum resistance.
- When transferred to wheat through conventional breeding, *Fhb7* significantly reduced FHB severity and mycotoxin accumulation.

**The β-1,6-glucanase gene (LpBGNL): Fungal endophyte to cool-season grasses**

- In a 2017 study published in *Scientific Reports*, Shinozuka et al. provided the first evidence for horizontal transfer of a **fungal nuclear gene** to an angiosperm lineage. [8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-07886-2)
- The gene, designated **LpBGNL** (*Lolium perenne* β-1,6-Glucanase-Like), was identified in perennial ryegrass (*Lolium perenne* L.), a cool-season grass species.
- *LpBGNL* shows 74–90% DNA sequence identity to β-1,6-glucanase genes of fungal endophytes, including *Epichloë festucae*, *Hypocrea lixii*, and *Trichoderma harzianum*.
- The gene encodes an enzyme that degrades **β-1,6-glucan**, a polysaccharide found specifically in fungal cell walls.
- *LpBGNL* orthologues were found in members of two Poeae sub-tribes—**Loliinae** (*Lolium temulentum*, *Festuca pratensis*, *Festuca arundinacea*, *Festuca ovina*) and **Dactylidinae** (*Dactylis glomerata*, *Dactylis marina*)—but NOT in more distantly related grasses such as *Poa* or *Phalaris*.
- The transfer was estimated to have occurred approximately **9–13 million years ago**, in the common ancestor of Loliinae and Dactylidinae, from a species ancestral to contemporary grass-associated *Epichloë* endophytes.
- The gene is actively expressed, with higher expression in roots and flowers, and shows evidence of purifying selection (Ks/Ka < 1), suggesting it is functional and potentially involved in defense against fungal pathogens.

**Other fungi-to-plant HGT candidates:**

- A systematic phylogenomic study by Richards et al. (2009, *Plant Cell*) examined four completely sequenced angiosperm genomes (*Arabidopsis thaliana*, rice, sorghum, and poplar) and found **no evidence for fungal-to-angiosperm HGT**, though they identified two and three reliable HGT events in moss (bryophyte) and lycophyte lineages, respectively. [9](https://academic.oup.com/plcell/article/21/7/1897/6093170)
- This apparent contradiction with later findings (such as *LpBGNL*) was resolved by recognizing that fungal-to-plant HGT can be **highly lineage-specific**—restricted to small clades—and would not be detected in surveys of distantly related model genomes.
- The mitochondrial **cox1 group I intron** in flowering plants represents another ancient fungal-to-plant transfer, having been acquired from a fungal species and subsequently spread among angiosperm lineages through plant-to-plant horizontal transfer. [10](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msn126)
- In *Amborella trichopoda*, the mitochondrial genome contains genes acquired from fungi (as well as green algae, mosses, and other angiosperms), demonstrating multi-kingdom HGT at the organellar level. [6](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1246275)

### 2.2 Plants to Fungi: Evidence and Limitations

Documented cases of gene transfer from plants to fungi are substantially rarer than the reverse direction:

- The Richards et al. (2009) systematic study used phylogenomic approaches to detect HGT between plants and fungi and found the pattern to be **"rare and ancient,"** with events detectable primarily in early-diverging land plant lineages (mosses, lycophytes) rather than in angiosperms. [9](https://academic.oup.com/plcell/article/21/7/1897/6093170)
- While the study identified candidate HGT events in both directions (fungi to plants and plants to fungi) in non-angiosperm lineages, **no convincing plant-to-fungus nuclear gene transfer has been documented in angiosperms** as of the pre-2021 literature.
- Fungal pathogens and endophytes certainly acquire plant-derived molecules (sugars, amino acids, hormones) during infection, but **stable integration of plant nuclear genes into fungal genomes has not been convincingly demonstrated** in the way that fungal-to-plant transfers have.
- One possible explanation: the physical asymmetry of infection—fungal hyphae grow *into* plant tissues, potentially releasing fungal DNA into plant cells through cell death and lysis, but the reverse (plant DNA entering and integrating into fungal nuclei) is less mechanically plausible given the fungal cell wall and the fact that fungal nuclei are not directly exposed to plant cytoplasm.

### 2.3 Directionality Assessment

Based on the available pre-2021 evidence:

| Direction | Documented Cases | Strength of Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Fungi to Plants | *Fhb7* (wheat), *LpBGNL* (ryegrass), cox1 intron (many angiosperms), *Amborella* mitochondrial genes | Strong (multiple independent cases, phylogenetic confirmation) |
| Plants to Fungi | Candidate events in moss/licophyte lineages (Richards et al. 2009); no confirmed angiosperm cases | Weak (no specific gene names, limited to early-diverging lineages) |

**Conclusion on directionality**: Gene transfer between fungi and plants appears to be **predominantly unidirectional**—from fungi to plants. This asymmetry likely reflects the physical mechanics of fungal infection and symbiosis: fungal hyphae penetrate plant tissues, fungal cells may lyse and release DNA in proximity to plant nuclei, and the intimate, prolonged contact of endophytic fungi (which grow within plant reproductive tillers and are maternally transmitted through seed) increases the probability of fungal DNA integration into the plant germline. The reverse direction (plant to fungus) is not impossible but appears to be exceedingly rare or mechanistically disadvantaged.

## 3. Between Bacteria and Plants: *Agrobacterium* T-DNA Transfer

*Agrobacterium tumefaciens* (now reclassified under the genus *Rhizobium* but commonly still referred to as *Agrobacterium*) is a soil bacterium that represents the best-understood natural genetic engineer of plants. It transfers a segment of its tumor-inducing (Ti) plasmid—called **T-DNA (transferred DNA)**—into plant cells, where it integrates into the plant nuclear genome.

### 3.1 The T-DNA Transfer Mechanism

The molecular mechanism of T-DNA transfer is one of the best-characterized HGT pathways in biology:

- **Ti plasmid**: *Agrobacterium* strains carry a large tumor-inducing (Ti) plasmid. The T-DNA region is delimited by **left border (LB)** and **right border (RB)** repeat sequences (each ~25 bp).
- **Virulence (*vir*) genes**: Upon sensing phenolic compounds (e.g., acetosyringone) and sugars released from wounded plant tissues, *Agrobacterium* activates a cascade of *vir* genes on the Ti plasmid.
- **T-strand generation**: The VirD1/VirD2 endonuclease nicks the T-DNA borders, and a single-stranded copy of the T-DNA (the "T-strand") is produced.
- **Nuclear targeting**: The T-strand is coated with VirD2 protein (which carries a nuclear localization signal) and is transported into the plant cell nucleus through the Type IV secretion system (T4SS)—a molecular syringe spanning the bacterial and plant membranes.
- **Integration**: The T-strand integrates into the plant nuclear genome, typically in transcriptionally active, AT-rich regions, through a process resembling non-homologous end joining (NHEJ).
- **T-DNA genes**: The transferred T-DNA contains **auxin biosynthesis genes** (*iaaM* and *iaaH*) that induce plant cell proliferation (crown gall formation); **cytokinin biosynthesis genes** (*ipt*/*tzs*) that promote cell division; and **opine synthesis genes** that produce novel amino acid–sugar conjugates (opines) which the bacterium can metabolize as carbon and nitrogen sources.
- ***Agrobacterium rhizogenes*** (now *Rhizobium rhizogenes*): a related species carrying a root-inducing (Ri) plasmid, transfers T-DNA containing **rol (root locus) genes** (*rolA*, *rolB*, *rolC*, *rolD*) that induce hairy root formation.

### 3.2 Sweet Potato: A Natural Transgenic Crop

The most striking example of natural *Agrobacterium*-mediated HGT in plants is the sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas* [L.] Lam.):

**Discovery (Kyndt et al., 2015, *PNAS*):**

- In 2015, Kyndt et al. reported that the genome of cultivated sweet potato contains **T-DNA sequences from *Agrobacterium***, making it the first recognized naturally transgenic food crop. [11](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)
- Two distinct T-DNA insertions were identified.

**IbT-DNA1** (corresponding to *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* TR-DNA):

- Contains four open reading frames (ORFs): **iaaM** (tryptophan 2-monooxygenase, auxin biosynthesis), **iaaH** (indoleacetamide hydrolase, auxin biosynthesis), **C-prot** (cytokinin biosynthesis-related), and **Acs** (ACC synthase, ethylene biosynthesis).
- Found in **all 291 cultivated sweet potato accessions** tested.
- **Absent** from wild *Ipomoea* relatives, suggesting the transfer occurred during or after domestication.

**IbT-DNA2** (corresponding to *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* TL-DNA):

- Contains at least five ORFs: **ORF14**, **ORF17n**, **rolB/rolC** (root-inducing genes), **ORF13**, and **ORF18**.
- The *rol* genes are known to induce hairy root formation in *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* infections.

**Gene expression:**

- At least **four genes from IbT-DNA1** and **two genes from IbT-DNA2** were shown to be actively expressed in sweet potato tissues, indicating that these are not merely silent remnants but functional genes producing proteins.
- The expression of auxin biosynthesis genes (*iaaM*, *iaaH*) may contribute to the naturally occurring phenotypic traits of sweet potato, potentially including storage root formation—a characteristic that makes sweet potato a valuable crop.

**Significance:**

- The sweet potato case demonstrates that **natural *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation has occurred in an important food crop**, and the transferred genes have been maintained through domestication.
- This finding challenges the binary distinction between "natural" and "genetically modified" organisms, as sweet potato—a universally consumed crop—carries bacterial genes acquired through a natural HGT process.
- The term **"natural GMO"** was coined to describe this phenomenon.

### 3.3 Widespread Distribution Beyond Sweet Potato

While sweet potato was the first documented case, subsequent research has revealed that natural *Agrobacterium*-mediated HGT is far more widespread:

- A research team led by Chen Ke at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (published in *The Plant Journal*) developed a bioinformatics pipeline to screen land plant genomes for *Agrobacterium*-derived T-DNA sequences. [12](https://www.csnbgsh.cn/sites/chenshan2020/static/gonggao-content.ashx?ctgid=ef3aeec8-7468-4bd1-9e3c-a2f65e15f58f)
- They detected T-DNA in **82 bryophyte species** (including 1 hornwort, 4 liverworts).
- T-DNA was also found in **5 lycophyte species**, **70 fern species**, **5 gymnosperm species**, and **2,452 angiosperm species** (with 2,338 dicots).
- This survey found T-DNA in economically important food plants including **coffee, cinnamon, strawberry, cassava, black pepper, and raspberry**, suggesting that natural *Agrobacterium*-mediated gene transfer is a pervasive phenomenon across the plant kingdom.
- These findings extend the concept of "natural transgenic plants" well beyond the original sweet potato and tobacco (*Nicotiana*) cases, indicating that *Agrobacterium*-mediated HGT has occurred recurrently throughout plant evolution.

**Key summary for Section 3:**

| Organism | T-DNA | Key Genes | Expression | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sweet potato (*I. batatas*) | IbT-DNA1 | iaaM, iaaH, C-prot, Acs | Yes (4 genes) | All 291 cultivated accessions |
| Sweet potato (*I. batatas*) | IbT-DNA2 | rolB, rolC, ORF13, ORF14, ORF17n | Yes (2 genes) | Cultivated accessions |
| Multiple land plants | Various | T-DNA-encoded proteins detected | Variable | >2,600 species across all major lineages |

## 4. Between Viruses and Plants: Viral Residents and Potential Vectors

Plant-infecting viruses, particularly DNA viruses of the family *Caulimoviridae* (pararetroviruses) and *Geminiviridae* (geminiviruses), can leave permanent genetic traces in host genomes. This section examines two questions: (1) Do viruses leave their genes inside plants after infection? (2) Can viruses act as carriers or vectors to facilitate gene transfer between different plants?

### 4.1 Endogenous Viral Elements in Plant Genomes

**Endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs):**

- Viruses of the family *Caulimoviridae* are double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses that replicate through an RNA intermediate (using reverse transcription), earning them the name "pararetroviruses." [13](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.coviro.2013.08.012)
- Unlike retroviruses, integration into the host genome is **not an essential step** in their replication cycle. Nevertheless, viral sequences have been found integrated in the nuclear genomes of a wide range of angiosperms.
- These integrated sequences are termed **endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs)** and have been discovered in multiple plant species.

**Banana (*Musa* spp.):**

- The genome of *Musa balbisiana* (a wild progenitor of cultivated bananas) contains integrated copies of **Banana streak virus (BSV)**, which belong to the genus *Badnavirus* within *Caulimoviridae*. [14](https://doi.org/10.1128/JVI.00212-08)
- Three species of BSV are found as infectious EPRVs: **BSOLV** (Banana streak Obino l'Ewai virus), **BSGFV** (Banana streak Goldfinger virus), and **BSIMV** (Banana streak Imove virus).
- These EPRVs can reconstitute functional viral genomes and trigger systemic infection under abiotic stress conditions (e.g., tissue culture, drought, cold), making banana a model system for studying EPRV activation.
- Research by Gayral et al. (2008, *Journal of Virology*) showed that a **single integration event** of BSGFV gave rise to the infectious EPRV alleles in *M. balbisiana* cv. Pisang Klutuk Wulung (PKW), with both alleles containing the full-length viral genome despite extensive rearrangement. [15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39a52ab24c1d33b9d36500b01b4cabfd9f0d9a72)
- **Banana endogenous virus sequences (BEVs)** have also been identified, representing ancient badnavirus-derived integrations. A study identified novel BEV variants (GZ5, GZ13), with GZ5 containing a complete **reverse transcriptase (RT)/RNase H** gene—the hallmark gene of retrotransposons and retroviruses. [16](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s42483-024-00256-7)

**Petunia (*Petunia hybrida*):**

- Endogenous **Petunia vein clearing virus (ePVCV-1)** sequences are integrated into the pericentromeric regions of *Petunia hybrida* chromosomes.
- ePVCV-1 is transcriptionally active, with expression increasing as plants age, and can generate infectious viral particles.
- Epigenetic regulation (DNA methylation, histone modification) controls EPRV silencing. The viral promoter tends to escape complete methylation, allowing low-level transcription that can increase under stress. [17](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267703223)

**Rice (*Oryza sativa*):**

- The rice genome contains **endogenous rice tungro bacilliform virus-like sequences (ERTBVs)**, which are preferentially inserted between **AT dinucleotide repeats (ATrs)**. [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/tpj.12002)
- ATRs appear to serve as "hot spots" for double-strand breaks and non-homologous end joining, trapping episomal DNA molecules—including viral sequences—into the genome.
- Comparison between rice subspecies (japonica and indica) confirmed preferential ERTBV insertion at ATr sites, suggesting an evolutionary mechanism for viral DNA capture.

**Yam (*Dioscorea cayenensis-rotundata* complex):**

- The genomes of West African yams contain **endogenous badnavirus sequences (eDBVs)** belonging to at least four distinct badnavirus species. [19](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.virusres.2014.01.007)
- PCR screening detected badnavirus RT-RNaseH sequences in 100% of *D. rotundata* samples tested, and sequencing confirmed these as genuine *Badnavirus* sequences.
- Serological and molecular analyses were used to distinguish between sequences amplified from viral particles and those from endogenous integrations.

**Geminivirus sequences in plant genomes:**

- While geminiviruses (single-stranded DNA viruses) replicate in the nucleus using host DNA polymerases, evidence for their stable integration into plant genomes is more limited than for caulimoviruses.
- Some studies have reported geminivirus-related sequences in plant genomes, but these are less well-characterized and may represent more recent or transient integrations.

### 4.2 Viruses as Carriers for Inter-Plant Gene Transfer

The second question—whether viruses can serve as "carriers" or "vectors" to facilitate gene transfer between different plant species—is more nuanced:

**Viruses as genetic engineering tools (not natural HGT):**

- **Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)** and **African cassava mosaic virus (ACMV)**-derived vectors have been engineered to carry and express foreign genes in plants. These are laboratory tools, not examples of natural HGT.
- **Tomato golden mosaic virus (TGMV)** and other begomoviruses have been developed as **transient expression vectors** for gene function studies, demonstrating that viral replication machinery can accommodate and amplify foreign sequences within plant cells.
- **Agrobacterium-mediated virus vectors** combine the transfer efficiency of *Agrobacterium* with the amplification capacity of viral replicons, but again represent engineered rather than natural systems.

**Evidence for natural virus-mediated inter-plant HGT:**

- **Direct evidence is scarce.** While viruses clearly mediate gene transfer between organisms in other systems (e.g., bacteriophages mediate gene transfer between bacteria through transduction), comparable natural inter-plant gene transfer mediated by plant viruses has not been convincingly demonstrated in the pre-2021 literature.
- **Potential mechanisms** by which viruses could facilitate inter-plant HGT include recombination or capture of host DNA: some plant viruses, particularly caulimoviruses, can occasionally encapsidate host DNA fragments. If such a virus infects a new host, the captured DNA could theoretically integrate into the new host's genome—similar to the "transduction" mechanism in bacteriophages.
- Another potential mechanism is **viral integration followed by host gene mobilization**: an EPRV integrated near a host gene could, upon reactivation and re-infection of a new host, carry adjacent host sequences to the new host.
- **Mixed infections facilitating recombination** is a third possibility: when multiple viruses or viral strains infect the same cell, recombination between viral genomes can generate chimeric molecules that may carry host-derived sequences.
- However, **no specific case of a plant gene being transferred from one plant species to another via a viral vector has been documented in nature** as of the pre-2021 literature.

**Honest assessment**: The evidence strongly supports that viruses **do** leave their genes in plant genomes (EPRVs in banana, petunia, rice, yam). However, the role of viruses as natural **carriers** facilitating gene transfer *between different plant species* remains speculative. The available evidence is limited to the demonstration that viral vectors *can* carry foreign genes in laboratory settings, but natural inter-plant HGT mediated by viruses has not been conclusively observed. This represents an open question in the field.

---

## Conclusion

This report has systematically examined four pathways of horizontal gene transfer (HGT) in the plant world, all mediated by intimate biological interactions:

**1. Parasitic plant–host HGT** is the best-documented pathway for plant-to-plant nuclear gene exchange. The haustorial bridge creates a direct conduit for molecular exchange, enabling both transient mRNA movement (demonstrated bidirectionally in *Cuscuta*–host systems) and stable genomic DNA integration (108 functional genes in *Cuscuta*, sorghum-derived genes in *Striga*). The convergent acquisition of strictosidine synthase-like genes in two independently evolved parasitic lineages (*Cuscuta* and *Striga*) suggests that HGT can provide adaptive advantages specific to the parasitic lifestyle.

**2. Fungi-to-plant HGT** is predominantly **unidirectional** (fungi to plants), with compelling cases including the *Fhb7* disease resistance gene in wheat, the *LpBGNL* β-1,6-glucanase gene in cool-season grasses, and the ancient *cox1* group I intron in angiosperm mitochondria. The reverse direction (plant to fungus) has not been convincingly documented in angiosperms, likely due to the physical asymmetry of infection: fungal hyphae penetrate plant tissues and may release DNA upon cell lysis, but plant DNA entering fungal nuclei faces greater barriers.

**3. *Agrobacterium*-mediated T-DNA transfer** represents the most mechanistically understood HGT pathway in nature. The sweet potato case—carrying two distinct T-DNA insertions with functional bacterial genes in all cultivated accessions—establishes that natural genetic modification has occurred in a major food crop. Subsequent genomic surveys have revealed that *Agrobacterium*-derived T-DNA is present in over 2,600 plant species across all major land plant lineages, fundamentally reshaping our understanding of how widespread natural HGT is in plants.

**4. Viral integration** (EPRVs) is well documented across diverse plant taxa (banana, petunia, rice, yam), confirming that viruses do leave their genes in plant genomes after infection. However, the role of viruses as natural carriers facilitating gene transfer *between different plant species* remains an open question with limited supporting evidence—laboratory viral vectors can carry foreign genes, but natural inter-plant HGT mediated by viruses has not been conclusively demonstrated.

**The overarching pattern** across all four pathways is that **physical intimacy between organisms is the key enabler of HGT**. Whether through parasitic haustoria, fungal hyphae, bacterial infection structures, or viral replication within cells, the close cell-to-cell contact created by these biological interactions creates bridges for genetic exchange that would not otherwise occur. The transferred genes, when functional, can provide adaptive advantages—disease resistance, cell wall degradation capabilities, hormone synthesis, or regulatory functions—that shape plant evolution in ways that traditional vertical inheritance alone cannot explain.
