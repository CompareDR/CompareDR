# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants: Parasitic Interfaces, Fungi, Bacteria, and Viruses

## Scope and interpretive framework

This report focuses on **work published through the end of 2020**, with emphasis on primary research that established specific plant HGT events or experimentally demonstrated plausible routes for transfer. In accordance with the research constraint in the question, **the specified 2021 article by Aubin, El Baidouri, and Panaud and all four specified URLs were excluded from the evidence base and are not cited or used below**.

An important distinction runs through all four sections. **Molecular transfer is not automatically horizontal gene transfer.** Host and parasite cells can exchange messenger RNA (mRNA), small RNAs, proteins, viruses, or DNA during an interaction. For an evolutionary HGT event, however, foreign genetic material must ultimately become stably incorporated into the recipient lineage and be inherited. The strongest pre-2021 studies therefore combine direct observations of molecular trafficking with genomic evidence such as foreign genes embedded in chromosomes, retained introns, flanking host DNA, phylogenetic incongruence, transcription, and inheritance. The large-scale *Cuscuta* analysis of Yang et al., for example, explicitly defined functional HGT in terms of cross-species DNA movement followed by genomic integration. [[1]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-019-0458-0)

A useful way to read the evidence is therefore:

- **Direct trafficking:** a molecule is experimentally observed crossing an organismal boundary, as with tomato mRNAs entering *Cuscuta pentagona*. This establishes a physical route but not, by itself, heritable HGT. [[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18631294/)
- **Historical genomic HGT:** a foreign sequence is embedded in a recipient genome, and its genealogy points to a different species or kingdom. Most *Striga*, fungal–plant, and natural *Agrobacterium* examples fall into this category. [[3]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20508124/)
- **Mechanistic bridge plus integration evidence:** parasitic haustoria and *Agrobacterium* are unusually informative because the route by which material could cross the species boundary is independently known. [[4]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-019-0458-0)

## Between Parasitic Plants

### The haustorium creates an unusually intimate route for gene exchange

The central structure is the **haustorium**, the invasive feeding organ by which a parasitic plant penetrates its host and connects with host tissues. In *Cuscuta* (dodder), haustoria penetrate host stems and establish connections to the vascular system; these interfaces permit unusually extensive movement of macromolecules between two plants. Large-scale genomic work subsequently argued that this long-lived, intimate contact is also the most plausible gateway for heritable host-to-parasite HGT. [[1]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-019-0458-0)

- In *Cuscuta pentagona* parasitizing tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*), David-Schwartz et al. directly tracked four host mRNAs into the parasite: transcripts encoding the **α- and β-subunits of pyrophosphate-dependent phosphofructokinase (LePFPα and LePFPβ)**, the Rubisco small subunit **LeRbcS**, and **GIBBERELLIC ACID INSENSITIVE (LeGAI)**. LePFP transcripts occurred in dodder parenchyma and phloem and could be detected far along the parasite stem. The results supported movement through **symplastic connections between host and parasite parenchyma, followed by entry into the parasite phloem**. [[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18631294/)

- A later genomic-scale experiment with *C. pentagona* and two hosts, *Arabidopsis thaliana* and tomato, found extensive **bidirectional mRNA exchange** across the haustorial interface rather than a one-way nutritional stream. Thus a parasitic connection can expose both partners to large populations of foreign RNA molecules. [[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25124438/)

This distinction is critical: the mRNAs observed crossing the haustorium are **mobile transcripts**, not necessarily new genes in the parasite genome. They demonstrate that the physical barrier between two flowering plants can become extraordinarily permeable. Genomic studies were needed to show that some host sequences actually became inherited parasite DNA. [[7]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18631294/)

### What genetic material actually becomes horizontally transferred?

By 2019, evidence pointed to a surprisingly broad spectrum of sequence types.

- **Nuclear protein-coding genes.** The landmark 2010 *Striga hermonthica* study discovered an expressed nuclear sequence called **ShContig9483** in the witchweed genome. Its homologues were widespread in grasses but absent from other sampled eudicots, and its phylogenetic position placed it with *Sorghum bicolor* sequences. Because sorghum is a natural host of *S. hermonthica*, the study inferred host-to-parasite capture from sorghum or a closely related grass. This was a particularly striking case because a monocot nuclear sequence had entered a eudicot parasite. [[8]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20508124/)

- **Genes with identifiable host-lineage origins.** In 2014, a **strictosidine synthase-like gene, SSL**, was independently detected as a horizontal acquisition in the shoot parasite *Cuscuta australis* and the root parasite *Orobanche aegyptiaca*. Phylogenetic analysis placed the donor genes among **Brassicaceae**, implying independent transfers from brassicaceous hosts into two distantly related parasite systems. [[9]](https://bmcplantbiol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2229-14-19)

- **Host genes that can remain functional after transfer.** An **albumin 1 / KNOTTIN-like gene** in *Phelipanche aegyptiaca* and related broomrapes clustered with papilionoid legume albumin genes. In legumes, these proteins have seed-storage and insecticidal roles; the parasite sequence was inferred to have been acquired from a legume host and retained through subsequent parasite evolution. [[10]](https://bmcecolevol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2148-13-48)

- **Large pieces of ordinary genomic DNA, not only processed transcripts.** Genome sequencing of five Orobanchaceae parasites in 2018 detected **106 candidate HGT genes** in the two obligate parasites *Orobanche minor* and *Aeginetia indica*—22 and 84, respectively—but none under the study's criteria in the facultative parasites *Pedicularis keiskei*, *Phtheirospermum japonicum*, and *Melampyrum roseum*. Almost all transferred genes retained introns at positions corresponding to possible host homologues. Some transferred genes were adjacent in the donor genome, implying movement of DNA fragments potentially exceeding **100 kb**. Those observations strongly favor **genomic DNA transfer**, rather than reverse transcription of mature mRNA, for these cases. [[11]](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/10/12/3196/5165405)

- **Coding and non-coding DNA, including transposable elements.** Yang et al. reported **108 transcribed and probably functional HGT events** in *Cuscuta campestris* and related dodders, plus **42 additional host-derived regions** consisting of transposon, pseudogene, or non-coding sequence. Genome structure again favored transfer of genomic DNA rather than retroprocessed RNA. Thus parasitic-plant HGT is not restricted to neat, intact protein-coding genes; comparatively large and biologically heterogeneous pieces of host DNA can cross the species boundary. [[1]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-019-0458-0)

- **Small-RNA-generating foreign loci.** Many horizontally acquired *C. campestris* sequences produced **24-nucleotide small RNAs**, a class associated with RNA-directed DNA methylation. Particularly intriguing was an acquired gene encoding a **leucine-rich-repeat protein kinase** whose locus overlaps a microRNA previously shown to regulate host gene expression. That creates the possibility that a gene first captured from a host lineage can later be repurposed by the parasite to influence subsequent hosts. [[1]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-019-0458-0)

### What does the *Cuscuta–Striga* comparison tell us?

- *Cuscuta* is the clearest mechanistic system because **host and parasite mRNAs can be directly observed crossing haustorial connections**, while genome sequencing independently shows numerous inherited host-derived DNA segments. The two phenomena are related in ecological opportunity but should not be assumed to be the same molecular mechanism. [[7]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18631294/)

- In *Striga hermonthica*, the 2010 **ShContig9483** result was an exceptionally clear evolutionary signature of host-derived nuclear DNA, but the historical transfer itself was not observed in real time. The safest conclusion is therefore that the *Striga* haustorial relationship creates the opportunity for exchange and that the parasite genome documents successful past capture. [[12]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20508124/)

- Comparative Orobanchaceae work strengthened the ecological connection: transfers were more conspicuous in highly host-dependent parasites, and the 2018 genome study found its large HGT complement specifically in the two obligate parasites examined. Direct dependence on a host therefore appears to increase the opportunity for DNA acquisition, although dependence alone does not identify the molecular integration mechanism. [[11]](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/10/12/3196/5165405)

**Bottom line:** parasitic plants do not merely siphon water and metabolites. Their haustoria establish vascular and sometimes symplastic bridges through which **mRNA and other nucleic acids can traffic immediately**, while evolutionary genomics shows stable acquisition of **nuclear genes, long genomic DNA fragments, transposable elements, pseudogenes, and non-coding sequences**. The strongest large-scale pre-2021 evidence points to **genomic DNA** as a major substrate of heritable host-to-parasite HGT. [[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18631294/)

## Between Fungi and Plants

### Is fungal–plant HGT one-way?

No. **Stable genomic HGT between plants and fungi has been inferred in both directions**, although it is much rarer than gene exchange among bacteria and its physical mechanism is usually less directly demonstrated than in *Agrobacterium* or parasitic-plant systems.

A broad 2009 phylogenomic analysis compared six plant genomes with a large taxonomic set of fungal and other genomes. After stringent phylogenetic testing, the authors retained **nine supported plant–fungus transfers: five inferred from fungi to plants and four from plants to fungi**. The events appeared rare and generally ancient. Notably, none of the fungi→plant cases identified in that dataset involved an angiosperm recipient, illustrating how limited the evidence still was at that time. [[14]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/)

The pre-2021 literature later supplied much more biologically vivid examples in both directions.

### Fungi → plants: the *Fhb7* resistance-gene example

The most important pre-2021 disease-resistance case was **Fhb7**.

- In 2020, Wang et al. cloned **Fhb7** from the grass *Thinopyrum elongatum*, a wild relative used in wheat breeding. Fhb7 encodes a **glutathione S-transferase (GST)** that detoxifies the trichothecene mycotoxins produced by *Fusarium* pathogens, thereby conferring resistance to Fusarium head blight and crown rot. [[15]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba5435)

- The striking evolutionary observation was that close Fhb7 GST homologues were not found in the surveyed plant lineages, whereas an approximately **97%-identical sequence occurred in an endophytic fungus of the genus *Epichloë***. Phylogenetic and sequence evidence therefore supported acquisition of Fhb7 by the *Thinopyrum* lineage from an endophytic *Epichloë*-like fungal donor. [[16]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba5435)

- Fhb7 was subsequently **introgressed by conventional distant hybridization from *Thinopyrum* into bread wheat (*Triticum aestivum*)**. That latter step is not horizontal gene transfer: the proposed HGT is the ancient fungus→*Thinopyrum* event, whereas movement from *Thinopyrum* into wheat was deliberate hybridization/breeding. Keeping those two events separate prevents a common conceptual error. [[15]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba5435)

This example is especially important because the transferred gene did not merely persist neutrally: it supplied a biochemical activity directly relevant to fungal disease resistance. The 2020 paper therefore connected cross-kingdom HGT to a demonstrable adaptive phenotype. [[16]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba5435)

### Plants → fungi: *Ave1* and plant-like fungal genes

The reverse direction is also documented.

- **Verticillium dahliae → Ave1.** In 2012, de Jonge et al. characterized the fungal effector **Ave1** in the vascular wilt pathogen *Verticillium dahliae*. Ave1 is recognized by the tomato immune receptor **Ve1**, but in susceptible hosts it contributes to fungal virulence. Sequence distribution and phylogeny led the authors to conclude that *Verticillium* had **acquired Ave1 from plants by HGT**. The precise ancestral plant donor could not be identified, so this should be described as plant-lineage→fungus rather than as transfer from one known modern plant species. [[17]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1119623109)

- **Plant-like subtilisin genes → *Colletotrichum*.** A separate 2013 analysis found a family of **plant-like subtilisin protease genes**, often referred to as **CPLSs (Colletotrichum plant-like subtilisins)**, in the plant-pathogenic fungal genus *Colletotrichum*. Their phylogeny supported a plant→fungus transfer into an ancestor of the genus, followed by inheritance and diversification in the fungal lineage. [[18]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0059078)

The *Ave1* case has an interesting evolutionary irony: a plant-derived gene became a **fungal virulence effector**, and a plant immune receptor then evolved—or was selected—to recognize the fungal protein. HGT can therefore move a gene across kingdoms and place it into a completely different ecological conflict. [[19]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1119623109)

### What mechanism moves genes between plants and fungi?

Unlike *Agrobacterium* transformation, no single universal plant–fungus transfer apparatus is known. The 2009 phylogenomic study established **direction and historical occurrence from genome relationships**, rather than observing DNA physically cross a fungal infection structure. [[20]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/)

Pathogenic fungi, endophytes, and mycorrhizal fungi nevertheless create precisely the ecological circumstances expected to favor rare transfer: fungal hyphae and plant cells coexist for long periods, sometimes with extremely intimate intracellular or apoplastic interfaces. The *Epichloë–grass* association invoked for Fhb7 is an endophytic relationship, while the *Verticillium* and *Colletotrichum* examples involve plant pathogens. The recurring theme is therefore **prolonged physical association**, even though the actual historical DNA-transfer steps remain unresolved. [[21]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba5435)

**Bottom line:** fungal–plant genomic HGT is **bidirectional**. Before 2021, compelling examples included **fungus→plant Fhb7**, which produced a GST-mediated anti-*Fusarium* trait in *Thinopyrum elongatum*, and **plant→fungus Ave1** in *Verticillium dahliae*, as well as plant-like subtilisins in *Colletotrichum*. These are evolutionary genomic events; they should not be conflated with the much more frequent temporary exchange of molecules during plant–fungal interactions. [[21]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba5435)

## Between Bacteria and Plants

### How *Agrobacterium* transfers T-DNA

*Agrobacterium* is exceptional because bacterial→plant HGT is not merely reconstructed from ancient genomes: the bacterium possesses a specialized molecular apparatus that **actively exports DNA into plant cells**.

The classical process can be summarized as follows.

- **A transferable DNA segment is defined on a bacterial plasmid.** Pathogenic *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* carries a tumor-inducing **Ti plasmid**, while *A. rhizogenes* carries root-inducing **Ri plasmids**. The transferable region is the **T-DNA**, delimited by short right- and left-border sequences. The bacterial **virulence (vir) genes themselves normally remain in the bacterium** and operate as the transfer machinery. Primary transformation work and later natural-integration studies established that the transferred segment can become part of plant nuclear DNA. [[22]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)

- **VirD proteins generate the transferable T-strand.** **VirD1/VirD2** process the T-DNA borders; VirD2 nicks the DNA and remains covalently associated with the **5′ end of a single-stranded T-DNA molecule**, the T-strand. Experiments using minimal VirD2 constructs demonstrated that the VirD2 relaxase domain and its secretion signal are sufficient for major parts of T-DNA processing and transfer. [[23]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26877720_Agrobacterium_-Mediated_T-DNA_Transfer_and_Integration_by_Minimal_VirD2_Consisting_of_the_Relaxase_Domain_and_a_Type_IV_Secretion_System_Translocation_Signal)

- **A type IV secretion system exports the DNA.** The VirD2–T-strand complex is recruited to the **VirB/VirD4 type IV secretion system (T4SS)**. Agrobacterial effector proteins, notably the single-stranded-DNA-binding protein **VirE2**, are also delivered to the plant. VirD2 and VirE2 participate in protecting and trafficking the transferred DNA toward the plant nucleus. [[23]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26877720_Agrobacterium_-Mediated_T-DNA_Transfer_and_Integration_by_Minimal_VirD2_Consisting_of_the_Relaxase_Domain_and_a_Type_IV_Secretion_System_Translocation_Signal)

- **The T-DNA can become chromosomal plant DNA.** Once in the nucleus, transferred DNA can be incorporated into a plant chromosome through host DNA-repair processes. Most infection events affect somatic tissue—crown-gall tumors or hairy roots—but on rare evolutionary occasions an insertion becomes heritable. Such inherited bacterial DNA is often called **cellular T-DNA, or cT-DNA**. Natural plant genomes provide direct evidence that this has happened repeatedly. [[24]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC23930/)

Thus *Agrobacterium* is effectively a natural genetic engineer: its infectious cycle includes delivery of bacterial plasmid DNA into a eukaryotic nuclear genome. [[25]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)

### Natural transgenic plants existed long before biotechnology

One of the clearest early cases is **tree tobacco, *Nicotiana glauca***. Aoki et al. found a chromosomal region resembling the T-DNA of *Agrobacterium rhizogenes*. The natural insertion contained recognizable homologues of four bacterial T-DNA genes:

- **NgrolB**
- **NgrolC**
- **NgORF13**
- **NgORF14**

Their location in ordinary *N. glauca* genomic DNA demonstrated that an ancient *Agrobacterium* transformation had entered the inherited plant lineage rather than remaining an ephemeral infection. [[26]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC23930/)

This is not an isolated laboratory curiosity. The sweet-potato work a decade and a half later established the same phenomenon in a major food crop. [[27]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)

### Sweet potato: a naturally transgenic crop

Kyndt et al. reported in 2015 that cultivated sweet potato, ***Ipomoea batatas***, contains at least two distinct *Agrobacterium*-derived T-DNA regions, named **IbT-DNA1** and **IbT-DNA2**, embedded in its nuclear genome. The insertion junctions with plant DNA and expression of bacterial-derived genes made this a particularly strong natural-HGT example. [[27]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)

**IbT-DNA1** contains genes related to the right T-DNA of *Agrobacterium*, including:

- **iaaM**, encoding a tryptophan monooxygenase involved in bacterial auxin biosynthesis;
- **iaaH**, encoding indoleacetamide hydrolase, the next step in that auxin pathway;
- **Acs**, an **agrocinopine synthase**-related gene;
- **C-prot**, another T-DNA-derived coding sequence. [[28]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)

IbT-DNA1 occurs within a sweet-potato **F-box gene** and includes duplicated/inverted T-DNA structure. The original 2015 survey found this insertion throughout the large collection of cultivated sweet-potato accessions examined, suggesting that the insertion long predates modern breeding. Several bacterial-derived genes were transcriptionally active in sweet-potato tissues. [[27]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)

**IbT-DNA2** is more similar to left T-DNA from *A. rhizogenes* and contains recognizable members of the **RolB/RolC family**, together with **ORF13, ORF14, ORF17n**, and an **ORF18/ORF17n-related sequence**. In a later genome assembly it was located within a plant **UcpB mitochondrial-carrier-family gene** region. [[28]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)

A 2019 study substantially refined the evolutionary story. It showed that the *Agrobacterium* sequences are **not confined to cultivated hexaploid sweet potato**:

- IbT-DNA1-related sequences also occurred in tetraploid *I. batatas*, ***Ipomoea cordatotriloba***, ***I. tenuissima***, and some related *Ipomoea* accessions.
- IbT-DNA2 was detected in tetraploid *I. batatas* and ***I. trifida***.
- Closely matching insertion sites among related plants indicated ancient transformation events predating the modern cultivated hexaploid crop. [[28]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)

This result is important because it corrects an overly simple interpretation of the 2015 discovery. The T-DNA was not something acquired recently during human cultivation; it was already present in the evolutionary background from which sweet potato arose. Human domestication subsequently propagated plants carrying it. [[28]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48691-3)

Whether the *Agrobacterium* genes **caused domestication-relevant traits such as storage-root development remains unresolved**. Their fixation and expression make an evolutionary contribution plausible, but presence and transcription alone do not prove that humans selected sweet potato because of the T-DNA. The 2015 and 2019 studies establish natural transformation far more securely than they establish the phenotypic consequences. [[29]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426443/)

**Bottom line:** *Agrobacterium* supplies the most mechanistically explicit example of bacteria→plant HGT. A VirD2-linked **single-stranded T-DNA** is exported through a **VirB/VirD4 type IV secretion system**, reaches the nucleus, and can become plant chromosomal DNA. Natural descendants carrying this DNA include *Nicotiana glauca* with **rolB/rolC/ORF13/ORF14** homologues and *Ipomoea batatas* with the **IbT-DNA1/2** gene sets. [[30]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26877720_Agrobacterium_-Mediated_T-DNA_Transfer_and_Integration_by_Minimal_VirD2_Consisting_of_the_Relaxase_Domain_and_a_Type_IV_Secretion_System_Translocation_Signal)

## Between Viruses and Plants

### Can viral genes remain permanently inside plant genomes?

**Yes—but this is not the normal outcome of every plant-virus infection.** Some viral sequences become **endogenous viral elements**, meaning that virus-derived DNA has entered a plant chromosome and is subsequently inherited.

Plant **pararetroviruses**, particularly members of the family Caulimoviridae, provide the clearest cases. Their replication involves DNA and reverse transcription, and ancient integrations have accumulated in plant nuclear genomes even though chromosomal integration is not required for the ordinary viral replication cycle. The resulting endogenous sequences can range from fragmented evolutionary fossils to unusually intact elements capable of regenerating infectious virus. The pre-2021 banana literature provides the strongest concrete example. [[31]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00212-08)

### Banana streak virus: an endogenous virus that can “wake up”

Wild banana ***Musa balbisiana*** contributes the **B genome** present in many cultivated banana and plantain hybrids. That B genome contains endogenous **Banana streak virus (BSV)** sequences.

- Gayral et al. demonstrated that a particular integrated **Banana streak virus** locus in the *M. balbisiana* genome originated from a single ancestral integration event and can serve as the source of **infectious endogenous pararetrovirus**. This is much stronger evidence than simply finding a short viral fossil: host-integrated viral DNA can, through rearrangement/recombination, regenerate an infectious episomal viral genome. [[32]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00212-08)

- In plantains carrying an AAB genome constitution, stresses associated with tissue culture, hybridization, or other conditions can activate endogenous BSV sequences from the inherited B genome. Because this creates disease problems during banana breeding, Tripathi et al. used **CRISPR/Cas9 in 2019 to mutate the endogenous BSV sequences**, preventing or greatly reducing their ability to produce infectious virus. [[33]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-019-0288-7)

This case gives an unusually vivid answer to “do viruses leave their genes behind?”: an ancestor was infected, virus-derived DNA became ordinary chromosomal plant DNA, descendants inherited it, and the integrated material retained enough viral information to regenerate infectious particles under appropriate conditions. [[34]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00212-08)

### Viral fossils are widespread, not unique to banana

Geering et al. discovered in 2014 that sequences from an ancient caulimovirid lineage they named **florendoviruses** are major components of multiple flowering-plant genomes. In some plants, endogenous florendovirus material occupies a substantial fraction of nuclear DNA. These elements demonstrate repeated, ancient virus→plant endogenization rather than a phenomenon restricted to the *Musa–BSV* system. [[35]](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms6269)

The evolutionary consequences are heterogeneous. Many endogenous viral sequences are fragmented and cannot make a virus. A minority may retain open reading frames or influence host regulation, whereas the *Musa* BSV loci are exceptional because some retain the capacity to reconstitute an infectious viral genome. [[31]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00212-08)

### Can a virus carry genes from one plant toward another?

Here the answer needs more qualification.

A plant virus unquestionably moves genetic material between infected cells and, when transmitted, between individual plants. The harder question is whether a virion can accidentally package **host-derived genetic material**, carry it into another plant, and thereby create a stable heritable host→host HGT event.

By 2015 there was good evidence for the **first part of that pathway**.

- Ghoshal et al. analyzed **Cucumber necrosis virus (CNV)** produced during infection and found that CNV particles did not contain viral RNA exclusively. A detectable fraction of encapsidated RNA was derived from the plant host. Particularly striking was enrichment for **retrotransposon and retrotransposon-like RNAs**, including long Ty3/Gypsy-related sequences containing portions of **Gag/Pol** coding regions. [[36]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.01466-15)

- The relevant viral component is the **CNV coat protein (CP)**, which forms the capsid and is responsible for RNA encapsidation. Because authentic virus particles can incorporate plant RNAs, the authors explicitly raised the possibility that plant viruses could act as vehicles for the **horizontal transmission of host sequences to new plants**. [[37]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.01466-15)

That finding is mechanistically important, but it does **not** complete the proof of natural plant-to-plant HGT. Packaging a host RNA into a virion establishes a plausible carrier stage. A full demonstration would additionally require showing that the packaged host sequence enters another plant, escapes degradation, becomes DNA if necessary, integrates into a chromosome—ideally in a germline or heritable lineage—and persists in descendants. The 2015 CNV work did not demonstrate that entire sequence of events. [[36]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.01466-15)

Accordingly, the pre-2021 evidence supports a carefully tiered conclusion:

- **Virus → plant genome:** definitely documented, with endogenous BSV in *Musa balbisiana* and widespread endogenous florendoviruses as clear examples. [[31]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00212-08)
- **Plant RNA → virus particle:** directly demonstrated for CNV, including retrotransposon-derived host sequences. [[37]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.01466-15)
- **Virus-mediated, stable plant A → plant B genomic HGT:** biologically plausible and supported by a demonstrated packaging mechanism, but **not yet established by the pre-2021 CNV evidence as a complete natural, heritable transfer chain**. [[37]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.01466-15)

**Bottom line:** viruses can leave long-lasting genetic footprints in plants, sometimes so intact that endogenous virus can re-emerge. They can also package host genetic material and therefore constitute plausible **natural HGT vectors**, but the strongest pre-2021 evidence separates “host sequence packaged and transported by a virus” from “host gene stably installed in the genome of a second plant.” [[38]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00212-08)

## Comparative synthesis

Across these four systems, the strength of the evidence and the physical routes of transfer differ markedly.

- **Parasitic plant ↔ host plant:** the **haustorium** supplies an intimate vascular/symplastic interface. Directly observed cargo includes mRNAs such as tomato **LePFP, LeRbcS, and LeGAI**; evolutionary genomic cargo includes **ShContig9483** in *Striga*, **SSL** genes in *Cuscuta australis* and *Orobanche aegyptiaca*, **albumin 1** in *Phelipanche*, and extensive coding/non-coding host DNA in *Cuscuta campestris*. Large-scale genome evidence strongly supports **genomic DNA**, sometimes in long pieces, as an important substrate of heritable transfer. [[39]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18631294/)

- **Fungus ↔ plant:** evolutionary HGT is **bidirectional**, although uncommon. The clearest pre-2021 fungus→plant adaptive case is ***Epichloë* → *Thinopyrum elongatum* Fhb7**, a GST gene conferring trichothecene detoxification and *Fusarium* resistance. In the reverse direction, plants contributed **Ave1** to *Verticillium dahliae* and a plant-like **subtilisin/CPLS** lineage to *Colletotrichum*. The genomic evidence is strong, but the physical transfer mechanism is generally reconstructed rather than observed. [[40]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/)

- **Bacterium → plant:** *Agrobacterium* is the most mechanistically resolved system. **VirD1/VirD2**, a VirD2-linked **T-strand**, and the **VirB/VirD4 T4SS** constitute an active cross-kingdom DNA-transfer apparatus. Rare germline fixation has produced natural transgenic plants, including ***Nicotiana glauca*** with **NgrolB, NgrolC, NgORF13, and NgORF14**, and sweet potato with **IbT-DNA1/2**, including **iaaM, iaaH, Acs, C-prot, RolB/RolC-family, ORF13, and ORF14** genes. [[30]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26877720_Agrobacterium_-Mediated_T-DNA_Transfer_and_Integration_by_Minimal_VirD2_Consisting_of_the_Relaxase_Domain_and_a_Type_IV_Secretion_System_Translocation_Signal)

- **Virus ↔ plant:** virus-derived DNA can become an inherited component of the plant genome, as shown by endogenous **Banana streak virus** in ***Musa balbisiana*** and widespread florendovirus elements. Conversely, **Cucumber necrosis virus coat protein** can encapsidate host RNAs, including **Ty3/Gypsy Gag/Pol-related retrotransposon RNA**, giving viruses a credible carrier mechanism. What remained missing before 2021 was a definitive natural experiment tracing such a packaged host gene all the way into a second plant's heritable genome. [[41]](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00212-08)

The broad pattern is therefore not simply that “genes jump between species.” **Persistent physical intimacy creates opportunities**, but different biological relationships solve different parts of the HGT problem. Haustoria make plant–plant cellular barriers permeable; fungi and plants leave phylogenetic evidence of rare historical exchanges during pathogenic or symbiotic associations; *Agrobacterium* possesses a dedicated DNA-transfer machine; and viruses can both become endogenous and package host sequences. The difficult evolutionary step in every system is not merely getting foreign nucleic acid into another organism—it is **integrating that material into a heritable genome and retaining it long enough for selection or drift to fix it**. The pre-2021 literature shows that all four systems can cross at least some of those barriers, with parasitic plants and *Agrobacterium* providing the clearest links between intimate biological interaction and stable plant-genome acquisition. [[42]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-019-0458-0)
