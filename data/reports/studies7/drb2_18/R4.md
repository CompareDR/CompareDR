# I want to investigate 'Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT)' in the plant world, especially transfers occurring through parasitic relationships

## Between Parasitic Plants: Gene Exchange via Parasitic Relationships

### General Mechanisms of Gene Transfer via Haustorial and Cellular Connections

- **Haustoria as the primary anatomical bridge for molecular exchange.** The haustorium is the defining parasitic organ—a multicellular structure that invades host tissue, differentiates into xylem-intercepting and phloem-intercepting hyphae, and establishes direct cytoplasmic continuity between two distantly related plant species. Westwood (2017) described the haustorial connection as an "extraordinarily successful vegetative graft," noting that it surpasses horticultural grafting in its ability to join phylogenetically distant species across both monocot and dicot lineages ([Westwood 2017, RNA Biology](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15476286.2017.1291482)). The haustorium contains a xylem-to-xylem connection in all parasitic plants, while phloem connections are present in holoparasites and some hemiparasites, enabling the transfer of water, nutrients, and macromolecules—including nucleic acids—between host and parasite.

- **Interspecific plasmodesmata (iPD) and phloem-to-phloem connections.** Ultrastructural and molecular evidence has accumulated for the existence of secondary plasmodesmata formed across the host–parasite cell wall interface, joining genetically distinct individuals of different species. Fischer et al. (2021) compiled a comprehensive table of such connections across multiple *Cuscuta* species and *Orobanche* species, showing both plasmodesmata (PD) and sieve pore (SP) connections at the parenchyma and phloem interface ([Fischer et al. 2021, Frontiers in Plant Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2021.641924/full)). These interspecific plasmodesmata are unique in that they connect separate individuals of unrelated species—something that, with rare exceptions in closely related grafted plants, exists only in parasitic plant–host relationships. The existence of shared plasmodesmata provides a symplastic pathway through which macromolecules, including proteins and nucleic acids, can move between parasite and host. In addition, phloem-to-phloem connections—though direct anatomical evidence of sieve pore continuity has not been documented in *Cuscuta*—are functionally active, as demonstrated by the bidirectional movement of viruses, proteins, and mRNAs ([Westwood 2017](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15476286.2017.1291482)).

- **The Orobanchaceae family as an ideal system for studying HGT frequency across parasitic dependency.** Orobanchaceae is the only plant family spanning the full spectrum of parasitic capabilities—from the non-parasitic *Lindenbergia*, to the facultative hemiparasite *Triphysaria versicolor*, the obligate hemiparasite *Striga hermonthica*, and the obligate holoparasite *Phelipanche aegyptiaca* (syn. *Orobanche aegyptiaca*). Yang et al. (2016) used this gradient to test whether HGT frequency correlates with parasitic dependency, finding that it does: the number of HGT events increased from the facultative hemiparasite to the obligate hemiparasite to the obligate holoparasite, with the non-parasitic *Lindenbergia* showing the fewest events ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). This finding suggests that the intimacy and duration of the haustorial connection—greatest in holoparasites that live largely internal to the host—directly facilitate genetic exchange.

- **Types of genetic material transferred.** Both nuclear and mitochondrial HGT have been documented in parasitic plants, with mitochondrial transfers being particularly common. The specific events and their quantitative details are covered in S1.2 (Yang et al. 2016: 52 HGT events, genomic DNA with intact introns) and S1.3 (Mower et al. 2010: mitochondrial *atp1*, *atp6*, *matR* transfer with unedited cytidine residues; *atpI* repeated acquisition by four extreme parasite lineages). Mobile RNA—including mRNAs, siRNAs, and potentially lncRNAs—also moves bidirectionally across the haustorial interface (discussed below).

- **Bidirectional transfer.** Gene exchange between parasitic plants and their hosts occurs in both directions (host→parasite and parasite→host), as demonstrated by both mRNA and mitochondrial gene studies. The quantitative evidence for bidirectional mRNA exchange (Kim et al. 2014) and mitochondrial gene transfer from *Cuscuta* to *Plantago* (Mower et al. 2010) are detailed in S1.2 and S1.3, respectively. Bidirectional mobility is also consistent with *Cuscuta*'s well-known ability to transmit viruses between plants bridged by the parasite.

- **Mobile nucleic acids (mRNAs, siRNAs, lncRNAs) as cross-kingdom signaling and transfer molecules.** The massive mRNA movement documented by Kim et al. (2014) represents a form of inter-organismal communication unique in scale. Westwood (2017) reviewed the evidence that *Cuscuta* and other parasitic plants also take up siRNAs from their hosts, indicating that multiple RNA species are capable of trans-specific movement. The haustorium enables this exchange by forming seamless symplastic connections that allow macromolecules to pass between species. The regulatory significance of this RNA exchange remains poorly understood, but the diversity of mobile transcripts—spanning thousands of genes—suggests that transferred RNAs may influence gene expression in the recipient, potentially including regulatory effects on haustorial development and nutrient acquisition ([Westwood 2017, RNA Biology](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15476286.2017.1291482)). These mobile nucleic acids may also serve as intermediates for HGT: the "massive mRNA movement" hypothesis posits that host mRNAs could be reverse-transcribed and integrated into the parasite genome, though direct evidence for this route is limited (see S1.2 for detailed findings on genomic DNA vs. retro-processed RNA integration).

- **Genomic DNA integration vs. retro-processed RNA.** The question of whether HGT in parasitic plants proceeds via mRNA intermediates (reverse-transcribed and integrated) or via direct DNA transfer has been explicitly addressed. Yang et al. (2016) found that genomic fragment integration is far more common than retro-processed RNA integration, as evidenced by the presence of intact introns in horizontally acquired genes. This finding is discussed in detail in S1.2.

- **Mitochondrial genomes are more susceptible to HGT than nuclear or plastid genomes.** Plant mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is highly active in HGT relative to nuclear and chloroplast genomes. Several factors contribute to this elevated susceptibility: (i) plant mitochondria possess active DNA uptake systems and are capable of fusion, providing a mechanism for foreign DNA entry and recombination; chloroplasts lack both capabilities ([Archibald 2010, BMC Biology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1741-7007-8-147)); (ii) active recombination processes and the absence of genomic downsizing pressures allow foreign DNA to persist in the mitochondrial genome ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). The specific evidence for gene conversion between foreign and native mitochondrial gene copies (Mower et al. 2010) and the giant *Amborella* mitochondrial genome incorporating algal/moss fragments are detailed in S1.3. In contrast, HGT involving plant nuclear and chloroplast DNA appears to be much rarer, with nuclear HGT events largely concentrated in parasitic plant lineages where haustorial contact provides the necessary physical bridge ([Archibald 2010, BMC Biology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1741-7007-8-147)).

- **Functional expression of HGT-acquired genes.** Horizontally acquired genes in Orobanchaceae are preferentially expressed in the haustorium and functionally biased toward haustorial functions, with purifying selection predominating in functionally transferred sequences. These findings from Yang et al. (2016) are discussed in detail in S1.2.

### Cuscuta as a Model System for Plant-to-Plant HGT

*   **Landmark quantitative framework for HGT and parasitic dependency (Yang et al. 2016).** A foundational study by Yang et al. (2016) in *PNAS* established a systematic phylogenomic pipeline to detect HGT across four Orobanchaceae species spanning the full spectrum of parasitic dependency: *Triphysaria versicolor* (facultative hemiparasite, partly heterotrophic when attached to a host), *Striga hermonthica* (obligate hemiparasite, photosynthetic but deriving most carbon from the host), *Phelipanche aegyptiaca* (syn. *Orobanche aegyptiaca*; obligate holoparasite, nonphotosynthetic and fully heterotrophic), and *Lindenbergia* (a non-parasitic relative used as a control). After phylogenetic screening and extensive validation, **52 high-confidence HGT events** were detected, often from lineages of known host plants. Critically, the number of HGT events increased with the degree of parasitic dependency—*Phelipanche* (most dependent) had the most, *Triphysaria* (least dependent) had fewer, and *Lindenbergia* (non-parasitic) had none—demonstrating that intimate haustorial feeding contact is a major driver of HGT. Analyses of intron sequences in donor and recipient lineages revealed that **genomic fragments were integrated far more often than retro-processed RNA sequences**, and that **purifying selection predominates** in functionally transferred sequences (with only a small fraction of adaptively evolving sites), indicating that acquired genes are maintained by selection rather than drifting neutrally. Furthermore, HGT-acquired genes are **preferentially expressed in the haustorium**—the specialized feeding organ that penetrates host tissue—and are **strongly biased in predicted gene functions** likely to contribute to haustorial function, suggesting that horizontally acquired genes are selectively retained because they enhance the parasite's ability to establish and maintain feeding connections ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).

*   **108 transcribed functional HGT events in *Cuscuta campestris* (Yang et al. 2019).** A follow-up study by Yang et al. (2019) in *Nature Plants* dramatically expanded the known scale of functional HGT in parasitic plants. Using genome sequencing and transcriptome data from *Cuscuta campestris* and related species, the researchers identified **108 transcribed and probably functional HGT events** (fHGTs), plus 42 additional regions containing host-derived transposons, pseudogenes, and non-coding sequences. Genome sequencing of *C. campestris* confirmed the presence of introns in the transferred sequences, supporting genomic DNA transfer rather than retroprocessed RNA as the mechanism. The study concluded that this represents "the most dramatic case known of functional horizontal gene transfer ever found in complex organisms" ([Yang et al. 2019, Nature Plants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/); [Penn State press release, 2019](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/parasitic-plants-use-stolen-genes-make-them-better-parasites)).

*   **Ancestral HGT events and the radiation of *Cuscuta*.** Between **16 and 20 of the 108 transcribed HGT events** are inferred as ancestral in *Cuscuta*, based on transcriptome sequences from species across the phylogenetic range of the genus. These ancient acquisitions predate the diversification of the genus and may have contributed to the successful radiation of *Cuscuta* parasites by providing them with host-derived genetic tools early in their evolutionary history ([Yang et al. 2019, Nature Plants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)).

*   **Convergent horizontal gene transfer across independent parasitic lineages.** One of the most remarkable findings of the Yang 2019 study was the discovery of **convergent HGT**: 18 *Cuscuta* fHGTs were acquired from the **same gene families** by independent HGT events in Orobanchaceae parasites (e.g., *Striga*, *Phelipanche*). The majority of these convergently acquired genes are **highly expressed in the haustorial feeding structures in both lineages**, suggesting that specific host genes are repeatedly targeted by natural selection in unrelated parasitic plant lineages because they provide adaptive advantages for parasitism. This convergent retention and expression strongly implies an adaptive role for specific horizontally acquired genes in parasite biology ([Yang et al. 2019, Nature Plants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/)).

*   **Specific host-derived genes and functional categories.** The HGT-acquired genes in *Cuscuta* span functional categories including **metabolism** and **parasitism enhancement**. One notable HGT event encodes a **leucine-rich repeat (LRR) protein kinase** that overlaps with a microRNA previously shown to regulate host gene expression, suggesting that HGT-derived parasite small RNAs may function directly in parasite–host interaction. Many of the *C. campestris* genes horizontally acquired from hosts are also frequent sources of **24-nucleotide small RNAs** typically associated with RNA-directed DNA methylation, implicating HGT sequences in the parasite's epigenetic regulatory machinery. The genome of *C. campestris* (Vogel et al. 2018) revealed additional signatures of parasitism, including loss of genes for high photosynthetic activity and soil nutrient uptake, consistent with its fully heterotrophic lifestyle ([Yang et al. 2019, Nature Plants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/); [Vogel et al. 2018, Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-04344-z)).

*   **Bidirectional transfer: Cuscuta sends genes back into the host.** HGT between *Cuscuta* and its hosts is not strictly unidirectional (host → parasite). Evidence for **parasite-to-host transfer** comes from mitochondrial gene studies: Mower et al. (2010) documented the horizontal acquisition of multiple mitochondrial genes—including **atp1, atp6, and matR**—from *Cuscuta* to *Plantago* (Plantaginaceae), followed by gene conversion between the transferred parasite genes and the host's native mitochondrial copies. This multi-gene transfer from parasite to host demonstrates that the haustorial connection allows genetic material to flow in both directions ([Mower et al. 2010, BMC Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/)). Additionally, the Penn State press release summarizing the Yang 2019 findings noted that stolen host genes may enable dodder to send **genetic "weapons" back into the host**, potentially including small RNAs that manipulate host gene expression ([Penn State press release, 2019](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/parasitic-plants-use-stolen-genes-make-them-better-parasites)).

*   **Genomic-scale bidirectional mRNA exchange (Kim et al. 2014).** The quantitative scale of cross-species molecular exchange was first revealed by Kim et al. (2014) in *Science*, who used RNA-seq to demonstrate **bidirectional mRNA transfer** between *Cuscuta pentagona* (dodder) and its hosts (Arabidopsis and tomato). Approximately **1% of Arabidopsis mRNA reads** were detected in dodder stems near the haustoria, and approximately **0.6% of *C. pentagona* mRNA reads** were detected in Arabidopsis stems. More than **9,000 different host transcripts** were represented in the mobile RNA pool, and over 8,000 dodder transcripts were found in host tissue, with 44% of the gene diversity represented among the mobile RNAs. This massive exchange of messenger RNA through the haustorial–phloem connection provides a plausible intermediate route for HGT: host mRNAs taken up by the parasite could, in principle, be reverse-transcribed and integrated into the parasite genome, although direct evidence for genomic DNA integration (rather than RNA-mediated transfer) is stronger ([Kim et al. 2014, Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25124438/); [Frontiers in Plant Science, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.980033/full)).

*   **Methodology for HGT detection.** The identification and confirmation of HGT events in *Cuscuta* and related Orobanchaceae relied on a multi-step approach: (1) **phylogenomic screening** using automated pipelines to build phylogenetic trees for each orthogroup containing genes from 22 representative sequenced plant genomes and six transcriptomes; (2) **transcriptomic evidence** from parasites grown on host plants with known genome sequences, enabling both HGT detection and expression analysis; (3) **validation procedures** including assessment of intron sequences (to distinguish genomic DNA integration from retro-processed RNA), evaluation of GC content and codon usage, and careful exclusion of contaminants; and (4) **PCR and sequencing validation** of candidate events. Three topological models were used to identify HGT: parasite genes nested within a donor clade, parasite genes placed as sisters to a donor clade, and single-node support for parasite–donor grouping ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).

*   **Evolutionary significance.** Collectively, these studies establish *Cuscuta* as the premier model system for plant-to-plant HGT. The combination of a broad host range, extensive haustorial feeding connections, and demonstrated bidirectional exchange of both mRNA and genomic DNA makes *Cuscuta* an unparalleled system for studying the mechanisms and adaptive consequences of cross-species gene transfer in multicellular eukaryotes. The discovery of convergent HGT—where unrelated parasitic lineages independently acquire similar functional gene families from their hosts—suggests that HGT is not merely a byproduct of parasitic feeding but a directed evolutionary process that shapes the genetic toolkit of parasitism ([Yang et al. 2019, Nature Plants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332314/); [Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).

### Striga and Other Parasitic Plants: Mitochondrial and Nuclear Gene Transfer

While *Cuscuta* has emerged as the premier model for plant-to-plant HGT, other parasitic plant genera—particularly *Striga*, *Rafflesia*, *Orobanche*, and *Pilostyles*—have revealed that horizontal gene acquisition is a widespread phenomenon across the parasitic plant tree of life. These systems collectively demonstrate that both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes are targets of HGT, that transfer can cross major phylogenetic boundaries, and that the resulting genomic chimerism can complicate phylogenetic inference.

#### *Striga hermonthica*: Cross-Lineage Nuclear Gene Transfer from Monocot Hosts

- **Yoshida et al. (2010, *Science*)** provided the first clear evidence of nuclear HGT between a parasitic plant and its host. By screening ~17,000 expressed genes of *Striga hermonthica* (witchweed), a eudicot root parasite of monocot cereal crops, the researchers identified a single gene, *ShContig9483*, that was highly similar to genes in sorghum (*Sorghum bicolor*) and rice (*Oryza sativa*) but absent from both parasitic and non-parasitic eudicot relatives of *Striga*. Phylogenetic analysis placed *ShContig9483* within the monocot clade, not with the expected eudicot relatives of *Striga*, indicating a relatively recent transfer from a grass host into the parasite genome ([Yoshida et al. 2010, *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20508124/); [RIKEN research highlight](https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/rr/6359/index.html)).

- This finding is remarkable because it demonstrates **cross-lineage HGT across a deep phylogenetic divide**: the donor (monocot grasses) and recipient (*Striga*, a eudicot asterid) diverged over 100 million years ago. The transfer was detected at the mRNA level, suggesting the foreign gene is transcribed in *Striga*. Although the function of the *ShContig9483* protein remains unknown, its discovery established that nuclear HGT in parasitic plants is not limited to closely related taxa and can bridge major angiosperm lineages ([Yoshida et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20508124/)).

- *Striga hermonthica* is an obligate hemiparasite of the Orobanchaceae family, photosynthetic but deriving most of its carbon from host roots. The intimate haustorial connection it forms with monocot hosts (sorghum, rice, maize) likely facilitates the transfer of nuclear genetic material, though the precise mechanism—whether via DNA, RNA, or a combination—remains to be fully resolved.

#### Mitochondrial Gene Transfer: A Widespread Phenomenon in Parasitic Plants

Mitochondrial HGT is disproportionately common in parasitic plants compared to nuclear HGT, a pattern first recognized through studies of the Orobanchaceae and Rafflesiaceae. Several landmark studies have defined the scope and mechanisms of this process.

#### Repeated Acquisition of *atpI* by Extreme Parasite Lineages

- **Barkman et al. (2007, *BMC Evolutionary Biology*)** conducted a broad phylogenetic analysis of 102 seed plant species using three mitochondrial genes (*atp1*, *coxI*, and *matR*). They inferred at least 11 independent origins of parasitism in angiosperms and discovered **multiple horizontal transfers of *atp1* (also designated *atpI*) from host to parasite lineages**. The gene *atpI* encodes ATP synthase subunit I, a core component of the mitochondrial energy-transduction machinery ([Barkman et al. 2007](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/mitochondrial-dna-suggests-at-least-11-origins-of-parasitism-in-a/)).

- The repeated acquisition of *atpI* occurred in **four extreme parasite lineages** where the parasite lives inside the host for much of its lifespan, maximizing the duration of intimate cellular contact. This repeated, independent acquisition of the same gene across distantly parasitic lineages suggests that *atpI* transfer is not a random event but is facilitated by the parasitic lifestyle itself—possibly due to the selective advantage of acquiring a functional mitochondrial gene from a closely associated host, or simply because the prolonged host–parasite cellular contact increases the probability of DNA transfer ([Yang et al. 2016, *PNAS*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113); [Barkman et al. 2007](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/mitochondrial-dna-suggests-at-least-11-origins-of-parasitism-in-a/)).

- In the endoparasite *Pilostyles* (family Apodanthaceae), Barkman et al. identified a **chimeric *atp1* gene sequence** composed of segments from multiple historical xenologous acquisitions, providing evidence that repeated host-to-parasite transfers have occurred within a single parasitic lineage over evolutionary time ([Barkman et al. 2007](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/mitochondrial-dna-suggests-at-least-11-origins-of-parasitism-in-a/)).

#### The *coxI* Group I Homing Intron: Frequent Horizontal Transfer Across Angiosperms

- The mitochondrial *coxI* gene (cytochrome c oxidase subunit I) has been invaded by a **group I homing intron** that has spread horizontally across diverse angiosperm lineages. This mobile genetic element encodes a homing endonuclease that facilitates its own propagation by inserting into intron-free alleles of *coxI*—a process that can occur between unrelated plant species when parasitic or graft-mediated contact provides a bridge for genetic exchange. Barkman et al. (2007) noted that the origins of parasitism in angiosperms are "strongly correlated with horizontal acquisitions of the invasive *coxI* group I intron," suggesting that the parasitic lifestyle promotes intron mobility ([Barkman et al. 2007](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/mitochondrial-dna-suggests-at-least-11-origins-of-parasitism-in-a/); [Sanchez-Puerta et al. 2008, *PMC*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2727383/)).

- The *coxI* group I intron has been one of the most frequently horizontally transferred genetic elements in plant mitochondria, with independent acquisitions documented in numerous angiosperm families, including both parasitic and non-parasitic lineages. Its spread is facilitated by the homing endonuclease mechanism, which can function across species boundaries when DNA is transferred through haustorial or graft connections ([Yang et al. 2016, *PNAS*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).

#### Massive Mitochondrial Gene Transfer in Rafflesiaceae

- **Xi et al. (2013, *PLOS Genetics*)** conducted the first comprehensive assessment of mitochondrial HGT in a parasitic plant clade, sequencing 38 mitochondrial protein-coding and rRNA genes from three Rafflesiaceae species (*Rafflesia cantleyi*, *Rafflesia tuan-mudae*, and *Sapria himalayana*) and their obligate hosts (*Tetrastigma* spp., Vitaceae). Strikingly, **24%–41% of the mitochondrial gene sequences showed evidence of HGT** in Rafflesiaceae, depending on the species. This is an order of magnitude higher than the ~2.1% of nuclear gene transcripts previously found to be horizontally acquired in *Rafflesia cantleyi* ([Xi et al. 2013, *PLOS Genetics*](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article/info%3Adoi%2F10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1003265)).

- Most of the transgenic mitochondrial sequences possessed **intact reading frames and were actively transcribed**, indicating they are potentially functional rather than pseudogenized. Some transgenes maintained synteny with both donor and recipient lineages, suggesting that native genes may have been displaced via homologous recombination—a process where the foreign gene replaces the native copy at the same genomic location ([Xi et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article/info%3Adoi%2F10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1003265)).

- The Rafflesiaceae are endophytic holoparasites lacking leaves and stems, with a very narrow host range on *Tetrastigma* (Vitaceae). The parasite and host are separated by at least 115 million years of evolution, making it straightforward to distinguish transgenes from native genes using phylogenomic tools. The Xi et al. (2013) study established for the first time that, although nuclear gene HGT is appreciable in parasitic plants, **mitochondrial gene HGT is substantially higher**—a pattern that may extend to other parasitic plant clades ([Xi et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article/info%3Adoi%2F10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1003265)).

#### Multi-Gene Mitochondrial Transfer and Gene Conversion: *Cuscuta* → *Plantago*

- **Mower et al. (2010, *BMC Biology*)** investigated the mechanistic details of plant-to-plant HGT by examining the parasitic genus *Cuscuta* and a small clade of *Plantago* species. A broad PCR survey of mitochondrial genes revealed that **at least three genes (*atp1*, *atp6*, and *matR*) were recently transferred from *Cuscuta* to *Plantago***. Quantitative PCR confirmed their mitochondrial location in the recipient *Plantago* species ([Mower et al. 2010, *BMC Biology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/); [faculty publication abstract](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/plantscifacpub/52/)).

- This study provided the best evidence to date that **multiple mitochondrial genes can be transferred via a single HGT event**, likely through a strictly DNA-mediated intermediate. The identical phylogenetic distribution of the three foreign genes within *Plantago*, combined with the retention of cytidines at ancestral RNA editing positions, supported a single transfer event rather than multiple independent acquisitions ([Mower et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/)).

- Critically, Mower et al. detected **three cases of gene conversion between native and foreign (transferred) copies of the *atp1* gene**. Gene conversion—where one DNA sequence is replaced by a homologous sequence from another copy—between co-resident foreign and native mitochondrial genes suggests that transferred genes can contribute to mitochondrial genetic diversity and may play an evolutionarily important role in the recipient genome. However, the three transferred genes appeared to have degraded into pseudogenes shortly after transfer, as none were detectably transcribed ([Mower et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/)).

- The discovery of gene conversion between parasite-derived and host mitochondrial genes has significant implications for phylogenetic inference: it can produce **chimeric gene sequences** that blur the boundaries between donor and recipient lineages, making it difficult to reconstruct the true evolutionary history of both the gene and the species. This genomic chimerism, first highlighted by Barkman et al. (2007) and further documented by Mower et al. (2010), is a hallmark of parasitic plant mitochondrial genomes ([Barkman et al. 2007](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/mitochondrial-dna-suggests-at-least-11-origins-of-parasitism-in-a/); [Mower et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/)).

#### The *Amborella* Mitochondrial Genome: A Foreign DNA Magnet

- Although not a parasitic plant, *Amborella trichopoda* (the sister species to all other living angiosperms) possesses the **largest known mitochondrial genome at ~3.9 megabases**. **Rice et al. (2013, *Science*)** reported that this enormous genome contains **six genome equivalents of foreign mitochondrial DNA**, acquired from green algae, mosses, and other angiosperms. Many of these horizontal transfers were large, including the acquisition of **entire mitochondrial genomes from three green algae and one moss** ([Rice et al. 2013, *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24357311/)).

- Rice et al. proposed a **fusion-compatibility model** to explain these findings: *Amborella* captures whole mitochondria from diverse eukaryotes (facilitated by its epiphytic growth habit, propensity to produce suckers from wounds, and low rate of mitochondrial DNA loss), followed by mitochondrial fusion (limited mechanistically to green plant mitochondria) and subsequent genome recombination. This case, while not involving parasitism, demonstrates that plant mitochondrial genomes are exceptionally permissive to foreign DNA integration—a property that is amplified in parasitic plants where intimate host contact provides a continuous source of foreign genetic material ([Rice et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24357311/)).

#### Other Parasitic Plants with Documented HGT

- **Orobanche (broomrape)**: Species of Orobanche (Orobanchaceae) have undergone extensive mitochondrial genome reconfiguration during the transition to holoparasitism. Studies have documented intracellular gene transfer from the chloroplast to the mitochondrial genome, and the first evidence of host-to-parasite mitochondrial gene transfer in Orobanchaceae has been reported in Orobanche, including the acquisition of *atp6* and other mitochondrial genes from host plants ([Mower et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/); [Petersen et al. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1567724919303411)).

- **Rafflesia**: In addition to the massive mitochondrial HGT documented by Xi et al. (2013), a prior study by **Xi et al. (2012, *PMC*)** identified ~2.1% of nuclear gene transcripts in *Rafflesia cantleyi* as likely acquired from its obligate host *Tetrastigma rafflesiae*, establishing the first case of nuclear HGT in a parasitic plant ([Xi et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3460754/)). The combined picture—~2% nuclear and 24–41% mitochondrial HGT—makes Rafflesiaceae the most extreme known case of foreign gene integration in plants.

- **Epifagus**: *Epifagus virginiana* (beechdrops), a holoparasite of beech trees in the Orobanchaceae, has also been investigated for HGT. Its highly reduced plastid genome and the retention of specific mitochondrial genes have provided insights into the genomic consequences of parasitism, though specific HGT events in *Epifagus* are less well characterized than in *Rafflesia* or *Cuscuta* ([Mower et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/)).

#### Functional Fate of Transferred Mitochondrial Genes

The studies above reveal a spectrum of outcomes for horizontally acquired mitochondrial genes:

- **Functional retention**: In Rafflesiaceae, most transferred mitochondrial genes have intact reading frames and are actively transcribed, suggesting they may be functional and have potentially replaced native copies through homologous recombination ([Xi et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article/info%3Adoi%2F10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1003265)).
- **Pseudogenization**: In the *Cuscuta* → *Plantago* transfer, the three acquired mitochondrial genes (*atp1*, *atp6*, *matR*) degraded into pseudogenes shortly after transfer and were not detectably transcribed ([Mower et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/)).
- **Gene conversion**: In *Plantago*, gene conversion between native and foreign *atp1* copies generated chimeric sequences, complicating phylogenetic inference but potentially contributing to mitochondrial genetic diversity ([Mower et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3022774/)).
- **Repeated acquisition**: The *atpI* gene has been independently acquired by four extreme parasite lineages, and *Pilostyles* shows evidence of multiple historical acquisitions of *atp1*, producing a chimeric gene sequence ([Barkman et al. 2007](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/mitochondrial-dna-suggests-at-least-11-origins-of-parasitism-in-a/)).

These findings collectively demonstrate that HGT in parasitic plants involves both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, with mitochondrial transfers being substantially more frequent. The intimate haustorial connection between parasite and host creates a permissive environment for genetic exchange, and the resulting genomic chimerism—exemplified by gene conversion, repeated acquisition of the same genes, and the integration of foreign DNA into functional genomic positions—has profound implications for plant genome evolution and phylogenetic reconstruction.

## Between Fungi and Plants: Bidirectional Gene Transfer

### Fungi-to-Plant Transfer: Disease Resistance and Metabolic Pathway Genes

Most fungal-to-plant HGT events are ancient and rare, often predating the rise of land plants, which makes them inherently difficult to detect ([Wikipedia – Plant–fungus HGT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer); [Rosewich & Kistler 2000](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)). Despite this, several well-characterized cases demonstrate that fungi have donated functionally important genes to plants, including disease resistance factors and entire metabolic pathways.

- **Fhb7 – a fungal-derived disease resistance gene in wheat:** The *Fhb7* gene encodes a glutathione S-transferase (GST) that confers broad resistance to *Fusarium* head blight (FHB) — a devastating fungal disease of wheat caused by *Fusarium* species that produce trichothecene mycotoxins. *Fhb7* was cloned by assembling the genome of *Thinopyrum elongatum* (tall wheatgrass), a wild relative used in wheat distant-hybridization breeding. The gene detoxifies trichothecenes through de-epoxidation. Crucially, *Fhb7* GST homologs are absent from all other plant genomes examined, and phylogenetic evidence strongly supports that *Th. elongatum* acquired *Fhb7* through horizontal gene transfer from an endophytic *Epichloë* fungal species. The gene was subsequently introgressed into bread wheat (*Triticum aestivum*), where it confers resistance to both FHB and crown rot without yield penalty ([Wang et al. 2020, *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32273397/); [Graingenes summary](https://graingenes.org/GG3/node/902)).

- **Phylogenomic survey of rice reveals 14 candidate plant–fungus HGT events:** Richards et al. (2009) conducted a phylogenomic analysis of the rice (*Oryza sativa*) genome, constructing phylogenetic trees from 1,689 identified genes and all available homologs (3,177 gene families). They identified **14 candidate plant–fungus HGT events**, nine of which showed infrequent (rare) transfer patterns between plants and fungi. The transferred genes encode proteins with diverse functions: **L-fucose permease sugar transporter**, **zinc-binding alcohol dehydrogenase**, **iucA/iucC family protein** (involved in siderophore biosynthesis), **membrane transporter**, **phospholipase/carboxylesterase**, **DUF239 domain protein**, **phosphate-response 1 family protein**, a hypothetical protein similar to zinc finger (C2H2-type) protein, and another conserved hypothetical protein. The study demonstrated that plant–fungus HGT follows a pattern of rare and ancient events, with most transferred genes being operational (metabolic) rather than informational ([Richards et al. 2009, *The Plant Cell*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer); [PMC2729602](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/)).

- **Ancient transfer of the shikimate pathway from fungi to ancestral land plants:** The shikimate pathway — a seven-step metabolic route producing aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan) — is found in prokaryotes, ascomycete fungi, apicomplexans, and plants, but is absent from most other eukaryotes. Richards et al. (2006) investigated the evolutionary origins of the eukaryotic shikimate pathway, finding evidence for gene fusions, horizontal gene transfer, and endosymbiotic replacements. The study proposed that the pathway's pentafunctional *arom* enzyme and other pathway components may have ancient origins in eukaryotes or were conveyed by eukaryote-to-eukaryote HGT. Ascomycete fungi, along with zygomycetes, basidiomycetes, apicomplexans, ciliates, and oomycetes, retained elements of an ancestral pathway inherited through the bikont/unikont eukaryote root. The acquisition of this pathway was a key evolutionary innovation, enabling plants to synthesize aromatic compounds essential for lignin, flavonoids, and other phenylpropanoid metabolites critical for land colonization ([Richards et al. 2006, *Eukaryotic Cell*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16963634/)).

- **Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) — a fungal-to-land-plant HGT via arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis:** Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) is the gateway enzyme of the phenylpropanoid pathway, catalyzing the deamination of phenylalanine to trans-cinnamic acid — the first committed step in the synthesis of flavonoids, lignin, and other phenylpropanoid compounds. PAL is present in fungi (including Basidiomycota such as *Rhodotorula* and Ascomycota such as *Aspergillus* and *Neurospora*), where it functions in phenylalanine catabolism for carbon and nitrogen. Emiliani et al. (2009) obtained robust phylogenetic evidence that the ancestor of land plants acquired PAL via horizontal gene transfer during symbioses with soil bacteria and fungi. The transfer likely occurred through a pre-Dikarya divergent fungal lineage, with a *Nostoc*-like or soil-sediment bacterium as an intermediate. The fungal PAL was then transferred to an ancestor of land plants via an ancient arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbiosis — the same symbiotic association that is thought to have facilitated plant colonization of land. This HGT event was a key adaptation: phenylpropanoid metabolism provided UV protection, antimicrobial defense, and structural support (lignin) that were critical for terrestrial life ([Emiliani et al. 2009, *Biology Direct*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2657906/)).

- **HGT of virulence factors among fungal pathogens — the ToxA/host-selective toxin example:** Horizontal gene transfer provides fungal pathogens with a powerful mechanism to acquire virulence capabilities and infect new host plants. The wheat tan spot pathogen *Pyrenophora tritici-repentis* produces a host-selective toxin (HST) called Ptr ToxA, which induces necrosis in wheat varieties carrying the susceptibility gene *Tsn1*. Evidence strongly suggests that ToxA was horizontally transferred from *Stagonospora nodorum* (now *Parastagonospora nodorum*) to *P. tritici-repentis* some time prior to 1941, as the two species share near-identical *ToxA* sequences with flanking regions showing high conservation. This interspecific transfer of a host-selective toxin gene enabled *P. tritici-repentis* to expand its host range and cause disease on wheat ([Wikipedia – Plant–fungus HGT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer); [USDA ARS publication](https://www.ars.usda.gov/research/publications/publication/?seqNo115=205354)). ToxA has since been shown to have transferred horizontally among at least three fungal wheat pathogens (*P. nodorum*, *P. tritici-repentis*, and *Bipolaris sorokiniana*), with transposon-mediated mechanisms implicated ([PMC6737239](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6737239/)).

- **Large sterigmatocystin/aflatoxin gene cluster transfer between *Aspergillus* and *Podospora*:** In a landmark study of HGT of a large gene cluster between fungi, Slot & Rokas (2011) discovered that a complete sterigmatocystin (ST) biosynthetic gene cluster in *Podospora anserina* was horizontally transferred from an *Aspergillus* species. The ST cluster spans approximately 54 kb and contains 23 genes responsible for producing sterigmatocystin — a highly toxic, mutagenic secondary metabolite that is a precursor to the aflatoxins. *Aspergillus nidulans* produces sterigmatocystin, while *Podospora anserina* had not been known to produce this compound. The two clusters show high conservation and microsynteny, including intergenic regions containing 14 binding sites for AfIR, a transcription factor for ST/aflatoxin biosynthetic gene activation. This represents one of the largest known horizontal transfers of a metabolic gene cluster between fungi and illustrates how HGT can drive metabolic diversity in filamentous fungi ([Slot & Rokas 2011, *Current Biology*](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(10)01651-9); [PubMed 21194949](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21194949/)).

- **Timing and evolutionary context:** Most fungal-to-plant HGT events are ancient, with the shikimate pathway and PAL transfers predating the rise of land plants (>450 million years ago), while the *Fhb7* transfer is comparatively recent, having occurred in the *Thinopyrum* lineage prior to its introgression into wheat. The rarity of detectable events reflects both the ancient nature of most transfers and the methodological challenges of detecting them; for a detailed discussion of detection indicators, the complexity hypothesis, and alternative explanations, see S5.2.

### Plant-to-Fungi Transfer: ACC Deaminase, Chitinase, and Symbiosis Genes

Plant-to-fungi horizontal gene transfer represents the reverse direction of the plant–fungus HGT axis. While fungi-to-plant transfers are relatively well-documented (see S2.1), the opposite direction—plants donating genes to fungi—has received comparatively less attention and remains an emerging area of study. The evidence that does exist points to ecologically intimate associations, particularly symbiosis, as the primary facilitators of such exchanges.

- **ACC deaminase gene transfer to *Amanita* ectomycorrhizal fungi.** The most prominent case of plant-to-fungi HGT involves the transfer of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase genes to ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi of the *Amanita* genus. Wang et al. (2020) identified gene families unique to ECM *Amanita* species and absent from closely related asymbiotic fungi, finding that horizontally transferred ACC deaminase genes are **highly up-regulated during symbiosis**, suggesting functional relevance to the symbiotic interaction ([Wang et al. 2020, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674699/)). This up-regulation implies that the transferred gene is not a silent genomic fossil but is actively transcribed when the fungus engages with its plant host.

- **Mechanism of ethylene modulation via ACC deaminase.** ACC deaminase (encoded by the *AcdS* gene) catalyzes the breakdown of ACC—the immediate precursor of the plant hormone ethylene—into ammonia and α-ketobutyrate ([Singh et al. 2015, *Frontiers in Microbiology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2015.00937/full)). In the context of symbiosis, a fungus expressing a plant-derived ACC deaminase gene could locally reduce ethylene concentrations in the host plant's root zone. Since elevated ethylene acts as a stress signal that can inhibit root growth and symbiotic establishment, the fungal ACC deaminase may help **moderate plant ethylene levels to facilitate or stabilize the symbiotic interaction**. This functional logic parallels the well-studied bacterial ACC deaminase system (e.g., in *Pseudomonas* spp.), where plant growth-promoting bacteria use ACC deaminase to reduce stress ethylene in the rhizosphere ([Singh et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2015.00937/full)). However, the bacterial and fungal cases are **independent phenomena**: bacterial ACC deaminase genes are widespread among Proteobacteria and have been extensively transferred horizontally within the bacterial domain ([Glick 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S094450131300150X)), whereas the *Amanita* ACC deaminase genes appear to have a plant origin.

- **Phylogenetic evidence and the Nascimento et al. (2014) study.** Nascimento et al. (2014) conducted a broad phylogenetic analysis of ACC deaminase genes across bacterial and fungal species. They found that ACC deaminase genes are **predominantly vertically inherited** across various bacterial and fungal classes, but their results also revealed **a considerable degree of horizontal gene transfer**, including interkingdom transfer events ([Nascimento et al. 2014, *Frontiers in Microbiology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4048297/)). This finding is significant because it establishes that while vertical inheritance is the dominant pattern, HGT—including transfers between distantly such as plants and fungi—has occurred. Earlier, Hontzeas et al. (2005) provided direct experimental evidence for horizontal transfer of ACC deaminase genes, demonstrating phylogenetic incongruence between ACC deaminase gene trees and organismal phylogenies ([Hontzeas et al. 2005, *Applied and Environmental Microbiology*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1287689/)).

- **Chitinase (CHI) gene: a plant defense gene with bacterial and fungal homologs.** The chitinase gene family provides an additional example of plant-to-microbe gene transfer. The plant-specific CHI gene (Arabidopsis locus AT2G43570), encoding a putative basic chitinase, is a well-characterized defense gene induced during Systemic Acquired Resistance. Bacterial homologues of this plant-specific CHI gene have been identified, suggesting that the gene has moved from plants into bacteria—and potentially fungi—via horizontal transfer ([Haimlich et al. 2024, *Nature Communications*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11131428/)). Chitinases degrade chitin, a major component of fungal cell walls, and their transfer from plants to microbes could potentially provide recipient organisms with novel capabilities for cell wall remodeling or defense against competing fungi. However, the exact directionality and timing of these transfer events remain subjects of ongoing investigation, as the ancient origin of chitinase gene families complicates phylogenetic inference.

- **Other potential plant-to-fungi gene transfers.** Beyond ACC deaminase and chitinase, additional cases of plant-to-fungi HGT have been proposed but remain less well-characterized. The broader pattern of plant–fungus HGT, as established by Richards et al. (2009) through phylogenomic analysis of rice, demonstrated that plant–fungus HGT events are **rare and ancient**, with most transfers predating the rise of land plants ([Richards et al. 2009, *The Plant Cell*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/)). The difficulty of detecting plant-to-fungi transfers specifically—as opposed to the more readily detectable fungi-to-plant direction—stems from the fact that fungal genomes are generally more compact and faster-evolving, making ancient integration events harder to identify. Additionally, the ecological intimacy required for gene transfer (symbiosis, parasitism, or shared niche occupation) may be less commonly achieved in the plant-to-fungi direction than in the reverse.

- **Ecological context facilitating plant-to-fungi HGT.** The ecological interactions that facilitate plant-to-fungi gene transfer are primarily **symbiotic and parasitic associations** where prolonged, intimate cellular contact occurs. Ectomycorrhizal symbiosis—where fungal hyphae penetrate plant root tissues and establish a nutrient-exchange interface—provides a plausible anatomical setting for nucleic acid exchange. Similarly, parasitic plant–fungi interactions, where fungal pathogens invade plant tissues, could create opportunities for gene transfer in either direction. The shared ecological niche of soil and rhizosphere environments, where plants, fungi, and bacteria coexist in close proximity, further increases the probability of interkingdom DNA transfer. As noted in the broader plant–fungus HGT literature, ecological association is an important but understudied factor in shared evolutionary histories ([Wikipedia: Plant–fungus horizontal gene transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)).

- **Challenges in determining directionality.** Determining whether a given HGT event occurred in the plant-to-fungi direction versus the fungi-to-plant direction is methodologically challenging, particularly for ancient transfers. The detection indicators (phylogenetic inconsistency, high sequence similarity, irregular distribution, genomic composition anomalies) and alternative explanations that must be ruled out are discussed in S5.2. For the ACC deaminase case, the combination of phylogenetic incongruence, the presence of the gene in plant-associated fungi but not in closely related non-symbiotic fungi, and its up-regulation during symbiosis collectively support—but do not conclusively prove—a plant-to-fungus transfer direction.

- **Plant-to-fungi HGT is likely understudied relative to the reverse direction.** The asymmetry in documentation between plant-to-fungi and fungi-to-plant HGT likely reflects a combination of biological reality and methodological bias. Fungi-to-plant transfers may be more detectable because plant genomes are generally larger and more slowly evolving, preserving ancient integration events as recognizable sequences. Additionally, many well-studied fungi-to-plant transfers involve metabolic pathway genes (e.g., shikimate pathway, phenylpropanoid pathway) that conferred clear adaptive advantages and were retained under purifying selection, making them easier to identify. Plant-to-fungi transfers, by contrast, may involve genes that are less functionally consequential in the fungal context or that have been lost or pseudogenized over time. The ACC deaminase case in *Amanita* is particularly notable precisely because the gene is functionally expressed and symbiosis-associated, making it an exception among plant-to-fungi transfers rather than a representative example.

### Bidirectional Exchange and Evolutionary Implications

**Bidirectional Nature of Plant–Fungus HGT.** The exchange of genetic material between plants and fungi is not a one-way street but a dynamic, bidirectional process, though the two directions differ markedly in frequency, evolutionary timing, and functional impact. Fungi-to-plant transfers are predominantly ancient, often predating the rise of land plants, and have equipped plants with foundational metabolic capabilities such as the [shikimate pathway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer) and [phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer), as well as more recent disease-resistance genes like [Fhb7 in wheat](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba5435). Plant-to-fungi transfers, while less numerous in the documented record, include functionally significant genes such as [ACC deaminase genes transferred to *Amanita* ectomycorrhizal fungi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4048297/) and are hypothesized to help fungi adapt to plant-associated niches by modulating host hormone levels. The relative scarcity of plant-to-fungi detections may reflect both genuine rarity and the greater difficulty of detecting transfers into the more compact, rapidly evolving fungal genomes. As [Fitzpatrick (2011)](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/3037/1/DF_Horizontal.pdf) noted, the fungal kingdom is one of the most densely sampled eukaryotic lineages for comparative genomics, yet the mechanisms and full scope of plant-to-fungus HGT remain understudied compared with the reverse direction.

**Evolutionary Impact on Both Kingdoms.** HGT has shaped the evolutionary trajectories of both plants and fungi in profound ways. For plants, fungal HGT has provided entire metabolic pathways—most notably the [shikimate pathway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer), whose enzymes share similarities with prokaryote homologs and may have been conveyed via eukaryote-to-eukaryote transfer—and the phenylpropanoid pathway enzyme PAL, which was likely transferred from a pre-Dikarya fungal lineage to an ancestor of land plants via [ancient arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer). These acquisitions were pivotal for plant colonization of land, enabling flavonoid and lignin synthesis. More recently, the [Fhb7 gene encoding a glutathione S-transferase](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba5435) was transferred from a fungus to *Thinopyrum elongatum* and subsequently into bread wheat (*Triticum aestivum*), conferring broad resistance to Fusarium head blight. For fungi, HGT from plants and other organisms has expanded metabolic diversity and pathogenic capabilities: the [sterigmatocystin/aflatoxin gene cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer) was transferred between *Aspergillus* and *Podospora anserina*, and [host-selective toxins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer) moved from *Stagonospora nodorum* to *Pyrenophora tritici-repentis*, enabling new host ranges. As [Rosewich & Kistler (2000)](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf) emphasized, HGT in fungi may have been more important than in other eukaryotes, contributing to genome remodeling, metabolic diversity, and the emergence of new pathogenic lineages.

**The Complexity Hypothesis.** The [complexity hypothesis of Jain, Rivera & Lake (1999)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.96.7.3801) provides a theoretical framework for understanding why certain genes are more amenable to HGT than others. Based on phylogenetic analysis of 312 orthologous gene sets across six complete prokaryotic genomes, the hypothesis distinguishes between **operational genes** (housekeeping functions: amino acid biosynthesis, intermediary metabolism, cofactor biosynthesis, cell envelope proteins) and **informational genes** (transcription, translation, tRNA synthetases, GTPases). Operational genes are transferred far more frequently because they are not embedded in large, complex macromolecular systems; their products can function independently after transfer. Informational genes, by contrast, are typically members of extensive, highly interconnected complexes (e.g., ribosomes, RNA polymerase), making successful integration and functional compatibility far less probable. The hypothesis also incorporates the **continual hypothesis**, which holds that HGT of operational genes is an ongoing process rather than a few massive ancient events. Although originally formulated for prokaryotes, this framework applies directly to plant–fungus HGT: the majority of documented transfers between fungi and plants involve operational genes (metabolic enzymes, transporters) rather than informational genes, consistent with the complexity hypothesis's predictions.

**Indicators for Detecting Ancient HGT Events.** Because most plant–fungus HGT events are ancient and rare, detection relies on indirect genomic and phylogenetic indicators rather than direct observation. [Rosewich & Kistler (2000)](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf) systematically outlined five categories of evidence: (a) **phylogenetic inconsistency**—topological discordance between a gene tree and the accepted species phylogeny; (b) **high sequence similarity**—unusually high DNA or amino acid identity between phylogenetically distant organisms; (c) **irregular distribution**—a gene or genetic element present in distantly related species but absent from closer relatives; (d) **habitat- or geography-associated sharing**—similar genes shared among unrelated species occupying the same ecological niche or geographic area; and (e) **gene characteristics inconsistent with the resident genome**—atypical GC content, codon usage, intron structure, or nucleotide composition. [Fitzpatrick (2011)](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/3037/1/DF_Horizontal.pdf) further described surrogate detection methods including patchy phyletic distribution, conserved synteny blocks absent from close relatives, and codon usage or GC composition variation along chromosomes. However, Fitzpatrick cautioned that surrogate methods (e.g., BLAST-based best-match approaches) can produce false positives and should never be used in isolation; phylogenetic inference remains the gold standard. As [Bock (2010)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19910236/) summarized in *Trends in Plant Science*, multiple independent lines of evidence should be cross-corroborated before inferring HGT.

**Alternative Explanations to Rule Out.** The indicators described above are not uniquely diagnostic of HGT. [Rosewich & Kistler (2000)](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf) emphasized that alternative explanations must be carefully excluded: **erroneous species phylogenies** (if the reference tree is wrong, apparent incongruence may be artifactual); **inappropriate comparison of paralogous sequences** (comparing a paralog rather than an ortholog can mimic HGT); **sporadic retention of shared ancestral characteristics** (differential loss in some lineages can create patchy distributions without HGT); **uneven rates of character change** across lineages (can produce long-branch attraction artifacts); and **introgressive hybridization** (sexual or parasexual gene flow between species can introduce foreign DNA without true horizontal transfer). Distinguishing HGT from these alternatives remains a "daunting task" and requires rigorous phylogenetic testing, ideally with multiple gene trees and statistical frameworks such as maximum likelihood tests, incongruence length difference tests, and parametric bootstrapping.

**Role of Transposable Elements and Mobile Genetic Elements.** Transposable elements (TEs) and other mobile genetic elements play a critical facilitative role in plant–fungus HGT. [Rosewich & Kistler (2000)](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf) documented that suspected HGT in fungi is largely restricted to "selfish" genetic elements—plasmids, introns, transposons—which have their own mobility mechanisms and can bypass barriers to horizontal transfer. TEs can mediate chromosomal rearrangement and integration of foreign genetic material, as illustrated by the [sterigmatocystin gene cluster transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer) between *Aspergillus* and *Podospora*, where mobile elements likely facilitated the large-scale genomic integration event. Plasmids, group I introns, and mycoviruses have all been implicated as vectors or facilitators of HGT in fungi, with evidence including discordant phylogenetic relationships between these elements and their host genomes, and their broad geographic distribution across unrelated fungal species. In the plant–fungus context, TEs may serve as intermediaries that capture, mobilize, and integrate foreign DNA during periods of close ecological association such as symbiosis or parasitism, when cellular barriers to DNA exchange may be transiently relaxed.

**Synthesis: A Dynamic but Asymmetric Exchange.** The collective evidence supports a model of plant–fungus HGT as a dynamic, bidirectional process that is nonetheless asymmetric in both frequency and evolutionary timing. Fungi-to-plant transfers are predominantly ancient, often dating to the colonization of land or earlier, and have provided plants with foundational metabolic pathways and, more recently, disease-resistance genes. Plant-to-fungi transfers are fewer in the documented record but functionally significant, potentially enhancing fungal adaptation to plant-associated lifestyles. The [complexity hypothesis](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.96.7.3801) explains the observed bias toward operational genes in both directions. As [Wickell & Li (2019)](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.16022) argued in *New Phytologist*, HGT has been a significant but often underappreciated force in plant evolution, with each pathway—including the fungal axis—contributing distinct types of adaptive genes. [Mehrabi et al. (2011)](https://academic.oup.com/femsre/article/35/3/542/541837) further demonstrated that HGT and even horizontal chromosome transfer in plant pathogenic fungi are powerful mechanisms for expanding host range and pathogenic capabilities, with direct implications for agricultural disease management. The combined reviews of [Rosewich & Kistler (2000)](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf), [Bock (2010)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19910236/), and [Wickell & Li (2019)](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.16022) converge on a key conclusion: while detection challenges persist—especially for ancient events—the weight of genomic, phylogenetic, and experimental evidence establishes plant–fungus HGT as a genuine and evolutionarily consequential phenomenon, not merely an artifact of incomplete data or flawed methodology.

## Between Bacteria and Plants: Agrobacterium T-DNA Transfer and Natural Transgenesis

### Agrobacterium T-DNA Transfer Mechanism and Natural Integration

**The Ti Plasmid and the T-DNA Region.** *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* is a soil-borne bacterial plant pathogen that causes crown gall disease, and its close relative *A. rhizogenes* causes hairy root disease. Both species harbor a large extrachromosomal element—the tumor-inducing (Ti) plasmid in *A. tumefaciens* and the rhizogenic (Ri) plasmid in *A. rhizogenes*—ranging from ~200 to 800 kbp in size. A defined segment of this plasmid, the T-DNA (transfer DNA), typically 10–30 kbp, is the portion actually transferred to the plant nuclear genome ([Gelvin 2003, *Annual Review of Plant Biology*](https://courses.washington.edu/esrm490h/BackgroundReading/AgroReview.pdf)). The T-DNA region is delimited by two short, highly conserved 25-bp direct repeat sequences: the left border (LB) and the right border (RB). These border sequences serve as the recognition sites for the VirD1/VirD2 border-specific endonuclease, which nicks the Ti plasmid DNA and defines the segment to be excised and transferred. The right border has been shown to be more critical for efficient T-DNA processing and transfer than the left border ([Gelvin 2003](https://courses.washington.edu/esrm490h/BackgroundReading/AgroReview.pdf)). The T-DNA region on native Ti and Ri plasmids carries bacterial genes that are eukaryotic in nature—they possess eukaryotic promoters, monocistronic transcription units, and eukaryotic polyadenylation signals—allowing them to be expressed once integrated into the plant genome ([Kado, UC Davis](https://www.uky.edu/~aghunt00/PLS620/14%20Agrobacterium%202016.pdf)).

**The *vir* Gene System and T-DNA Processing.** The processing, transfer, and integration of T-DNA are governed by a set of virulence (*vir*) genes also located on the Ti/Ri plasmid, organized into several operons. The key *vir* genes and their functions are:

- ***virA***: Encodes a membrane-localized sensor kinase that detects phenolic compounds (e.g., acetosyringone) and sugars released from wounded plant cells. VirA autophosphorylates on a histidine residue and transfers the phosphate to VirG, constituting a two-component regulatory system that activates transcription of all other *vir* genes ([Kado, UC Davis](https://www.uky.edu/~aghunt00/PLS620/14%20Agrobacterium%202016.pdf)).
- ***virG***: Encodes a DNA-binding transcriptional activator that, when phosphorylated by VirA, binds to *vir* gene promoters and induces their expression ([Kado, UC Davis](https://www.uky.edu/~aghunt00/PLS620/14%20Agrobacterium%202016.pdf)).
- ***virD1/virD2***: Encode the border-specific endonuclease that nicks the Ti plasmid at the T-DNA borders. VirD2 remains covalently attached to the 5′ end of the excised single-stranded T-DNA (the "T-strand"), which is essential for both transfer and subsequent integration into the plant genome ([Gelvin 2003](https://courses.washington.edu/esrm490h/BackgroundReading/AgroReview.pdf)).
- ***virC***: Recognizes "overdrive" sequences near the right border, enhancing T-strand production and processing efficiency ([Kado, UC Davis](https://www.uky.edu/~aghunt00/PLS620/14%20Agrobacterium%202016.pdf)).
- ***virE***: Encodes a single-stranded DNA-binding protein (VirE2) that coats the T-strand during transit through the bacterial and plant cell cytoplasm, protecting it from nucleases and facilitating nuclear import ([Gelvin 2003](https://courses.washington.edu/esrm490h/BackgroundReading/AgroReview.pdf)).
- ***virB***: A set of 11 genes (*virB1–virB11*) that encode the transfer apparatus—a type IV secretion system (T4SS) that forms a pilus and translocates the T-strand–VirD2 complex and VirE2 effector proteins from the bacterium into the plant cell ([Gelvin 2003](https://courses.washington.edu/esrm490h/BackgroundReading/AgroReview.pdf); [Zupan et al. 1997](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07352689709701951)).

Tzfira and Citovsky (2006) provided a comprehensive review of this Vir protein system, describing how the T-strand–VirD2–VirE2 complex (the "T-complex") is formed, translocated through the T4SS, imported into the plant nucleus via the host importin-α pathway, and integrated into the plant genome ([Tzfira & Citovsky 2006, *Current Opinion in Biotechnology*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0958166906000115)). Notably, the *vir* region itself is not transferred to the plant cell—only the T-DNA segment is excised and mobilized ([Kado, UC Davis](https://www.uky.edu/~aghunt00/PLS620/14%20Agrobacterium%202016.pdf)).

**T-DNA Genes and Their Effects on Plant Metabolism.** The T-DNA carried by *A. tumefaciens* typically contains genes encoding enzymes for auxin biosynthesis (e.g., *iaaM/iaaH*, tryptophan monooxygenase and indoleacetamide hydrolase) and cytokinin biosynthesis (e.g., *ipt*, isopentenyl transferase), as well as opine synthase genes (e.g., *ocs* for octopine, *nos* for nopaline). The overproduction of auxin and cytokinin in transformed plant cells disrupts normal phytohormone homeostasis, leading to uncontrolled cell proliferation and the formation of crown gall tumors. The opines—amino acid and sugar derivatives produced by the transformed plant cells—are specifically utilized by the infecting *Agrobacterium* as a carbon and nitrogen source, giving the bacterium a competitive advantage in the rhizosphere ([Gelvin 2003](https://courses.washington.edu/esrm490h/BackgroundReading/AgroReview.pdf); [Kado, UC Davis](https://www.uky.edu/~aghunt00/PLS620/14%20Agrobacterium%202016.pdf)). In *A. rhizogenes*, the Ri plasmid T-DNA carries *rol* (root-inducing loci) genes (*rolA*, *rolB*, *rolC*) that alter plant growth regulator metabolism, inducing the formation of proliferating "hairy roots" rather than tumors. The *rolB* gene product releases active auxin from inactive conjugates, while *rolC* encodes a cytokinin glucosidase that releases free cytokinins ([Matveeva et al. 2014, *Frontiers in Plant Science*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).

**Integration into the Plant Nuclear Genome.** T-DNA integration occurs through **illegitimate recombination** (also termed non-homologous end joining, NHEJ), a mechanism that joins two DNA molecules without requiring extensive sequence homology. This was demonstrated by Bundock et al. (1996), who showed that T-DNA integrates randomly at various genomic loci with microhomology (1–7 bp) at the integration junctions, and that integration does not require specific target sequences ([Bundock et al. 1996, *PNAS*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.93.26.15272)). The VirD2 protein, covalently attached to the 5′ end of the T-strand, plays a role in targeting the T-strand to the plant nucleus and may also participate in the integration reaction. Plant DNA repair proteins—including those involved in NHEJ such as Ku70/80, DNA ligase IV, and XRCC4—are recruited to facilitate T-DNA integration ([Gelvin 2003](https://courses.washington.edu/esrm490h/BackgroundReading/AgroReview.pdf)). The integration is largely random with respect to genomic position, although there is some evidence for a bias toward transcriptionally active regions and gene-rich areas ([Tzfira et al. 2004, *Trends in Genetics*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168952504001544)).

**Natural T-DNA Integration in Wild Plants.** Naturally integrated cT-DNA has been documented in multiple wild plant species, including *Nicotiana* (15+ species), *Linaria vulgaris*, *L. dalmatica*, and cultivated sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*); these cases are detailed in §3.2.

**Agrobacterium as Nature's Only Known Plant Genetic Engineer.** Unlike the other HGT pathways discussed in this report (parasitic plant haustorial transfer, fungal symbiotic transfer, viral integration), Agrobacterium-mediated T-DNA transfer is unique in being a naturally evolved, dedicated DNA transfer mechanism from a bacterium to plants. No other bacterial genus has been shown to naturally transfer DNA to plant genomes in a targeted, programmed fashion. The entire *vir* gene system—comprising sensory detection of plant signals, T-strand processing, T4SS-mediated translocation, nuclear import, and genomic integration—represents a sophisticated co-evolved molecular machinery. This natural mechanism has been harnessed for biotechnology: by replacing the native T-DNA oncogenes with genes of interest, scientists have used *Agrobacterium* as a vector to create transgenic plants in over 100 species, making it the dominant technology for plant genetic engineering ([Gelvin 2003](https://courses.washington.edu/esrm490h/BackgroundReading/AgroReview.pdf); [Tzfira & Citovsky 2006](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0958166906000115)). The discovery of naturally integrated cT-DNA in *Nicotiana*, *Linaria*, and *Ipomoea* (§3.2) demonstrates that this same mechanism has been operating in nature long before humans exploited it in the laboratory.

### Natural T-DNA Integration in Nicotiana, Linaria, and Sweet Potato

**Discovery of cT-DNA in *Nicotiana glauca* — the first naturally transgenic plant**

- In 1983, White et al. reported the surprising finding that the genome of untransformed *Nicotiera glauca* (tree tobacco) contains DNA sequences homologous to the T-DNA of the Ri (root-inducing) plasmid of *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* ([White et al. 1983, *Nature*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)). The discovery was made accidentally: while using Southern blot analysis to detect pRiA4b T-DNA sequences in laboratory-transformed *N. glauca* tissues, a hybridization signal was also detected in uninfected plant tissues, revealing that the plant's own genome carried T-DNA-like sequences. This was the first demonstration that Agrobacterium T-DNA could integrate into a plant genome and be inherited vertically in nature, without human intervention.
- The integrated sequences were termed "cellular T-DNA" (cT-DNA). Sequencing revealed that the *N. glauca* cT-DNA is organized as an **imperfect inverted repeat**, with a left arm of ~7,968 bp and a right arm of ~5,778 bp, both derived from the T-DNA of a mikimopine-type Ri plasmid (similar to pRi1724) ([Suzuki et al. 2002](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1046/j.1365-313x.2002.01468.x); [Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)). The nucleotide sequence similarity between the two arms exceeds 96%, suggesting an ancient duplication event.
- The cT-DNA carries homologs of several T-DNA oncogenes: **Ng*rolB***, **Ng*rolC*** (both left and right arm copies, though the right-arm *rolC* contains an early stop codon), **NgORF13** and **NgORF14** (present in both arms), and the opine synthesis gene **Ng*mis*** (mikimopine synthase) ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)). The T-DNA donor is assumed to be a mikimopine strain of *A. rhizogenes*.
- Furner et al. (1986) surveyed 17 *Nicotiana* species and found cT-DNA in six species from subgenera *Rustica* and *Tabacum*: *N. glauca*, *N. otophora*, *N. tomentosiformis*, *N. tomentosa*, *N. benavidesii*, and *N. tabacum* ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)). Subsequent broader screening by Intrieri and Buiatti examined 42 *Nicotiana* species representing all sections and detected at least one T-DNA gene in **15 species** across all three subgenera, indicating multiple independent integration events or widespread ancestral presence with subsequent losses ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).
- *Nicotiana tabacum* (cultivated tobacco) contains a *rolC* homolog (*trolC*) and two ORF13 homologs (*tORF13-1* and *tORF13-2*), making it also a naturally transgenic plant ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).

**cT-DNA in *Linaria* — a second independent integration event**

- T-DNA sequences were also discovered in *Linaria vulgaris* and *L. dalmatica* (toadflax), representing an independent natural integration event in a different plant family (Plantaginaceae) ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)). In *L. vulgaris*, the cT-DNA is present in **two copies** organized as a tandem imperfect direct repeat.
- The *Linaria* cT-DNA carries a distinct gene complement from the *Nicotiana* cT-DNA: **Lv*ORF2***, **Lv*ORF3***, **Lv*ORF8***, **Lv*rolA***, **Lv*rolB***, **Lv*rolC***, **Lv*ORF13***, **Lv*ORF14***, and the **Lv*mis*** gene ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)). Notably, *Linaria* retains a *rolA* homolog that is absent from the *Nicotiana glauca* cT-DNA, and the organization as a tandem direct repeat (rather than inverted repeat) differs structurally.
- All screened *L. vulgaris* and *L. dalmatica* plants contained the same T-DNA oncogenes and *mis* gene, suggesting the integration predates the divergence of these two species ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).

**Expression and evolutionary implications of cT-DNA**

- The cT-DNA genes in both *Nicotiana* and *Linaria* are **transcriptionally active** — they are still expressed in contemporary plants, indicating that they may serve functional roles rather than being silent genomic fossils ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)). The *rol* genes (root-inducing loci) are known from laboratory studies to influence plant hormone metabolism (auxin and cytokinin pathways), and their persistent expression suggests possible adaptive benefits.
- Matveeva et al. (2014) speculated that ancient plants transformed by *A. rhizogenes* might have acquired a **selective advantage** in competition with parental species, and that T-DNA insertion events may have influenced the evolution of these genera — potentially even contributing to the creation of new plant species ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).
- Critically, integrated cT-DNA differs fundamentally from crown gall tumors or hairy root disease: the T-DNA is **stably inherited through sexual reproduction**, expressed constitutively, and does **not cause tumor formation** or disease symptoms. The plants are phenotypically normal yet carry foreign bacterial DNA in their genomes ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).

**Sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) — a naturally transgenic food crop**

- In 2015, Kyndt et al. published a landmark study in *PNAS* demonstrating that the genome of cultivated sweet potato contains two distinct Agrobacterium T-DNA insertions, making it a naturally transgenic food crop ([Kyndt et al. 2015, *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)). The discovery was made unexpectedly while researchers at the International Potato Center (CIP) and Ghent University were screening the sweet potato genome for viral sequences.
- **IbT-DNA1**: This insertion was detected in **all 291 cultivated sweet potato accessions** surveyed from around the world, but was **absent from closely related wild relatives** ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1419685112); [CGSpace abstract](https://cgspace.cgiar.org/items/cb0104e3-c082-41ed-9bd6-346cfc411dea)). Its universal presence in cultivated clones but absence from wild relatives suggests that the T-DNA integration occurred **before or during early domestication** and that the insertion may have provided a trait that was **positively selected** by farmers over millennia.
- **IbT-DNA2**: This second insertion was detected in **45 of 217 genotypes** surveyed, including both cultivated and some wild species ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)). It contains at least **five intact ORFs** with significant homology to *A. rhizogenes* T-DNA genes: **ORF14**, **ORF17n**, **RolB/RolC family**, **ORF13**, and **ORF18/ORF17n** ([ResearchGate figure](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Organization-of-Ib-T-DNA1-and-Ib-T-DNA2-in-the-genome-of-sweet-potato-A-Ib-T-DNA1-of_fig1_275353186); [Shkryl et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0981942822004727)).
- Both IbT-DNA1 and IbT-DNA2 are **expressed** in sweet potato plant tissue. The genes they carry — including *rol* genes involved in root development and hormone metabolism — were shown to be transcriptionally active in the cultivar "Huachano" ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1419685112); [ScienceDaily, 2015](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150421084204.htm)). The persistence of expression over evolutionary time suggests these genes conferred a beneficial trait that was maintained during domestication.
- Sweet potato is one of the earliest domesticated crops, with archaeological evidence from the **Chilca Canyon in Peru dating to 8,000–10,000 years ago** ([TheScienceBreaker](https://www.thesciencebreaker.org/en/breaks/plant-biology/gmos-are-not-a-human-invention-sweet-potato-is-a-naturally-transgenic-food-crop)). The T-DNA integration thus likely occurred thousands of years before the modern era of biotechnology.
- The T-DNA insertions in sweet potato are derived from *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* (not *A. tumefaciens*), consistent with the pattern observed in *Nicotiana* and *Linaria* ([Kyndt et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)).

**Significance: challenging the "natural vs. artificial" dichotomy**

- The combined evidence from *Nicotiana* (15+ species), *Linaria* (2 species), and sweet potato demonstrates that **multiple plant species are naturally transgenic** — they carry stably integrated, expressed Agrobacterium T-DNA in their genomes without any human intervention ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full); [Kyndt et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)). These are not disease states but inherited genomic features passed down through sexual reproduction.
- The sweet potato case is particularly significant because it is a **major food crop** consumed globally for millennia, demonstrating that naturally transgenic organisms have been part of the human diet long before the advent of modern biotechnology. As Prof. Lieve Gheysen noted, this discovery "demonstrates that genetic modification also happens in nature" and challenges the paradigm that transgenic status is exclusively artificial ([ScienceDaily, 2015](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150421084204.htm)).
- Beyond sweet potato, the *Nicotiana* findings are also relevant to food crops: *N. tabacum* (tobacco) is itself naturally transgenic, carrying *rolC* and *ORF13* homologs in its genome ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)). While no other food crops with naturally integrated T-DNA have been confirmed as of the pre-2021 literature, the possibility remains open, particularly given that T-DNA integration events appear to be relatively recent in evolutionary terms and may have gone undetected in other species.
- The mechanism of natural T-DNA integration in these wild species is the same as that exploited in laboratory genetic engineering: *Agrobacterium* transfers T-DNA from its Ri or Ti plasmid into the plant cell, where it integrates into the nuclear genome via non-homologous recombination. The key difference is that in nature, the transformed cells gave rise to **whole, fertile plants** that inherited the T-DNA — whereas in the laboratory, tissue culture is required to regenerate whole plants from transformed cells ([Matveeva et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)).

## Between Viruses and Plants: Endogenous Viral Elements and Viral Vectors

### Endogenous Viral Elements (EVEs) in Plant Genomes

Endogenous viral elements (EVEs) are viral DNA sequences that have integrated into the host plant genome and are inherited vertically through meiosis. Unlike endogenous retroviruses, which arise from retroviruses that encode an integrase enzyme to mediate targeted insertion, most plant EVEs originate from viruses that lack integrase and integrate via **illegitimate (non-homologous) recombination** with chromosomal DNA. The distinction is critical: retroviral integration is enzyme-mediated and precise, whereas non-retroviral integration is a stochastic process that generates fragmented, often rearranged insertions. Holmes (2011), in a foundational review in *Cell Host & Microbe*, surveyed the major evolutionary aspects of EVEs across all domains of life, establishing that their formation in germline cells is a prerequisite for vertical inheritance and that they serve as molecular fossils of past viral infections ([Holmes 2011](https://www.cell.com/cell-host-microbe/fulltext/S1931-3128(11)00285-X)).

- **Caulimoviridae (pararetroviruses) as the dominant source of plant EVEs.** The family Caulimoviridae comprises double-stranded DNA pararetroviruses that replicate via reverse transcription of an RNA intermediate. Because their replication cycle involves a DNA phase, these viruses are particularly prone to genomic integration. Endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs) have been identified in numerous plant species and represent the most abundant class of plant EVEs. The integrated counterparts of most Caulimoviridae members have now been documented across diverse angiosperms, and they are sometimes so numerous that they constitute major repetitive DNA families in their host genomes ([Mette et al. 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11823438/); [Geering et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25381880/)).

- **TEPRVs: the first discovered plant EVEs.** Tobacco endogenous pararetroviruses (TEPRVs) in *Nicotiana tabacum* (tobacco) were the first virus-derived repetitive sequence family found in any plant. Mette et al. (2002) reported that TEPRVs comprise 500–1,000 copies dispersed throughout the tobacco genome, with 91–98% DNA sequence similarity across copies despite containing defective open reading frames (ORFs) that do not encode functional proteins. No corresponding exogenous virus has ever been detected, and tobacco shows no signs of infection under normal growth conditions. TEPRVs are heavily methylated at symmetric CG and CWG dinucleotides and are negligibly transcribed — features consistent with a role in **homology-dependent virus resistance** via DNA methylation-mediated silencing of incoming viral genomes. The remarkable conservation of TEPRV copy number, organization, and methylation status in both tetraploid tobacco and its diploid ancestor *Nicotiana sylvestris* since polyploid formation further supports a beneficial function. The authors proposed that the ancestral virus infected germ cell precursors, enabling meiotically heritable insertions, and that dispersed TEPRV copies could coordinately methylate invading homologous viral genomes, triggering promoter silencing and repression of virus replication ([Mette et al. 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11823438/)).

- **Banana streak virus (BSV) integration in *Musa balbisiana*.** Banana streak virus, a mealybug-transmitted member of the genus *Badnavirus* (family Caulimoviridae), provides a striking example of endogenous pararetroviral sequences that retain pathogenic potential. Gayral et al. (2008) demonstrated that *Musa balbisiana* (the B-genome banana) carries integrated BSV copies (eBSV) that originated from a **single integration event** in the banana genome. These endogenous BSV sequences are present in all B-genome bananas but are absent from *Musa acuminata* (A genome). Critically, eBSV can be **reactivated** to produce infectious virus particles — particularly in interspecific hybrids (A × B) or under stress conditions such as in vitro propagation — converting a silent genomic element into an active episomal infection. Gayral et al. (2010) further showed that two BSV species integrated approximately **640,000 years ago** in banana evolution and have since been subject to different evolutionary fates, with some copies retaining the capacity to produce infectious virions while others have degenerated ([Gayral et al. 2008](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00212-08); [Gayral et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2898222/)).

- **Endogenous florendoviruses as major components of plant genomes.** Geering et al. (2014), publishing in *Nature Communications*, revealed that endogenous florendoviruses — a new genus proposed for previously unclassified PVCV-like (Petunia vein clearing virus-like) EPRVs — are **major components of plant genomes**. From all plant genomes examined, the authors assembled 76 entire or nearly full-length florendovirus genomes of 7.2–8.5 kbp, indicating that these elements are not rare genomic fossils but abundant, widespread constituents of plant DNA. Florendoviruses are molecular fossils of reverse-transcribing viruses and represent hallmarks of virus–plant coevolution. Their discovery underscored that EVEs derived from Caulimoviridae are far more prevalent across plant lineages than previously appreciated, and that some endogenous viral lineages have no known exogenous counterpart, suggesting the ancestral viruses are extinct ([Geering et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25381880/)).

- **Widespread HGT from circular single-stranded DNA viruses.** Beyond pararetroviruses, Liu et al. (2011), publishing in *BMC Evolutionary Biology*, documented **widespread horizontal gene transfer** from circular single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) viruses — including geminiviruses, circoviruses, and nanoviruses — into the germlines of diverse eukaryotes. The study found that sequences related to these viruses have been integrated into the genomes of plants, fungi, animals, and protists. Notably, nanoviruses and circoviruses have been frequently transferred to a broad range of eukaryotic species. Geminiviruses, which are plant-specific viruses with circular ssDNA genomes, were among the earliest recognized non-retroviral plant EVEs, with initial reports dating to the mid-1990s. These findings demonstrated that the capacity for viral genomic integration is not limited to reverse-transcribing viruses but extends to any virus whose DNA form can access the host nucleus ([Liu et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198968/)).

- **Non-retroviral integration mechanisms.** The mechanism by which non-retroviral viral sequences integrate into plant nuclear genomes does not involve viral integrase enzymes. Instead, integration occurs through **illegitimate (non-homologous) recombination** between viral DNA and host chromosomal DNA, likely mediated by endogenous DNA repair pathways such as non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) or microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ). This process typically generates fragmented, rearranged insertions that lack the organized structure of retroviral proviruses. Chu et al. (2014), in a review in *Current Plant Biology*, discussed the current understanding of non-retroviral sequences integrated into plant genomes and their association with plant–virus evolution, noting that these elements represent a distinct class of EVEs with different integration dynamics and evolutionary trajectories compared to retroviral/pararetroviral EVEs ([Chu et al. 2014](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014CPBio...1...55C/abstract)).

- **Expression, exaptation, and antiviral defense.** While many EVEs are transcriptionally silent and heavily methylated (as exemplified by TEPRVs), some integrated viral genes are conserved and expressed, suggesting they have been co-opted for cellular functions — a process formally termed exaptation. The proportion of EVEs that are transcriptionally active versus silent varies by element and host species, and the concept that EVEs can serve as raw material for evolutionary innovation has gained support from comparative genomic studies showing that some endogenous viral ORFs have been preserved under purifying selection. A key hypothesized function of EVEs is providing plants with heritable antiviral defense through RNA silencing (siRNA production) and/or DNA methylation-mediated silencing of incoming viral genomes. The detailed discussion of functional co-option and antiviral defense mechanisms is presented in S4.3 ([Holmes 2011](https://www.cell.com/cell-host-microbe/fulltext/S1931-3128(11)00285-X); [Geering et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25381880/)).

- **Prevalence across plant lineages and implications for virus–plant coevolution.** EVEs are widespread across vascular plants. In strawberry (*Fragaria* spp.), recent surveys have identified abundant endogenous pararetroviruses — 786 sequences belonging to at least five endogenous viral genera (Florendovirus, Gymnendovirus, Fernendovirus, Yendovirus, Zendovirus, among others) — demonstrating that EVE diversity is not limited to a few model species. The distribution of EVEs across plant lineages reflects both the host range of the ancestral viruses and the frequency of germline integration events. Because EVEs are inherited vertically, they serve as **phylogenetic markers** to study plant evolutionary relationships: the presence or absence of specific EVE integration events in different species can reveal shared ancestry and divergence times. In strawberry, endogenous viral sequences have been proposed as markers to clarify phylogenetic and evolutionary relationships among *Fragaria* species. The ancient origin of many EVEs — some predating the divergence of major plant families — makes them valuable molecular fossils for reconstructing the deep history of virus–plant coevolution ([Geering et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25381880/); [Liu et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198968/)).

- **Genomic fossils versus functional elements: an ongoing debate.** The dual nature of EVEs — as both molecular fossils of extinct viruses and potential sources of adaptive genetic innovation — remains a central tension in the field. Some EVEs, like TEPRVs, appear to be stably maintained defensive elements; others, like eBSV in banana, retain pathogenic potential and can be reactivated under stress; still others have been co-opted for host functions. The balance between deleterious effects (insertional mutagenesis, reactivation risk) and adaptive benefits (antiviral defense, gene regulation, metabolic innovation) likely varies case by case. What is clear is that viral integration has been a recurrent and consequential force in plant genome evolution, contributing repetitive DNA, regulatory sequences, and potentially novel genes across hundreds of millions of years of plant–virus coevolution ([Holmes 2011](https://www.cell.com/cell-host-microbe/fulltext/S1931-3128(11)00285-X); [Mette et al. 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11823438/)).

### Viruses as Potential Vectors and Carriers for Inter-Plant Gene Transfer

The question of whether viruses can act as natural intermediaries to shuttle genes between distinct plant species sits at the boundary between established fact and active hypothesis. What is well-documented is the integration of viral sequences *into* plant genomes as endogenous viral elements (EVEs; covered in S4.1). What remains far less certain is whether viruses can transduce host plant genes *from one plant species to another*—a process that would require a virus to acquire a host genomic sequence during infection of one plant, then deliver that sequence into the germline of a subsequently infected plant. The evidence for this scenario is largely experimental or derived from non-plant systems, and its occurrence in natural plant ecosystems remains an open question.

**Known mechanism: host-to-virus DNA capture during infection.** The most compelling evidence that viruses can pick up host genomic sequences comes from the baculovirus–moth system. Gilbert et al. (2016) used ultra-deep sequencing of 21 baculovirus (*Autographa californica* multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus) populations infecting two moth species (*Trichoplusia ni* and *Spodoptera exigua*) and discovered 86 distinct moth DNA sequences integrated into viral genomes during infection, including 69 transposable elements from 13 superfamilies. On average, 4.8% of viruses in these populations carried at least one moth sequence, and a total of 27,504 insertions were detected across the 21 populations. Crucially, at least 21 of the moth transposable elements integrated into viral genomes showed evidence of repeated horizontal transfers between various lepidopteran species, demonstrating that viral-borne host DNA can move between host species—though no insertion was maintained after 10 successive infection cycles, indicating constant turnover rather than stable genomic integration ([Gilbert et al. 2016, PLOS Genetics](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371/journal.pgen.1005838)). This study established the proof of concept that viruses can capture host DNA and transfer it between individuals of different species, but the transient nature of the insertions in viral populations means the critical second step—stable integration of the viral-borne host sequence into the recipient germline—has not been demonstrated in this or any plant system.

**Plant-to-virus HGT: documented but rare.** Gene transfer from plants to viruses has been reported, most notably for the *Hsp70* (heat shock protein 70) gene. Chu et al. (2014) noted that HGT from plant to virus has been documented for Hsp70, and that gene transfer between plants and viruses can occur in both directions—host-to-virus and virus-to-host ([Chu et al. 2014, Current Opinion in Virology](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S221466281400005X)). Liu et al. (2011) demonstrated widespread integration of viral sequences (particularly Rep-related sequences from geminiviruses, nanoviruses, and circoviruses) into eukaryotic genomes, confirming that virus-to-plant HGT is a recurrent and ancient process ([Liu et al. 2011, BMC Evolutionary Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198968/)). However, plant-to-virus transfer of cellular genes (as opposed to viral sequences integrating into plant genomes) appears to be far rarer and less characterized, and the functional significance of such events for plant evolution remains unclear.

**The TransVir project and the viral vector hypothesis.** Clément Gilbert and colleagues formalized the conditions required for viruses to act as vectors of horizontal transfer between eukaryotes: (1) the virus must be transmissible between two host species; (2) the virus must not kill either host (so that the transferred element can persist); and (3) the transferred element must reach the germline of the second host for vertical inheritance. Their TransVir project (ANR-funded, 2015–2021) provided the first empirical support for the initial step—host-to-virus capture—showing that on average ~5% of baculovirus genomes carry a transposable element from butterfly caterpillars ([Université Paris-Saclay, 2020](http://www.universite-paris-saclay.fr/en/news/viruses-potential-genetic-vectors-transfer-between-species)). The second step—virus-to-host transfer of the captured element into a new host species—remains to be demonstrated. Gilbert has emphasized that while viruses are plausible candidate vectors because they replicate inside host cells and their genetic material is in close proximity to host DNA, the actual mechanisms of DNA exchange remain mysterious ([Université Paris-Saclay, 2020](http://www.universite-paris-saclay.fr/en/news/viruses-potential-genetic-vectors-transfer-between-species)).

**Artificial viral vectors in biotechnology: a human-engineered contrast.** Plant molecular biology routinely uses engineered viral vectors—such as those derived from *Tobacco mosaic virus* (TMV), *Potato virus X* (PVX), or *Geminivirus*—to deliver foreign genes into plant cells for transient expression or virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS). These systems exploit the natural infection cycle of plant viruses but are entirely human-designed: the viral genome is modified to carry a gene of interest, and the infection is controlled in laboratory or greenhouse settings. Unlike the hypothetical natural viral-mediated HGT, artificial viral vectors do not typically result in stable genomic integration of the delivered gene, and they are not transmitted vertically through the germline. Their existence demonstrates that viruses *can* carry genetic material between plant cells, but the engineering involved underscores how far natural systems are from achieving the same outcome without human intervention ([Wikipedia: Horizontal gene transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer)).

**Grafting as a natural route for viral and genetic transmission between plants.** Grafting—both as a horticultural practice and a natural phenomenon (root grafts between adjacent trees)—creates direct vascular connections between distinct plants, enabling the exchange of nutrients, hormones, RNA, and even entire organellar genomes. Stegemann et al. (2012) demonstrated that complete chloroplast genomes can travel across the graft junction between sexually incompatible *Nicotiana* species, resulting in stable transfer of plastid genomes ([Stegemann et al. 2012, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3289295/)). Goldschmidt (2014) reviewed evidence that both plastid and nuclear genome transfer can occur between distinct *Nicotiana* species in the graft union zone, leading to the formation of alloploid cells that can give rise to novel alloploid plants under laboratory conditions ([Goldschmidt 2014, Frontiers in Plant Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00727/full)). Wang et al. (2017) further showed that grafting can lead to direct transfer of entire nuclear and plastid genomes across the graft junction ([Wang et al. 2017, New Phytologist](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.14383)). While grafting is not a viral-mediated process per se, it is relevant here because: (1) it demonstrates that plant-to-plant transfer of large genomic fragments *does* occur naturally through intimate tissue contact, providing a precedent for the kind of transfer that viral vectors would need to achieve; and (2) grafting can transmit viruses between plants, and if a virus carrying host DNA were present in the grafted tissue, the vascular connection could theoretically facilitate its movement. However, no study has demonstrated that viruses transferred through grafting carry host genomic sequences between plant species in nature.

**Comparison with other HGT pathways.** The four HGT pathways examined in this report differ markedly in their documentation and mechanistic clarity. Agrobacterium-mediated transfer (S3) is the best-characterized natural mechanism for cross-species gene transfer into plants, with documented cases of stable, inherited integration in wild species. Parasitic plant HGT (S1) is also well-supported, with dozens of confirmed events and mechanistic understanding via haustorial connections. Fungal-plant HGT (S2) is supported primarily by ancient events detected through phylogenomics. Viral-mediated inter-plant gene transfer, by contrast, lacks direct empirical support in natural plant ecosystems. The evidence from the baculovirus–insect system shows that the *mechanism* is biologically plausible, but the key gap is demonstrating stable germline integration in a recipient plant species following viral transduction of a donor plant's gene.

**Implications if confirmed.** Were viral-mediated inter-plant HGT to be documented in nature, it would represent a potentially significant but currently unquantified force in plant evolution. Viruses infect a vast range of plant species, and their replication inside host cells places them in intimate contact with host DNA. If even a tiny fraction of viral infections resulted in the capture and transfer of host genes to new plant species, the cumulative effect over evolutionary time could rival that of parasitic plant HGT or Agrobacterium-mediated transfer. The phenomenon could facilitate the spread of adaptive traits—such as disease resistance genes or metabolic innovations—across distantly related plant lineages that lack the tissue-contact mechanisms of parasitic plants or the wound-entry route of Agrobacterium. However, given the current absence of direct evidence, viral-mediated inter-plant HGT remains a hypothesis requiring targeted empirical investigation, with the TransVir framework providing a roadmap for the necessary experiments.

### Functional Co-option of Viral Genes in Plant Genomes

- **EVEs as a genomic fossil record with dual potential.** Endogenous viral elements (EVEs) — viral DNA sequences integrated into host plant genomes and inherited vertically — were long regarded as mere genomic "junk" or molecular fossils of past infections. However, accumulating evidence shows that some EVE-derived genes have been co-opted (exapted) for host cellular functions, transforming viral integration from a potentially deleterious event into a source of evolutionary innovation. Holmes (2011) provided a foundational review of EVE evolution, emphasizing that these elements represent an ancient and ongoing process by which viral sequences become permanent fixtures of host genomes, with outcomes ranging from complete transcriptional silence to functional integration ([Holmes 2011, *Cell Host & Microbe*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22018237/)).

- **Antiviral defense via integrated viral sequences — "immunological memory."** One of the most significant proposed functions of EVEs is antiviral defense. Plants lack an adaptive immune system but possess RNA silencing (RNA interference, RNAi) as a central antiviral pathway. During viral infection, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) intermediates are processed by DICER-like (DCL) enzymes into small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) that guide the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to degrade viral RNA ([Li et al. 2018, *PLOS Pathogens*](https://journals.plos.org/plospathogens/article?id=10.1371/journal.ppat.1007228)). Integrated viral sequences can serve as a form of "immunological memory": when homologous viruses reinfect the plant, EVE-derived siRNAs can prime the RNA silencing machinery for a faster and stronger defense. This concept is supported by the observation that EVEs are targeted by RNA silencing pathways in plants — viral siRNAs are produced from endogenous viral sequences, and this epigenetic regulation can keep EVEs transcriptionally repressed, preventing their reactivation while simultaneously providing a reservoir of antiviral siRNAs ([Chabannes et al. 2024, *New Phytologist*](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.20112); [Wang et al. 2022, *Current Opinion in Virology*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S221457452100136X)). In banana, endogenous Banana streak virus (eBSV) sequences in *Musa balbisiana* (B genome) are controlled at the epigenetic level in BB diploids, preventing their awakening while maintaining a silencing-based regulatory mechanism ([Chabannes et al. 2024](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.20112)).

- **Transcriptionally active vs. silent EVEs.** The proportion of EVEs that are transcriptionally active versus silent varies considerably across plant species and viral families. Many EVEs are fragmented, rearranged, and transcriptionally silent — effectively "broken" and "in hiding" — having accumulated mutations that render them nonfunctional over evolutionary time. For example, in sugar beet (*Beta vulgaris*), endogenous pararetroviruses are largely silenced and have escaped elimination from the genome through epigenetic taming ([bioRxiv 2020](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.12.02.407783.full)). In tomato, endogenous pararetroviruses (EPRVs) are mostly silent but some are co-expressed with host gene networks during fruit ripening, suggesting tissue-specific regulatory roles ([Current Opinion in Plant Biology 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214662825001331)). In *Fritillaria imperialis*, an endogenous pararetrovirus repeat is transcriptionally active and retains a more open chromatin configuration ([The Plant Journal 2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/tpj.12673)). The EgEVE_1 element in *Eucalyptus* represents a genome-wide analysis of a transcriptionally active EVE, demonstrating that some viral integrations retain expression capacity ([PMC 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5452135/)). In yam species, transcriptionally active endogenous geminiviral sequences have been identified that may be functionally expressed ([MDPI Viruses 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4915/16/8/1306)). Overall, the majority of EVEs appear to be transcriptionally silent, with a minority retaining expression — but this minority is biologically significant.

- **Exaptation of viral genes for cellular functions beyond defense.** Liu et al. (2011) demonstrated widespread horizontal gene transfer from circular single-stranded DNA viruses (geminiviruses, circoviruses, nanoviruses) to eukaryotic genomes and found that some of the transferred viral genes were **conserved and expressed**, suggesting that these genes have been co-opted to assume cellular functions in the host genomes ([Liu et al. 2011, *BMC Evolutionary Biology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198968/)). Chu et al. (2014) reviewed the evolution of endogenous non-retroviral genes integrated into plant genomes, documenting that plant-virus co-evolution has produced EVEs with diverse fates — from complete pseudogenization to functional integration into host regulatory networks ([Chu et al. 2014, *Current Opinion in Plant Biology*](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014CPBio...1...55C/abstract)). The mechanisms of co-option include: (1) viral promoter sequences being co-opted as host gene regulatory elements, (2) viral coding sequences being repurposed for metabolic or structural functions, and (3) viral sequences serving as sources of non-coding RNAs that regulate host gene expression. However, it must be noted that most functional co-option of plant EVEs has been **inferred computationally** (through phylogenetic conservation, expression data, and sequence analysis) rather than confirmed through rigorous experimental validation such as knockout or complementation studies. This contrasts with animal systems, where retroviral gene co-option has been experimentally confirmed more extensively (e.g., syncytin genes in mammals).

- **EVEs as phylogenetic markers.** The use of endogenous viral sequences as phylogenetic markers — including the strawberry (*Fragaria*) EPRV survey (786 sequences across five viral lineages) and the diagnostic BSV integration marker in *Musa balbisiana* — is discussed in S4.1 (Prevalence across plant lineages and implications for virus–plant coevolution).

- **Deleterious effects vs. adaptive benefits.** Viral integration is a double-edged sword. The potentially deleterious effects include: disruption of host genes at integration sites, production of harmful viral proteins, and metabolic burden of maintaining large integrated sequences (e.g., endogenous pararetroviruses can constitute a significant fraction of plant genomes). Plants have evolved mechanisms to control EVE integration and expression, including RNA silencing pathways that keep EVEs epigenetically repressed and DNA methylation that prevents reactivation ([Chabannes et al. 2024](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.20112)). The adaptive benefits — while real — appear to be the exception rather than the rule: most EVEs are neutral or slightly deleterious, with functional co-option occurring only in a subset of cases where the viral sequence happens to provide a selective advantage.

- **Comparison of viral HGT with other HGT pathways.** A cross-pathway comparison of viral HGT with bacterial (Agrobacterium), fungal, and parasitic plant HGT — including relative frequency, functional categories, and evolutionary timescales — is provided in S5.1 (Comparative Analysis of HGT Mechanisms Across the Four Pathways).

- **RNA silencing as the primary mechanism targeting EVEs.** The RNA silencing pathway — involving DCL enzymes, RNA-dependent RNA polymerases (RDRs), and ARGONAUTE (AGO) proteins — targets EVEs in a manner analogous to its targeting of exogenous viruses. EVE-derived dsRNA transcripts are processed into siRNAs that can silence both the EVE itself (autonomous regulation) and homologous exogenous viruses (cross-protection). This dual function makes the RNA silencing pathway a critical interface between viral integration and plant immunity. The 24-nucleotide siRNA class, associated with RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM), is particularly important for maintaining EVE repression in plants, as demonstrated by the epigenetic control of endogenous pararetroviruses in *Fritillaria* and banana ([The Plant Journal 2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/tpj.12673); [Chabannes et al. 2024](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.20112)). This epigenetic regulation not only prevents EVE reactivation but also provides a tunable system for plants to balance the potential benefits of EVE expression against the risks of viral reactivation.

## Synthesis and Cross-Cutting Perspectives

### Comparative Analysis of HGT Mechanisms Across the Four Pathways

The four HGT pathways examined in this report—parasitic plant ↔ host, fungi ↔ plants, bacteria → plants, and viruses → plants—differ markedly in their mechanisms, directionality, frequency, functional outcomes, and evolutionary timescales. Yet collectively they reveal that HGT is a significant and recurrent force in plant evolution, contributing adaptive genes across a wide range of functional categories.

- **Mechanistic basis of transfer.** Each pathway relies on a distinct biological interface. Parasitic plant HGT is enabled by the **haustorium**, a specialized feeding organ that penetrates host tissues and establishes direct cytoplasmic continuity through plasmodesmata and phloem-to-phloem connections, allowing movement of nuclear and mitochondrial DNA as well as RNA between parasite and host ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). Fungus–plant HGT is facilitated by intimate ecological associations—symbiotic (arbuscular mycorrhizal) or parasitic—where cellular proximity and shared niches enable DNA exchange, often mediated by transposable elements and mobile genetic elements ([Wikipedia: Plant–fungus HGT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)). Bacterial HGT to plants is mechanistically the best characterized: *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* employs a dedicated **Ti plasmid-encoded type IV secretion system** (VirB/VirD4) to transfer a defined T-DNA segment into the plant nuclear genome, making it "the only known natural genetic engineer of plants" ([Tzfira & Citovsky 2006, Annual Review of Plant Biology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16677432/); [Kyndt et al. 2015, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)). Viral HGT occurs when viral DNA—primarily from **Caulimoviridae** (pararetroviruses) and **Geminiviridae** (circular ssDNA viruses)—integrates into the host nuclear genome, often via non-homologous end joining or reverse transcription intermediates ([Liu et al. 2011, BMC Evolutionary Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198968/); [Geering et al. 2014, Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms6269)).

- **Directionality of transfer.** The pathways differ fundamentally in whether exchange is unidirectional or bidirectional. **Parasitic plant ↔ host transfer is bidirectional**, with genomic-scale bidirectional mRNA exchange quantitatively demonstrated (see S1.2 for Kim et al. 2014 findings). **Fungus ↔ plant transfer is also bidirectional**, though the directions are asymmetric in functional impact: fungi have donated major metabolic pathways to ancestral land plants (see S2.1), while plants have donated genes such as **ACC deaminase** to ectomycorrhizal *Amanita* fungi (see S2.2; [Nascimento et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4048297/)). **Bacteria → plant transfer is predominantly unidirectional**: *Agrobacterium* transfers T-DNA into plant genomes, and while plants can donate genes to bacteria in other contexts, the Agrobacterium-mediated pathway itself flows from bacterium to plant. **Virus → plant transfer is also predominantly unidirectional**: viral sequences integrate into plant genomes as endogenous viral elements (EVEs), and although plant-to-virus HGT has been hypothesized, viral-mediated transduction of plant genes between different plant species remains largely theoretical and is not well-documented in natural ecosystems.

- **Frequency and detectability.** HGT frequency varies enormously across the four pathways and is closely tied to the age of the events. **Parasitic plant HGT is the most frequent among plant-to-plant transfers**: Yang et al. (2016) identified 52 high-confidence HGT events in Orobanchaceae transcriptomes, with frequency increasing with parasitic dependency—from the facultative hemiparasite *Triphysaria versicolor* to the obligate holoparasite *Phelipanche aegyptiaca* ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). Yang et al. (2019) further identified 108 transcribed functional HGT events in *Cuscuta campestris* alone. **Agrobacterium-mediated natural transgenesis** has been documented in at least 15 *Nicotiana* species, *Linaria vulgaris*, *L. dalmatica*, and cultivated sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*), representing multiple independent integration events ([Matveeva et al. 2014, Frontiers in Plant Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full); [Kyndt et al. 2015, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)). **Fungus–plant HGT is rare and ancient**: Richards et al. (2009) identified only 14 candidate plant–fungus HGT events in the rice genome, nine showing infrequent transfer patterns, and noted that most such events predate the rise of land plants ([Richards et al. 2009, Plant Cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)). **Viral HGT (EVEs) is widespread but ancient**: endogenous viral elements are major components of many plant genomes, with endogenous florendoviruses alone constituting a significant fraction of some plant genomes ([Geering et al. 2014, Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms6269)), and widespread HGT from circular ssDNA viruses to eukaryotic genomes has been documented ([Liu et al. 2011, BMC Evolutionary Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198968/)).

- **Evolutionary timescales.** The four pathways span vastly different timescales, which directly affects their detectability. **Fungus–plant and virus–plant HGT events are predominantly ancient**, often predating the divergence of major land plant lineages. The shikimate pathway and PAL transfers from fungi to plants occurred in ancestral land plants, and most EVEs in plant genomes are molecular fossils of infections that occurred millions of years ago ([Wikipedia: Plant–fungus HGT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer); [Geering et al. 2014, Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms6269)). **Parasitic plant HGT events are more recent and ongoing**, as evidenced by the correlation with parasitic dependency, the presence of intact introns in transferred genomic fragments, and the expression of HGT-acquired genes in the haustorium ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). **Agrobacterium-mediated natural transgenesis** represents recent events on an evolutionary timescale: the IbT-DNA1 insertion in sweet potato is present in all cultivated clones but absent from wild relatives, suggesting integration after domestication (within the last 8,000–10,000 years) ([Kyndt et al. 2015, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)). This gradient—from ancient (fungal, viral) to recent (parasitic, bacterial)—means that the most mechanistically well-characterized pathways (parasitic plant and Agrobacterium) are also the easiest to detect, while ancient transfers require phylogenomic inference and are more prone to artifacts.

- **Functional categories of transferred genes.** Each pathway contributes distinct types of adaptive genes to plant genomes, though some functional themes recur. **Parasitic plant HGT** contributes genes preferentially expressed in the haustorium and strongly biased toward functions contributing to parasitic feeding—nutrient acquisition, haustorial development, and suppression of host defenses ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). Stolen host genes benefit the parasite by enhancing parasitism; Yang et al. (2019) showed that related *Cuscuta* species independently acquired similar functional host genes, a phenomenon termed **"convergent horizontal gene transfer."** **Fungus–plant HGT** has contributed major metabolic capabilities: the **shikimate pathway** (from symbiotic fungi to ancestral land plants), **phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL)** (from a pre-Dikarya fungal lineage to ancestral land plants via arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis), and disease resistance genes such as **Fhb7** (a glutathione S-transferase conferring Fusarium head blight resistance in wheat, derived from *Thinopyrum elongatum* via a fungal donor) (see S2.1 for detailed discussion; [Richards et al. 2006, Eukaryotic Cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer); [Emiliani et al. 2009, Biology Direct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)). In rice, 14 candidate HGT events included genes for sugar transport, siderophore biosynthesis, and membrane transport ([Richards et al. 2009, Plant Cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)). **Bacteria–plant HGT** via Agrobacterium has introduced **rol genes** (root-inducing loci), auxin biosynthesis genes (*iaaM*, *iaaH*), and opine biosynthesis genes (*acs*, *mis*, *mas*) into plant genomes; in naturally transgenic species like sweet potato, these genes are expressed and may have been co-opted for developmental functions ([Kyndt et al. 2015, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1419685112); [Matveeva et al. 2014, Frontiers in Plant Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full)). **Virus–plant HGT** has contributed **EVEs** that can be co-opted for antiviral defense (via RNA silencing and siRNA production against related viruses), gene regulation, and potentially metabolic functions (see S4.3 for detailed discussion of functional co-option; [Liu et al. 2011, BMC Evolutionary Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198968/)). The recurring functional themes across pathways include **disease resistance** (Fhb7 from fungi, EVE-derived antiviral defense from viruses), **metabolic capabilities** (shikimate pathway and PAL from fungi, rol genes from Agrobacterium), and **symbiotic/parasitic adaptation** (haustorium-related genes from host plants, ACC deaminase from plants to fungi).

- **Unifying frameworks.** The "complexity hypothesis" (Jain et al. 1999) provides a unifying explanation for why operational genes (metabolism, transport, stress response) are transferred more frequently than informational genes (replication, transcription, translation) across all four pathways (see S5.2 for detailed discussion).

- **Overall significance for plant evolution.** Bock (2010) established in a foundational review that HGT in plants is "increasingly being recognized as" a significant evolutionary force, with mechanisms ranging from illegitimate recombination to vector-mediated transfer, and that transferred genes can be functionally integrated and subject to purifying selection ([Bock 2010, Trends in Plant Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19910236/)). Wickell & Li (2019) further argued that "HGT to and from plant lineages is more common than previously suspected" and has "undoubtedly had profound impacts on plant evolution as a whole" ([Wickell & Li 2019, New Phytologist](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.16022)). The four pathways examined here collectively demonstrate that HGT has contributed to: (1) **adaptation to parasitism** (host-derived haustorium-enhanced genes in *Cuscuta* and *Striga*), (2) **acquisition of metabolic capabilities** (shikimate pathway and PAL from fungi, opine and auxin metabolism from Agrobacterium), (3) **disease resistance** (Fhb7 GST from fungi, EVE-derived antiviral RNA silencing from viruses), and (4) **symbiotic interactions** (ACC deaminase from plants to *Amanita* fungi, PAL transfer via arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis). No single pathway dominates; rather, each contributes a distinct portfolio of adaptive genes at different evolutionary timescales, and together they paint a picture of plant genomes as dynamic, permeable systems shaped by continuous genetic exchange with diverse organisms.

### Methodological Challenges in HGT Detection

Detecting horizontal gene transfer (HGT) in plant genomes is a formidable analytical challenge that spans all four pathways examined in this report—parasitic plant↔host, fungi↔plant, bacteria↔plant, and virus↔plant. The core difficulty lies in distinguishing genuine horizontally acquired sequences from artifacts arising from contamination, phylogenetic error, paralogy, and incomplete lineage sorting. These challenges are not uniform across pathways: recent events (e.g., Agrobacterium-mediated T-DNA integration, parasitic plant HGT) leave clearer genomic signatures, whereas ancient transfers (e.g., fungal-to-plant, viral-to-plant) are obscured by millions of years of subsequent evolution.

#### Shared Methodological Challenges Across All Four Pathways

- **Contamination in sequencing data.** A pervasive risk in any phylogenomic screen is the misidentification of contaminant sequences—whether from host tissue, associated microbes, or laboratory reagents—as horizontally acquired plant genes. Yang et al. (2016) explicitly noted that "HGT inferences with gene trees can also suffer from high error rates due to factors including misidentification of contaminant sequences" and therefore implemented a comprehensive validation procedure involving PCR, genomic library screening, and transcriptome evidence to rule out contamination ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). Bock (2010) similarly emphasized that stringent controls against contamination are a prerequisite for any HGT claim involving plants ([Bock 2010, Trends in Plant Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19910236/)).

- **Database incompleteness.** Early HGT detection in plants relied heavily on BLAST-based approaches, which are "subject to high false positive errors from incomplete sampling in the database" ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). When donor lineages are unsampled or underrepresented in public databases, a plant gene may appear to have a close fungal or bacterial homolog simply because the true intermediary taxa have not been sequenced. This problem is especially acute for ancient transfers where intermediate donor lineages may be extinct. Rosewich & Kistler (2000) noted that HGT in eukaryotes "remains largely anecdotal" partly because genomic databases were insufficient to distinguish HGT from vertical inheritance with missing taxa ([Rosewich & Kistler 2000, Annual Review of Phytopathology](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)).

- **Phylogenetic artifacts: long-branch attraction and incomplete lineage sorting.** Gene trees can conflict with species trees for reasons unrelated to HGT. Long-branch attraction (LBA) can artifactually group distantly related sequences on long branches, mimicking the topology expected under HGT. Incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) can cause gene trees to differ from the species phylogeny due to the retention of ancestral polymorphisms, producing patterns that resemble horizontal transfer. Yang et al. (2016) listed "insufficient taxon sampling, complex gene birth and death processes, gene tree errors, inappropriate rooting, and frame-shift errors" as sources of error in HGT inference from gene trees ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). Rosewich & Kistler (2000) cautioned that "discriminating between HGT and alternatives can be a daunting task" because phylogenetic incongruence alone is not proof of HGT ([Rosewich & Kistler 2000](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)).

- **Paralogous sequences.** Inappropriate comparison of paralogous sequences—genes related by duplication rather than speciation—can produce false HGT signals. If a gene duplication occurred in an ancestral lineage and one paralog was subsequently lost in some descendants but retained in others, a phylogeny may erroneously suggest that the retained paralog was horizontally acquired from a distant relative. Rosewich & Kistler (2000) listed "inappropriate comparison of paralogous sequences" as a key alternative explanation that must be ruled out ([Rosewich & Kistler 2000](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)).

#### Indicators Used to Infer HGT and Alternative Explanations to Rule Out

- **Phylogenetic inconsistency (character-state discordance).** The primary indicator of HGT is a gene tree topology in which a sequence from one species nests within a clade of distantly related organisms, contradicting the accepted species phylogeny. Rosewich & Kistler (2000) described this as "inconsistency between the phylogeny of the genetic element in question and the traditionally accepted phylogeny of the organisms" ([Rosewich & Kistler 2000](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)). Yang et al. (2016) formalized this into three topological models, requiring bootstrap support ≥50 at key nodes to flag a candidate HGT ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).

- **High sequence similarity across distant taxa.** Unusually high DNA or amino acid identity between phylogenetically distant organisms can signal recent HGT. However, this indicator is less reliable for ancient transfers, where sequence divergence erases the signal of high similarity. As the Wikipedia summary of plant–fungus HGT notes, high similarity is a useful indicator but must be interpreted cautiously because "alternative hypotheses and explanations for such findings include erroneous species phylogenies" ([Wikipedia: Plant–fungus horizontal gene transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)).

- **Irregular distribution patterns.** A gene that is patchily distributed across a phylogeny—present in distantly related species but absent from closer relatives—may indicate HGT rather than repeated loss. Rosewich & Kistler (2000) listed "irregular distribution of a genetic element in a variety of species or lineages" as a key indicator ([Rosewich & Kistler 2000](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)). However, uneven rates of gene loss across lineages can produce similar patterns, so this indicator must be combined with phylogenetic evidence.

- **Genomic composition anomalies: GC content, codon usage, and intron characteristics.** Horizontally acquired genes often retain compositional features of their donor genome, such as atypical GC content, codon usage bias, or intron structures that differ from the recipient genome. Rosewich & Kistler (2000) listed "characteristics of a gene (GC content, codon usage, introns, etc.) inconsistent with the resident genome" as a key indicator of past HGT ([Rosewich & Kistler 2000](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)). Parametric methods that scan genomes for segments deviating in GC content or codon usage from the genomic background have been used since the earliest systematic HGT screens, including the identification that ~18% of the *E. coli* genome consists of horizontally acquired sequences ([Rosewich & Kistler 2000](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)). However, compositional convergence and mutational bias can also produce atypical GC content, so this indicator alone is insufficient.

- **Alternative explanations that must be ruled out.** Rosewich & Kistler (2000), following Avise, outlined five non-HGT explanations for apparent character-state discordance: (1) erroneous species phylogenies, (2) inappropriate comparison of paralogous sequences, (3) sporadic retention of shared ancestral characteristics, (4) uneven rates of character change in different lineages, and (5) introgressive hybridization ([Rosewich & Kistler 2000](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/HCK/horizon.pdf)). All of these must be systematically excluded before HGT is invoked. Introgressive hybridization is particularly relevant for plant–plant transfers (both parasitic and viral-mediated), as interspecific hybridization can transfer entire genomes across species boundaries without true HGT.

#### The Complexity Hypothesis and Differential HGT Rates

- **Informational vs. operational genes.** The complexity hypothesis, formulated by Jain, Rivera, and Lake (1999), proposes that a major factor governing HGT frequency is gene complexity: "informational genes are typically members of large, complex systems," making them less likely to be successfully transferred, while operational genes—those encoding standalone functions such as metabolic enzymes or transporters—are transferred at higher frequencies ([Jain et al. 1999, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.96.7.3801)). This framework applies across all four pathways examined in this report. For instance, in the fungal-to-plant transfers documented by Richards et al. (2009), the 14 candidate HGT events in rice were predominantly operational genes—sugar transporters, alcohol dehydrogenases, siderophore biosynthesis proteins—rather than informational genes such as ribosomal proteins or DNA polymerase subunits ([Richards et al. 2009, Plant Cell](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/); [Wikipedia: Plant–fungus horizontal gene transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)). Similarly, the 52 HGT events identified in Orobanchaceae by Yang et al. (2016) were "strongly biased in predicted gene functions contributing to haustorial function"—operational genes involved in metabolism and transport rather than core informational processes ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)).

- **Continual transfer of operational genes.** Jain et al. (1999) further proposed that operational genes have been horizontally transferred continuously since the divergence of prokaryotes, rather than in discrete bursts, implying that HGT is an ongoing process that is difficult to capture in snapshot genomic comparisons ([Jain et al. 1999, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.96.7.3801)). This continual model means that the HGT events detected in any given study represent only a fraction of the total transfer activity, with many transient acquisitions lost to selection or drift before they can be detected.

#### Detection Difficulty Gradient: Recent vs. Ancient Events

- **Recent HGT events (Agrobacterium, parasitic plants).** HGT events that occurred recently in evolutionary time are comparatively easier to detect because the transferred sequences retain high similarity to their donor genomes, compositional signatures are still distinct, and integration sites may be identifiable. In the Agrobacterium system, naturally integrated T-DNA (cT-DNA) in *Nicotiana glauca* and sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) was identified by direct sequence homology to known T-DNA sequences, and the rol genes and other T-DNA-encoded ORFs were clearly of bacterial origin ([Matveeva et al. 2014, Frontiers in Plant Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2014.00326/full); [Kyndt et al. 2015, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1419685112)). In parasitic plants, Yang et al. (2016) exploited transcriptome data from parasites grown on hosts with known genome sequences, allowing them to identify donor lineages with high confidence and validate integration using genomic libraries ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). The ability to grow parasite and host together and trace mobile nucleic acids in real time—as in the Kim et al. (2014) study of *Cuscuta pentagona*, which quantified bidirectional mRNA exchange—provides a temporal anchor that is unavailable for ancient transfers.

- **Ancient HGT events (fungi, viruses).** Detecting ancient transfers is substantially harder because accumulated mutations erode sequence similarity, compositional signatures decay toward the recipient genome's equilibrium, and intermediate donor lineages may be extinct. As the Wikipedia summary of plant–fungus HGT states, "since these events are rare and ancient, they have been difficult to detect and remain relatively unknown" ([Wikipedia: Plant–fungus horizontal gene transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant%E2%80%93fungus_horizontal_gene_transfer)). Richards et al. (2009) identified only 14 candidate plant–fungus HGT events in the entire rice genome, and noted that "most plant–fungus HGT events are ancient and rare" ([Richards et al. 2009, Plant Cell](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729602/)). For viral endogenous elements (EVEs), the challenge is compounded by the fact that viral sequences may integrate via non-homologous end joining or microhomology-mediated repair rather than site-specific integration, producing fragmented and rearranged insertions that are difficult to distinguish from genomic decay products. The distinction between retroviral/pararetroviral integration (via reverse transcription) and non-retroviral integration mechanisms further complicates detection, as different integration mechanisms leave different genomic footprints.

- **Timescale and signal decay.** The detection difficulty gradient can be understood as a function of time since transfer: recent events (years to thousands of years) preserve donor-like composition and high sequence identity; intermediate-age events (millions of years) retain phylogenetic signal but lose compositional distinctiveness; ancient events (tens to hundreds of millions of years) may leave only a phylogenetic signal that is increasingly vulnerable to erosion by subsequent substitutions, gene conversion, and loss of the donor lineage. Yang et al. (2016) demonstrated that genomic DNA integration was far more common than retro-processed RNA integration in parasitic plants, partly because genomic fragments are more stable over evolutionary time and thus more likely to be detected ([Yang et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1608765113)). For the most ancient transfers, such as the proposed shikimate pathway and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) transfers from fungi to ancestral land plants, detection relies entirely on phylogenetic incongruence and the absence of the gene in outgroup lineages—indicators that are inherently more ambiguous than direct sequence comparison.

#### Synthesis

Across all four HGT pathways, the methodological toolkit is broadly similar—phylogenomic screening, compositional analysis, PCR/transcriptomic validation, and careful exclusion of alternative explanations—but the weight given to each method shifts with the age and nature of the event. Recent transfers (Agrobacterium T-DNA, parasitic plant HGT) can be validated by direct sequence comparison and experimental demonstration of integration, while ancient transfers (fungal-to-plant metabolic pathways, viral EVEs) require sophisticated phylogenetic reconstruction and the systematic exclusion of ILS, paralogy, and compositional convergence. The complexity hypothesis provides a unifying framework explaining why operational genes dominate HGT inventories across all pathways, and why informational genes are rarely transferred. As Wickell & Li (2019) argued in their evolutionary synthesis, the cumulative evidence from all four pathways demonstrates that HGT is a significant but often underappreciated force in plant evolution, with detection challenges being the primary barrier to a fuller understanding of its scope ([Wickell & Li 2019, New Phytologist](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.16022)).
