# Can a Vision-Language Model Substitute for Specialist Labelers of Skin-Lesion Images? A Technical Report with an Experimental Plan

## Executive Summary

The question this report addresses is narrower than "can AI diagnose skin disease": it is whether a modern **vision-language model (VLM)** — a general-purpose multimodal system such as GPT-4V/GPT-5, Gemini, or Claude — can replace board-certified dermatologists as the **labeling workforce** for medical images of skin lesions, for example when constructing training and evaluation datasets. The honest reading of the 2023–2026 evidence is: **not yet, autonomously; plausibly, as a pre-labeler inside a gated hybrid pipeline.** General-purpose VLMs remain well below dermatologists on image-only skin-lesion diagnosis — GPT-4V reached only **6.2% top-1 accuracy** on a standardized dermatology benchmark in a Stanford evaluation ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.04.12.24305744v2.full.pdf)) and **66.7% sensitivity for malignancy versus 87.0% for dermatologists** on paired clinical–dermoscopic images ([JAAD International](https://www.jaadinternational.org/article/S2666-3287(24)00177-9/pdf)). Newer generations are improving — GPT-5 reached **68.0% top-1 accuracy and 96.6% top-3 accuracy** on 500 HAM10000 images ([Diagnostics](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/23/3052)) — but still show a dangerous asymmetry: a tendency to call malignant lesions benign, plus documented performance drops on darker skin tones ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.04.12.24305744v2.full.pdf)). Meanwhile, the economics of machine pre-labeling are compelling: in a controlled crowdsourcing study, zero-shot GPT-4 matched or beat a carefully built MTurk pipeline at **$122 versus $4,508** ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3613904.3642834)), and AI pre-annotation cut physician annotation workload by at least **30%** in a medical-imaging database study ([PLOS/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208487/)). This report therefore proposes a **four-phase, pre-registered experimental program with explicit decision gates** (retrospective benchmark → multi-reader study → pragmatic downstream-utility trial), designed so that each phase must pass a quantitative non-inferiority gate before the next begins. The plan is executable with public datasets (HAM10000, Derm7pt, PAD-UFES-20, DDI/Fitzpatrick17k), roughly **10–15 board-certified dermatologists**, and a reference-standard budget of a few thousand histology-verified images.

## 1. Problem Framing and Decision Question

"Standing in for human specialists when labeling medical images" is a distinct claim from "matching specialists at diagnosis," and conflating the two is the most common design error in this literature. A labeler's job in dataset construction is not to manage a patient but to assign **reproducible, bias-controlled annotations** — diagnostic category, malignancy status, dermoscopic attributes (e.g., the 7-point checklist criteria), and quality flags — against a written guideline, at scale, and at a cost the project can bear. The correct decision question is therefore comparative and operational: *for a defined labeling task, does the VLM produce labels that are (a) as accurate as the labels a panel of specialists would produce against an independent reference standard, (b) at least as consistent as specialists are with each other, and (c) equivalent in downstream utility — i.e., models trained on VLM labels perform no worse than models trained on expert labels?* This three-part formulation mirrors how the field already thinks about ground truth: the HAM10000 dataset, for instance, accepted four tiers of evidence as ground truth — histopathology (>50% of lesions), reflectance confocal microscopy, follow-up stability over 1.5 years, and expert consensus — precisely because a single unassisted reader is not a gold standard ([Scientific Data](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018161.pdf)).

Two features of the problem make it harder than generic image annotation. First, **the human baseline itself is noisy**: interobserver agreement between dermatologists on dermoscopic images is only moderate — Cohen's κ of **0.47 for specific diagnosis** and **0.50 for benign-versus-malignant** in a teledermoscopy concordance study ([Dermatology Practical & Conceptual/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3663402/)), and even dermatopathologists improved from Fleiss' κ 0.447 to only 0.496 when dermoscopy was added to histologic assessment ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32338293/)). This means "perfect agreement with experts" is the wrong bar; the VLM must land **within the human inter-rater band**, a criterion we formalize in Section 4. Second, the failure modes of VLMs are qualitatively different from those of tired or inconsistent humans: VLMs hallucinate structured but wrong outputs, are sensitive to prompt phrasing, can refuse to answer (GPT-4V refused **116 of 412** radiologic prompts in one study, requiring re-prompting ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11836096/))), and may carry the benchmark images in their training data — a contamination risk that a retrospective-only design cannot rule out. The plan below treats these as testable risks rather than footnotes.

## 2. What the Current Evidence Shows

### 2.1 VLMs versus specialists on skin-lesion images

The empirical picture is consistent across study designs: general-purpose VLMs are **text-strong but image-weak** in dermatology, with rapid generational improvement. A pilot study of 54 curated images found GPT-4V identified the primary diagnosis from images alone in only **54%** of cases, versus **89%** from text scenarios and 89% for image-plus-text, with a demonstrated bias toward textual over visual evidence ([Cureus/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12686177/)). A larger Stanford benchmark across four imaging domains found GPT-4V's dermatology top-1 accuracy of **6.2%** (top-3: 21%) and — critically for a fairness arm — a **significant accuracy drop on darker skin tones** (p < 0.001) ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.04.12.24305744v2.full.pdf)). The most direct head-to-head against specialists paired dermoscopic and clinical images: GPT-4V's sensitivity for malignancy improved from 50.8% (single modality) to **66.7%**, still significantly below dermatologists' **87.0%** (p < 0.001), although its specificity (79.0%) exceeded the dermatologists' (63.2%, p = 0.024) ([JAAD International](https://www.jaadinternational.org/article/S2666-3287(24)00177-9/pdf)).

Newer models narrow but do not close the gap. GPT-5 on HAM10000 reached **68.0% top-1 accuracy, 70.9% malignancy-discrimination accuracy, and 96.6% top-3 accuracy**, outperforming GPT-4V/4T/4o, yet misclassified malignant lesions as benign more often than the reverse — an asymmetry with obvious consequences if such labels seeded a training set ([Diagnostics](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/23/3052)). On 772 histopathology-confirmed basal-cell-carcinoma images, the best web-based models (ChatGPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Flash) reached only **50.7–75.0% detection accuracy**, leading the authors to conclude current off-the-shelf LLMs are "not clinically suitable" for BCC detection ([JID Innovations](https://www.jidinnovations.org/article/S2667-0267(26)00015-9/fulltext)). Domain-adapted models do better: Med-Gemini-2D reached **73.3% accuracy** (vs. 60.6% for Gemini Ultra) on 6-class PAD-UFES-20, approaching the specialized Derm Foundation model at 76.9% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.03162v1)). Figure 1 summarizes the three studies that report sensitivity and specificity for both human specialists and an algorithm on skin-lesion images.

Head-to-head evidence: algorithms and VLMs versus human specialists on skin-lesion images

The same figure carries a second message: **task-specific CNNs already match or beat specialist averages** on constrained benchmarks — the ensemble in Tschandl et al.'s 511-clinician study exceeded the mean clinician melanoma sensitivity (81.9% vs 73.1%) ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10906187/)), and Brinker et al.'s CNN outperformed 136 of 157 dermatologists (86.6%) on a 100-image melanoma-versus-nevus task ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8705277/)). If the organizational goal is only cheap labels of benchmark quality, a fine-tuned classifier may already be the rational tool; the case for a VLM rests on its **flexibility** (multi-attribute labels, rationales, free-text descriptions, new label schemas without retraining) and its **lower marginal cost per new task**. The experiment should therefore compare the VLM not only against humans but against a domain-adapted classifier baseline, so the decision is "VLM versus specialist versus specialist-model pre-labeler," not "VLM versus nothing."

### 2.2 LLM-as-annotator economics and downstream evidence

Outside dermatology, the substitution question has been tested more directly. In a CHI 2024 experiment, zero-shot GPT-4 matched or exceeded a meticulously engineered MTurk pipeline on labeling accuracy, cost **$122.08 versus $4,508**, and took two days rather than weeks ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3613904.3642834)). In medical imaging, a stepwise pre-annotation loop (AI labels, junior physicians correct, seniors verify) cut manual workload by **at least 30%** at 1,360 images and reached parity with junior physicians at 6,800 images, eliminating the junior-read step entirely ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208487/)). Most relevant to our Phase 3, a study that labeled 227,827 radiology reports with open-source LLMs found downstream image classifiers trained on LLM labels **outperformed those trained on rule-based CheXpert labels** and were resilient to label noise when the test set was expert-labeled ([Radiology: AI/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12920854/)). The caveat from human–AI interaction research is that **wrong AI suggestions actively degrade human performance**: a 12-study, 67,700-evaluation meta-analysis of skin-cancer diagnosis found AI assistance raised pooled clinician sensitivity from 74.8% to 81.1%, but clinicians performed worse when the AI was wrong — the automation-bias mechanism that will also contaminate any "human reviews VLM labels" design ([npj Digital Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01031-w?fromPaywallRec=false)).

## 3. Experimental Plan

### 3.1 Objectives, hypotheses, and scope

The program tests one primary hypothesis and three secondary hypotheses, all pre-registered before Phase 1 data are unblinded:

| ID | Hypothesis (all against pre-registered thresholds) | Primary metric |
|---|---|---|
| **H1 (primary)** | VLM sensitivity for malignancy (melanoma + BCC + SCC) is **non-inferior** to the consensus of the specialist panel, margin **−5 percentage points** | Paired sensitivity difference vs. reference standard |
| **H2** | VLM–expert agreement on diagnostic labels lies **within the observed expert–expert agreement band** (lower 95% bound of pairwise κ) | Cohen's/Fleiss' κ, Gwet's AC1 |
| **H3** | VLM attribute labels (7-point-checklist criteria) achieve per-attribute agreement **non-inferior to −10 pp** vs. expert consensus | Per-attribute AC1 / macro-F1 |
| **H4 (downstream)** | A classifier trained on VLM labels is **non-inferior** to one trained on expert labels on an independent, expert-verified test set (margin −3 pp balanced accuracy) | Balanced accuracy, AUC |

Scope decisions that must be fixed in the protocol: (1) **Tasks** — binary malignancy label, multi-class diagnosis (HAM10000 7-class / Derm7pt 5-class), and attribute-level labels from the 7-point checklist (pigment network, streaks, pigmentation, regression structures, dots/globules, blue-whitish veil, vascular structures), for which Derm7pt provides 1,011 cases with paired clinical–dermoscopic images and structured annotations ([SFU/Derm7pt via GitHub](https://github.com/xraikeele/DermFormer)). (2) **Modalities** — dermoscopic and clinical (smartphone) images tested separately, because PAD-UFES-20's 2,298 smartphone images with 21-field metadata and 58.4% biopsy confirmation probe a very different regime than curated dermoscopy ([Data in Brief/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7479321/)). (3) **Models under test** — 2–3 frontier VLMs with pinned versions and snapshot dates, plus one domain-adapted baseline (e.g., a HAM10000-trained classifier), because single-model studies (five dermatologists, one GPT-4V build, predominantly Fitzpatrick type II skin) have repeatedly produced non-generalizable conclusions ([JAAD International](https://www.jaadinternational.org/article/S2666-3287(24)00177-9/pdf)).

### 3.2 Data, reference standard, and stratification

The evaluation corpus should be assembled to make every stratum of the labeling problem visible, with **histopathology as the reference standard wherever the labeling claim is diagnostic truth** — echoing HAM10000's evidence tiers ([Scientific Data](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018161.pdf)) and the caution that even experts "had difficulties identifying whether lesions were benign or malignant purely visually," which is why pathology-confirmed labels matter ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v225/groger23a/groger23a.pdf)). A recommended composition: (a) ~1,500–2,000 dermoscopic images from HAM10000/ISIC with histopathology or confocal confirmation; (b) ~500 paired clinical–dermoscopic cases from Derm7pt for attribute labeling; (c) ~800–1,000 smartphone clinical images from PAD-UFES-20, all cancers biopsy-proven ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7479321/)); and (d) the full 656-image Diverse Dermatology Images (DDI) set plus a Fitzpatrick17k subsample for the fairness arm, because DDI is biopsy-confirmed and balanced across light and dark skin with an accompanying dermatologist panel ([Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abq6147)), while Fitzpatrick17k's pool skews roughly **47% types I–II versus 13% types V–VI** ([Emergent Mind summary of Groh et al.](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/fitzpatrick-17k-dataset)). Sampling must be stratified by diagnosis, Fitzpatrick skin type, image modality, and acquisition site, with patient-level deduplication across sources.

Two contamination controls are mandatory. First, **membership testing**: because ISIC and HAM10000 images circulate on the public web, every image should be checked for near-duplicate presence in likely training corpora where feasible, and the headline result should be replicated on a **held-out, never-published institutional set** (even a few hundred images) before any adoption decision — the gap between benchmark performance and fresh-data performance is itself a study outcome. Second, version logging: model identifiers, API dates, prompts, and decoding settings must be frozen and recorded, since an analysis of LLM studies in top medical journals found only **15.1% adequately reported stochasticity handling** ([MI-CLEAR-LLM review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13024205/)).

### 3.3 Phases and decision gates

Figure 2 shows the gated structure. Each gate is a pre-registered quantitative threshold; failing a gate does not end the program — it demotes the VLM to the **hybrid role** (pre-labeler with 100% expert verification), which the evidence already supports economically ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208487/)).

Phased experimental plan with pre-registered decision gates

**Phase 0 — Instrumentation and pilot (n ≈ 200 images).** Finalize the annotation guideline (label definitions, exemplars, tie-breaking rules) exactly as one would for human annotators; develop the structured-output schema (JSON: primary label, top-3 differential, per-attribute calls, confidence 0–100, abstain flag); freeze prompts after a small grid search over phrasing, few-shot exemplars, and chain-of-thought settings on a development slice. Phase 0 exists because prompt brittleness is a first-order effect — minor wording changes "can markedly alter outputs" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13024205/)) — and because structured outputs make label extraction deterministic rather than a parsing exercise. Deliverables: frozen prompt suite, parse-failure rate < 1%, pilot agreement estimates used only to refine the sample-size assumptions (discordance ψ) for Phase 1.

**Phase 1 — Retrospective benchmark (n ≈ 3,000–4,000 images).** The VLM labels the full corpus blinded to reference labels; the reference standard is histopathology-first per Section 3.2. Primary analysis: paired non-inferiority of malignancy sensitivity versus the specialist panel's labels (see Section 4 for sizing). Secondary analyses: per-class accuracy and macro-F1; top-3 accuracy (which reached 92–96.6% for GPT-5 on two dermoscopic sets and may already suffice for a "suggested differential" labeling aid ([Diagnostics](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/23/3052))); calibration (expected calibration error) and **risk–coverage analysis** — how accuracy improves as the lowest-confidence or self-consistency-discordant fraction is routed to humans. Abstention behavior deserves dedicated measurement because current LLMs "often remain highly confident even when predictive accuracy decreases," and verbalized uncertainty framing does not reliably fix this ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09080)); selective-prediction methods from medical text and VLM literature offer the routing machinery ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18050v1), [ACL Findings](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.767.pdf)).

**Phase 2 — Multi-reader agreement study (≥10 dermatologists).** A stratified subsample (e.g., 1,000 images) is labeled independently by at least 10 board-certified dermatologists and the VLM, all blinded to each other and to reference labels, on the same interface with the same guideline. This phase measures whether the VLM sits **inside the human disagreement cloud**: compute pairwise Cohen's κ among dermatologists to define the human band (literature values for dermoscopic diagnosis are only κ ≈ 0.47–0.53 ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3663402/))), then test whether VLM–dermatologist κ is non-inferior to the lower bound of that band. Because severe class imbalance inflates κ paradoxes, **Gwet's AC1** should be the primary agreement statistic for imbalanced attributes, as in the radiology labeling study that adopted it for exactly this reason ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12920854/)). Phase 2 also runs the **robustness battery**: 5 prompt paraphrases × 3 decoding seeds per image (label-flip rate as a brittleness metric), image perturbations (rotation, color jitter, ruler/hair artifacts), and counterfactual skin-tone manipulation, given documented color bias — darkening a lesion's pigment caused models to flip benign lesions to malignant ([JMIR Dermatology](https://derma.jmir.org/2024/1/e55508)).

**Phase 3 — Pragmatic pipeline trial (downstream utility).** The decisive, often-skipped test: train identical classifier architectures (fixed seed, architecture, and training budget) on (a) the VLM-labeled corpus, (b) the expert-labeled corpus, and (c) a hybrid corpus (VLM labels + expert correction of the uncertain fraction), then compare on a locked, expert-verified, histology-confirmed test set. The radiology-report precedent — downstream classifiers proved resilient to LLM label noise, with test-set labeling schema materially changing measured performance ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12920854/)) — is why the test set here must carry the strongest labels available. H4's non-inferiority margin (−3 pp balanced accuracy) is tested with bootstrap confidence intervals. A Phase 3 failure with Phase 1–2 passes is itself an informative result: labels can look "right" against experts yet encode systematically different decision boundaries than expert supervision would teach.

### 3.4 VLM configuration and prompting protocol

The VLM must be treated as a **measurement instrument**, not a chatbot. Configuration rules: temperature 0 (accuracy is empirically stable from 0.0–1.5 for GPT-4-class models, degrading only above ~1.75 ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13024205/))); structured JSON output with schema validation and automatic re-query on parse failure; an explicit "insufficient image quality / abstain" option, because forced-choice formats hide the refusals that occurred in 28% of radiology prompts before re-prompting ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11836096/)); and **self-consistency sampling** (k = 5 at low temperature) whose disagreement rate serves as the routing signal for selective prediction, given evidence that self-consistency variants support error-controlled abstention ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00299)). Two prompt conditions should be pre-specified as arms rather than tuned ad hoc: image-only versus image-plus-metadata (age, site, history), because the GPT-4V literature shows multimodal context can move accuracy from 54% to 89% ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12686177/)) — but the labeling pipeline must document which context a human labeler would legitimately have had, to keep the comparison fair.

### 3.5 Human comparator design

The specialist panel needs the same engineering discipline as the model. Recruit **10–15 board-certified dermatologists**, stratified by years of dermoscopy experience, mirroring the multi-reader designs that exposed experience effects in the human–machine literature ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10906187/)). Every image in the Phase 1/2 overlap receives **dual independent reads plus adjudication of disagreements by a third senior dermatologist**, replicating the consensus tier used by reference datasets themselves ([Scientific Data](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018161.pdf)). Readers are blinded to reference labels, to each other, and to which labels come from the VLM; the reading interface is identical to the model's information diet (no metadata for image-only arms). To keep the review honest, Phase 2 embeds **seeded trap images** (known histology) and repeat images (test–retest reliability per reader), because label-quality studies show expert agreement on label-error detection is itself only moderate, especially for dermoscopy ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v225/groger23a/groger23a.pdf)). Time per image is logged for the economic model in Section 5.

## 4. Endpoints and Statistical Analysis

### 4.1 Primary endpoint and sample size

The primary endpoint is the **paired difference in sensitivity for malignancy** (VLM minus specialist-panel consensus) against the histopathology reference, tested as a non-inferiority hypothesis with a McNemar-type paired analysis — the standard machinery for comparing two tests on the same subjects ([Turkish Journal of Emergency Medicine](https://turkjemergmed.com/full-text/811)). Because paired designs hinge on the **discordance rate ψ** (the fraction of reference-positive cases on which the two readers disagree), sample size cannot be fixed without a pilot estimate; Figure 3 shows the required number of malignant cases across ψ and margin choices at one-sided α = 0.025 and 90% power. At a plausible ψ = 0.15 and the pre-registered margin of −5 pp, the study needs **~630 malignant (reference-positive) images**, i.e., roughly 3,150 total images at 20% malignancy prevalence — consistent with the Phase 1 corpus in Section 3.3. Tightening the margin to −3 pp more than doubles the requirement (~1,750 malignant cases), a trade-off the steering committee should price explicitly.

Required malignant cases by non-inferiority margin and discordance rate

Multiplicity is controlled hierarchically: H1 (Phase 1) must pass before H2/H3 (Phase 2) are confirmatory, and H4 (Phase 3) is confirmatory only if earlier gates pass; within phases, secondary endpoints are reported with 95% CIs without confirmatory claims. All binary comparisons use exact or Yates-corrected McNemar variants when discordant counts are small ([MetricGate methodology notes](https://metricgate.com/docs/paired-sensitivity-specificity-mcnemar/)), and AUC comparisons use DeLong. Analyses are run once on the locked dataset; any post-hoc cut (e.g., by skin tone) is labeled exploratory unless pre-specified below.

### 4.2 Agreement, calibration, fairness, and robustness endpoints

Agreement (H2/H3) uses Gwet's AC1 as primary and Cohen's/Fleiss' κ as secondary, with the **human band** defined as the 95% bootstrap interval of pairwise dermatologist agreement; the VLM passes if its mean pairwise agreement with dermatologists exceeds the band's lower bound — a deliberately asymmetric criterion reflecting that replacement requires being *no worse than a typical expert among experts*, not better than all of them. Calibration is reported as ECE plus reliability diagrams, motivated by repeated findings that medical VLMs "consistently suffer from severe calibration errors, often assigning high confidence to incorrect predictions" ([BMVC/arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15226v1)); the operational endpoint is the **risk–coverage curve**: accuracy as a function of the coverage fraction after routing the most uncertain x% to humans. Fairness is a gate, not a garnish: malignancy sensitivity and label agreement are reported per Fitzpatrick stratum (I–II / III–IV / V–VI) with equalized-odds gaps, because both the Stanford GPT-4V benchmark ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.04.12.24305744v2.full.pdf)) and LLaVA's failure to recognize melanoma in skin of color ([JMIR Dermatology](https://derma.jmir.org/2024/1/e55508)) show tone-dependent degradation — a labeling tool that is systematically worse on dark skin encodes inequity into every downstream model. Robustness endpoints include the prompt-paraphrase label-flip rate (target < 5%), test–retest self-agreement across decoding seeds, and performance under controlled image degradations.

## 5. Economic and Operational Evaluation

The business case should be computed, not asserted. Modeled on market rates — specialist medical-image annotation is advertised around **$150/hour** ([OpenTrain listing](https://www.opentrain.ai/jobs/board-certified-radiologist-medical-image-annotation--1740644016725x528735914365026300/)) and a diagnostic label takes roughly 30–60 seconds of expert time — a gold-standard pipeline (dual reads + adjudication of ~20% of cases) for 10,000 images costs on the order of **$41,000**, while a VLM-only pipeline with 2% audit sampling costs a few hundred dollars, and a hybrid (VLM pre-labels, experts verify the ~30% uncertain or discordant fraction) lands near **$5,700** (Figure 4). These are planning assumptions to be replaced with measured times in Phase 2, but they are directionally consistent with the controlled comparisons: GPT-4's $122 versus MTurk's $4,508 ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3613904.3642834)) and the 30–70% workload reductions from AI pre-annotation in medical-imaging pipelines ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208487/)).

Illustrative cost model for labeling 10,000 skin-lesion images

Two non-obvious costs belong in the ledger. First, **verification is not free of bias**: presenting annotators with model pre-labels shifts the label distribution (automation bias), and the skin-cancer meta-analysis confirms clinicians degrade when AI advice is wrong ([npj Digital Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01031-w?fromPaywallRec=false)) — so the hybrid arm must include blinded verification of a random subset where the VLM was confident, not just where it was uncertain. Second, **model drift**: proprietary VLM behavior changes across versions, so the economic case must include periodic re-validation (a fixed audit set re-scored at each model update) as a recurring line item, with MI-CLEAR-LLM-style reporting of versions, prompts, and stochasticity as the documentation standard ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13024205/)).

## 6. Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Evidence basis | Mitigation in this plan |
|---|---|---|
| Benchmark contamination (images in VLM training data) | Public sets circulate widely; benchmark results may overstate fresh-data accuracy | Near-duplicate screening + never-published institutional holdout set as the confirmatory replication |
| Confident wrong labels (hallucination) | LLMs show poor uncertainty awareness and confident errors even with missing information ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09080)) | Self-consistency discordance routing; abstention option; risk–coverage gate in Phase 1 |
| Malignant→benign asymmetry | GPT-5 misclassified malignant as benign more than the reverse ([Diagnostics](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/23/3052)) | Sensitivity (not accuracy) as primary endpoint; per-class error taxonomy in Phase 1 |
| Skin-tone inequity | Significant accuracy drop on darker skin ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.04.12.24305744v2.full.pdf)); Fitzpatrick17k skew ([Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/fitzpatrick-17k-dataset)) | FST-stratified gate with equalized-odds reporting; DDI as balanced validation set ([Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abq6147)) |
| Prompt brittleness / non-determinism | Prompt design materially alters outputs; only 15.1% of studies report stochasticity ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13024205/)) | Prompt freeze, paraphrase battery, temperature 0, k-sample self-consistency, full logging |
| Automation bias in hybrid review | Clinicians worsen with wrong AI advice ([npj Digital Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01031-w?fromPaywallRec=false)) | Blinded review subsets; seeded trap images; measure reviewer override rates by VLM confidence |
| Refusals / parse failures | GPT-4V refused 116/412 radiology prompts before re-prompting ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11836096/)) | Structured schema with bounded re-query; refusal rate reported as a coverage cost, not silently re-prompted away |

A governance note belongs here: the study is evaluating a **labeling tool, not a diagnostic device**, so it falls short of triggering diagnostic-AI regulatory pathways; nevertheless, running it to diagnostic-study standards is what makes the conclusion defensible to the dermatologists whose work it proposes to reorganize. Reporting should follow **STARD-AI** (published September 2025 in *Nature Medicine*, extending STARD 2015 with 18 AI-specific items) ([CASRAI guideline summary](https://casrai.org/guides/stard-tripod-checklist)) and the dermatology-specific **CLEAR Derm** checklist ([JAMA Dermatology](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamadermatol.2021.4915)), with TRIPOD+AI conventions for the downstream models trained in Phase 3.

## 7. Decision Framework

The gates translate statistics into an adoption decision:

| Gate | Pass criterion (pre-registered) | If passed | If failed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** (Phase 1) | VLM malignancy sensitivity non-inferior to expert panel (lower 95% CI of paired difference > −5 pp); parse/refusal-adjusted coverage ≥ 95% | Proceed to reader study | Demote to hybrid pre-labeler; re-scope task (e.g., attribute labels only) |
| **B** (Phase 2) | VLM–expert mean pairwise AC1 ≥ lower bound of expert–expert band; no FST stratum gap > 5 pp in sensitivity; prompt-flip rate < 5% | Proceed to pipeline trial | Restrict to low-ambiguity subsets where the VLM does meet the band |
| **C** (Phase 3) | Downstream classifier trained on VLM labels non-inferior (−3 pp balanced accuracy) on locked expert-verified test set | Adopt VLM as primary labeler with QC sampling, abstention routing, and quarterly re-audit | Hybrid becomes the standing pipeline: VLM pre-labels, experts verify 100% |

The framework's deliberate asymmetry is worth stating plainly: **a single failed gate does not produce a negative paper and a discarded model** — it produces a precise statement of where the VLM can and cannot be trusted, which is exactly what an annotation program needs. Current evidence suggests the most probable near-term outcome is Gate C conditional adoption for attribute labels and pre-labeling, with diagnostic labels for malignant categories remaining expert-anchored: models already show strong descriptive fluency over dermoscopic features (color, shape, texture, distribution) ([arXiv biomedical-imaging evaluation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.05256)) even where their top-1 diagnostic calls lag specialists.

## 8. Limitations of This Plan

Three honest caveats. First, the plan is anchored to today's VLMs; the field's year-over-year movement (GPT-4V's 54% image-only accuracy in late 2023 to GPT-5's 96.6% top-3 on HAM10000 ([Cureus/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12686177/), [Diagnostics](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/23/3052))) means gates should be re-run at each major model release rather than treated as settled. Second, histopathology itself is not an unassailable reference standard — dermatopathologist inter-rater reliability for melanocytic lesions is only moderate (Fleiss' κ ≈ 0.45–0.50) ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32338293/)) — so reference-standard discordance should be quantified and reported, not assumed away. Third, public datasets underrepresent darker skin and rare morphologies even after adding DDI ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343783/)), so the fairness arm's power for Fitzpatrick V–VI will remain limited unless a dedicated collection effort supplements it. None of these caveats blocks the experiment; all of them belong in its protocol.

**Bottom line.** The experiment proposed here is designed to make the replacement question answerable with evidence rather than enthusiasm: four phases, three quantitative gates, and a default-to-hybrid rule. On current evidence, a modern VLM should not be handed autonomous labeling authority for skin-lesion images — but it has likely already earned a supervised seat at the annotation bench.
