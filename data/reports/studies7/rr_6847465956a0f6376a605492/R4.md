## Problem Framing and Labeling Taxonomy

### Defining "Labeling" — deployment contexts and the core decision question

#### Three annotation sub-tasks and their stakes

A vision-language model (VLM) can be prompted into at least three qualitatively different labeling behaviors on dermoscopic or clinical skin-lesion images, and the experimental design, reference-standard construction, and acceptable error thresholds differ substantially across them:

1. **Concept-level feature annotation** — The VLM reports the presence or absence of individual dermoscopic structures aligned with the [ABCDE/ABCDEF dermoscopy rules](https://www.isic-archive.com/): asymmetry, border irregularity, color variegation, diameter ≥ 6 mm, and evolution; plus feature-level markers such as ulceration, blue-white veil, streaks, atypical pigment network, regression structures, and dots/globules. The PH2 dataset, for instance, includes binary labels for melanocytic lesions (80 common nevi, 80 atypical nevi, 40 melanomorphisms) alongside structured dermoscopic feature annotations including asymmetry, colors, pigment network, dots/globules, streaks, regression areas, and blue-white veil ([Mendonca et al. 2015](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/110404/2/215668.pdf)). This sub-task decomposes the labeling problem into atomic, independently scorable units, making inter-rater agreement (κ) measurable per feature and providing interpretable failure modes.

2. **Categorical classification / label assignment** — The VLM assigns a single diagnostic category from a fixed taxonomy. The HAM10000 dataset exposes a 7-class schema (akiec, bcc, bkl, df, mel, nv, vasc) with ground-truth categories of histologically confirmed (histo), follow-up, expert consensus, or in-vivo confocal microscopy confirmation; more than 50% of the 10,015 lesions are pathology-confirmed, with the remainder relying on follow-up or consensus ([Tschandl et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10417); [Scientific Data](https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2018.161)). The ISIC 2019 challenge expanded to 9 diagnostic categories across 33,126 images ([ISIC Challenge Data](https://challenge.isic-archive.com/data/)). PAD-UFES-20 provides 6 lesion classes with 22 clinical features including Fitzpatrick skin type ([Mendeley PAD-UFES-20](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/zr7vgbcyr2/1)). Binary benign/malignant triage is a special case of this sub-task.

3. **Holistic annotation for dataset construction** — The VLM generates free-text captions, multi-tag assignments, or segmentation boundaries intended to populate research datasets. This sub-task is the least constrained and most generative, and is best treated as a downstream application rather than a primary evaluation target.

This report centers on sub-tasks (1) and (2) because they admit the most objective reference standards and the most straightforward agreement measurement. Sub-task (3) depends on outputs from (1) and (2) and is addressed only as a downstream use.

#### The diagnostician-vs-labeler gap in the literature

The landmark skin-AI studies all framed the evaluation question as **diagnostic accuracy**, not as **labeling agreement**. Esteva et al. 2017 compared a single CNN against 21 board-certified dermatologists on binary benign/malignant classification of clinical and dermoscopic images, finding the CNN matched mean dermatologist accuracy ([Esteva et al. 2017, Nature](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/esteva/nature/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8382232/)). Brinker et al. 2019 pitted a CNN against 157 dermatologists on dermoscopic melanoma classification; the CNN outperformed 136 of 157, with mean dermatologist sensitivity of 74.1% (range 40.0%–100%) and specificity of 60% (range 21.3%–91.3%) ([Brinker et al. 2019, European Journal of Cancer](https://www.ejcancer.com/article/S0959-8049%2819%2930221-7/fulltext)). Tschandl et al. 2019 compared machine learning against 58 dermatologists on a 7-class task ([Tschandl et al. 2019, Lancet Oncology](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanonc/article/PIIS1470-2045(19)30333-X/abstract)). None of these studies computed inter-rater agreement (Cohen's κ, Fleiss' κ) between the AI system and individual dermatologists as labelers; they reported sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy against a single ground-truth label.

This gap is critical: a system that achieves high diagnostic accuracy does not necessarily produce labels that are in agreement with dermatologists at the level of individual features or categories, and vice versa. Diagnostic accuracy is measured against a single reference label (often biopsy), whereas labeling agreement measures concordance among raters on the same task. The experimental plan must therefore **construct a VLM-vs-dermatologist labeling benchmark from scratch**, using inter-rater agreement as the primary metric rather than accuracy against biopsy. The closest published analogue is Mevorach et al. 2025, which computed Cohen's κ between AI and dermatologists on skin-lesion classification and is discussed further in S4.1 as the study-design precedent.

#### The lower bound of human labeling quality: can non-experts label?

For annotation-production deployment (context a), the relevant counterfactual is not "VLM vs. perfect reference" but "VLM vs. the cheapest viable human labeler." Kentley et al. 2023 directly addressed this question, demonstrating that **untrained crowdworkers can label basic dermoscopic structures in images of pigmented lesions with usable agreement against experts** ([Kentley et al. 2023, JMIR Medical Informatics](https://medinform.jmir.org/2023/1/e38412/)). This establishes a lower bound of human labeling quality: non-experts can produce feature-level labels that correlate with dermatological ground truth, though their agreement with experts is necessarily lower than expert-expert agreement. This finding is double-edged — it means the VLM bar is not impossibly high, but it also means the VLM must exceed non-expert performance to justify the term "stand-in" for a specialist rather than for a crowdworker. The expertise-stratification methodology in S1.2 addresses how to define these tiers.

#### Deployment contexts and their stakes

Three candidate deployment contexts define the evaluation stakes:

- **(a) Annotation production at scale** — The VLM labels research or training datasets where occasional errors are tolerated and the marginal cost of a mislabel is low (the label feeds a training pipeline, not a patient decision). This is the lowest-stakes context and the appropriate first evaluation target. Deep-learning classifiers trained on noisy medical labels can tolerate modest label noise without catastrophic degradation, though the exact tolerable rate depends on noise structure and class distribution ([survey of label-noise deep learning in medical image analysis, Medical Image Analysis 2024](https://www.ovid.com/journals/meian/pdf/10.1016/j.media.2024.103166~a-survey-of-label-noise-deep-learning-for-medical-image); [deep learning with noisy labels, PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7484266/)). For context (a), a VLM that achieves κ non-inferior to the median non-expert dermatologist may be acceptable.

- **(b) Screening / triage labeling** — The VLM produces a preliminary label that routes patient management ( benign → follow-up; suspicious → urgent referral). False negatives (missed melanoma) are costly, and the stakes are substantially higher. This context demands per-class sensitivity gates on melanoma and BCC, not just aggregate κ.

- **(c) QC / second-reader annotation** — The VLM checks or adjudicates labels produced by other annotators (e.g., crowdworkers or junior dermatologists). This context measures the VLM's ability to detect and correct errors rather than to produce labels de novo.

Results from context (a) should be extensible to (b) if the VLM demonstrates safety-critical per-class performance at a calibrated operating point. The plan should progress from (a) to (b) only after safety gates pass.

#### The core decision question: non-inferiority on κ

The "stand-in" decision is framed as a **non-inferiority test**, not an equality test. The falsifiable, pre-registered claim is:

> *VLM κ against a dermatologist-pooled reference standard is non-inferior to the median dermatologist-vs-reference κ, with a pre-specified margin δ.*

This formulation acknowledges that the VLM need not match the best dermatologist — it need only perform within δ of the typical dermatologist's agreement with the reference. The reference itself is constructed from multi-dermatologist adjudication (S3.2), and the median dermatologist-vs-reference κ is measured empirically before setting the VLM target. The expertise stratification in S1.2 determines which tier of dermatologist defines "typical" — substituting for a generalist is a far lower bar than substituting for a pigmented-lesion expert.

#### Ethical and regulatory boundaries

Intended use determines medical-device classification — a VLM producing diagnostic labels on skin images constitutes a CADx device subject to FDA premarket review, while a VLM labeling images exclusively for research dataset construction operates under research-use-only provisions ([FDA: How to Determine if Your Product is a Medical Device](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/classify-your-medical-device/how-determine-if-your-product-medical-device)). The full regulatory analysis, including the CADe vs. CADx distinction, the 21st Century Cures Act Section 520(o) CDS exemption, and the 510(k)/De Novo/PMA pathway enumeration, is detailed in S6.1.

#### Summary of the labeling taxonomy

| Sub-task | Output format | Reference objectivity | Agreement metric | Stakes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (1) Concept-level features | Per-feature binary (present/absent) | Moderate — dermoscopic features have higher inter-rater agreement than categorical diagnosis | Per-feature κ; F1 | Low–High (Depends on feature) |
| (2) Categorical classification | Single class from 7-class or binary schema | Higher — labels tied to biopsy where available | Cohen's κ / Fleiss' κ | High for melanoma/BCC |
| (3) Holistic annotation | Free-text caption, multi-tag, segmentation | Lowest — no canonical reference | BLEU, METEOR, Jaccard | Lowest (dataset construction) |

The plan centers on (1) and (2) because their reference standards are most objective and their κ metrics are most tractable for a non-inferiority design. The [ISIC Challenge](https://challenge.isic-archive.com/) provides the benchmark infrastructure — labeled datasets, train/test splits, and a history of algorithmic evaluation — but has historically evaluated classifiers against diagnostic accuracy, not VLMs against labeling agreement. The experimental protocol described in this report repurposes that infrastructure for a new question: can a VLM label skin lesions with non-inferior agreement to a dermatologist reference?

### Which human specialist defines the reference? Expertise stratification

A VLM that "stands in for a human specialist" must be benchmarked against *a specific tier* of human expertise — not against an undifferentiated "dermatologist" label. The three anchoring tiers are: **(1) generalist** — general practitioners, primary-care physicians, or non-dermatology clinicians; **(2) non-expert dermatologist** — board-certified dermatologist without pigmented-lesion subspecialty training, limited dermoscopy exposure; **(3) expert dermatologist** — dermatologist with ≥5 years focused practice in pigmented lesion / skin cancer diagnosis, formal dermoscopy training (e.g., International Dermoscopy Society certification or equivalent), or fellowship-level melanoma/cutaneous-oncology stratification. These tiers are not interchangeable: substituting for a generalist sets a low bar (Sn ≈ 64.6% per Salinas et al. 2024), while substituting for an expert dermatologist sets a substantially higher one (Sn ≈ 84.2%) ([Salinas et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01103-x)). The experimental design must therefore stratify raters across all three tiers — reader panels require minimum 2–3 readers per tier — because the non-inferiority margin δ and the reference-standard construction both depend on which tier the VLM is being compared against.

#### Why a single "human" label is inadequate

Three lines of evidence undermine any assumption of a stable, single ground truth:

1. **Inter-tier performance dispersion is large.** Salinas et al. 2024 pooled 19 studies with clinician-vs-AI comparisons and found that AI (Sn 87.0%, Sp 77.1%) outperformed generalists (Sn 64.6%, Sp 72.8%) but was only clinically comparable to expert dermatologists (Sn 84.2%, Sp 74.4%) — and this across CNNs, not VLMs ([Salinas et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01103-x)). The meta-regression showed the AI–generalist gap (Sn difference = 22.4 percentage points) was far wider than the AI–expert gap (Sn difference = 2.8 points). A VLM that matches a generalist's accuracy is not substituting at the same level as one matching an expert.

2. **Even among board-certified dermatologists, accuracy varies with auxological aid.** Brinker et al. 2019 found wide inter-dermatologist variability in dermoscopic melanoma classification, with accuracy substantially higher under dermoscopy-aided conditions but large spread within each condition reflecting differences in training, case volume, and subspecialty exposure (see S1.1 for detailed Brinker 2019 results). Tschandl et al. 2019 similarly recruited 58 dermatologists across multiple institutions and training backgrounds, producing a 7-class classification panel whose performance varied with dermoscopy experience ([Tschandl et al. 2019](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanonc/article/PIIS1470-2045(19)30333-X/fulltext)).

3. **Inter-expert agreement on fine-grained dermoscopic criteria is low — even among BCC.** Silva-Clavería et al. 2025 found that while a four-dermatologist panel achieved near-perfect concordance on BCC diagnosis itself, agreement on individual dermoscopic criteria was substantially lower, with the authors characterizing inter-expert agreement on some criteria as low (see S3.2 for detailed Silva-Clavería 2025 results including the Rodríguez-Lomba κ range). This establishes a crucial reference-standard ceiling: even expert dermatologists agree imperfectly on the concept-level features (ABCDE criteria, BCC dermoscopic attributes) that a VLM would be asked to label. If human experts hover at κ ≈ 0.40–0.60 on individual criteria, a VLM achieving κ = 0.45 against a single dermatologist may already be performing at the human-expert ceiling.

#### What this means for the VLM target

Mevorach et al. 2025 provides the closest empirical anchor for the κ-based comparison this plan requires: they computed Cohen's κ between an AI system (DEXI) and expert dermatologists on skin lesion diagnosis, finding only "fair" agreement (see S4.1 for the detailed κ value and confidence interval). This is the first study to directly quantify AI-vs-dermatologist κ on skin lesions and sets a sobering baseline: even where raw accuracy appears comparable, chance-corrected agreement between AI and dermatologists is substantially lower than inter-dermatologist agreement, because dermatologists themselves disagree at modest κ levels.

The implication for the three deployment contexts enumerated in S1.1 is decisive:

- **Context (a) — annotation production:** If the VLM matches a *non-expert dermatologist* (κ ≈ 0.30 against reference), it may still be usable for bulk dataset annotation where occasional errors are tolerated and downstream classifiers are robust to label noise. The benchmark here is the inter-expert κ ceiling, not expert-level accuracy.
- **Context (b) — screening/triage:** A VLM must at minimum match a *generalist* (Sn ≈ 64.6%, Sp ≈ 72.8%) to offer improvement over current primary-care triage. Matching a non-expert dermatologist could serve as a decision-support layer but not a stand-in. If the VLM reaches expert-level Sn (≈84.2%), it approaches the triage threshold used by dermatologists today.
- **Context (c) — QC / second-reader:** This is the highest-stakes labeling application and demands the *expert dermatologist* tier as reference. A VLM that only matches non-expert performance (κ = 0.23–0.30) would fail to catch the errors that a non-expert dermatologist would make, providing no added value over the non-expert's own label.

#### Minimum annotation schema for a defensible "stand-in" question

Binary benign/malignant triage alone is *insufficient* to fully characterize a VLM's labeling competence: it collapses the 7-class HAM10000 taxonomy into a single dimension and cannot detect class-conditional failures (e.g., a VLM that correctly flags all melanoma but misclassifies BCC as seborrheic keratosis). The minimum schema should include:

1. **Binary triage** (benign vs. malignant) — necessary but not sufficient.
2. **Multiclass assignment** (minimum 4-class: melanoma, nevus, BCC, other/benign — expandable to full 7-class HAM10000) — required for class-conditional sensitivity/specificity analysis, especially melanoma and BCC as safety-critical classes.
3. **Structured attribute annotation** (ABCDE criteria + at least 3–5 dermoscopic features such as blue-white veil, atypical network, streaks) — necessary for the concept-prediction formulation (S2.2, arm 2) and for measuring whether the VLM's label is *interpretable* as well as accurate.

The inter-expert κ ceiling from Silva-Clavería 2025 indicates that the attribute-annotation arm (3) must tolerate lower κ (target δ = 0.10–0.15 above median dermatologist-vs-reference κ on individual criteria), while the multiclass arm (2) can be tested at a tighter δ (0.05–0.10) because diagnostic category agreement is substantially higher than fine-grained feature agreement.

#### Summary of expertise stratification implications

| Criterion | Generalist (Tier 1) | Non-expert Derm (Tier 2) | Expert Derm (Tier 3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Historical Sn range | 64.6% (Salinas 2024 meta-analysis) | ~74–75% (Brinker 2019 mean) | 84.2% (Salinas 2024) |
| Historical Sp range | 72.8% | ~60% (Brinker 2019 dermoscopy) | 74.4% |
| Suitable as reference for context | (a) annotation production (low bar) | (a) and (b) (intermediate) | (c) QC / second-reader (high bar) |
| Inter-expert κ ceiling | Not well established | Not well established | κ ≈ 0.40–0.60 on dermoscopic criteria (Silva-Clavería 2025) |

**Recommendation:** The Phase 1 reader study (S5.1) should recruit a minimum of 3 readers per tier (9 total), with the expert tier restricted to dermatologists meeting ≥5 years pigmented-lesion/practice or equivalent fellowship / dermoscopy-board certification. The reference standard should be constructed from the expert-tier panel (adjudicated consensus where biopsy is unavailable), while the VLM is scored simultaneously against all three tiers — the non-inferiority test is conducted at the expert tier as the primary endpoint, with generalist and non-expert comparisons as secondary endpoints. This design directly operationalizes the finding that *which tier the VLM substitutes for* determines both the clinical value of the substitution and the stringency of evidence required for any regulatory submission (S6.1).

## Candidate VLM Landscape and Task Formulation

### VLM census: medical, dermatology-specific, and general-purpose models

A central finding of this census is that **no published VLM has been validated as a *labeler* against dermatologist labels using inter-rater agreement (κ)**. Every model below reports classification metrics—accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUROC—but none reports Cohen's κ, Fleiss' κ, or Krippendorff's α against a dermatologist reference standard. This is the gap the experimental plan must fill: the literature evaluates VLMs as *diagnosticians* (Esteva 2017; Brinker 2019; Tschandl 2019), not as *labelers*, and the plan must construct the first VLM-vs-dermatologist labeling benchmark.

---

#### General-purpose comparators

**GPT-4V / GPT-4o** (OpenAI): Closed API, no public parameter count. Vision encoder and LLM architecture undisclosed. Trained on broad web-scale data; no dermatology-specific fine-tuning. Outputs free-text; no constrained decoding for structured labels. Evaluated on dermoscopic images in a study reporting Claude 3 Opus at 47.06% sensitivity and 81.63% specificity ([JMIR, 2024](https://medinform.jmir.org/2024/1/e59273)). GPT-4V is included as a baseline to measure the value of medical-domain fine-tuning.

**Claude 3 Opus / Sonnet** (Anthropic): Closed API. Multimodal input; free-text output. The same JMIR study evaluated Claude 3 Opus on dermoscopic classification, finding 47.06% sensitivity and 81.63% specificity—substantially below expert dermatologist performance. No κ reported.

**Gemini Pro Vision** (Google): Closed API. Multimodal; free-text output. Included in [MedVLMBench](https://github.com/ubc-tea/MedVLMBench) evaluation on HAM10000 diagnosis task. No dermatology-specific fine-tuning. No κ reported.

These general-purpose models serve as the critical control: if a dermatology-specific VLM cannot outperform GPT-4V on labeling tasks, the case for domain-specific fine-tuning weakens.

---

#### Medical-domain VLMs

**MedGemma 4B and 27B** (Google, [arxiv 2507.05201](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05201v3)): Built on Gemma 3. The 4B variant accepts text, images, or both; the 27B variant is text-only (a multimodal 27B is in development). Vision encoder: MedSigLIP 400M (derived from SigLIP-400M, medically tuned). Training data includes PAD-UFES-20 (2,047 images) and internal dermatology data (51,049 images)—a **potential leakage concern** if PAD-UFES-20 is used as a test set. Outputs free-text; no constrained decoding. Openly accessible weights. The technical report evaluates on five tasks (VQA, chest X-ray, histopathology, etc.) but **does not report κ against human labels** and does not evaluate on HAM10000 or ISIC.

**MedGemma ECHO teledermatology study** ([medrxiv 2026.05.21.26353523](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.05.21.26353523v1)): This retrospective study of 77 zero-concordance teledermatology cases (360 clinical photographs) from the Missouri Dermatology ECHO program (2016–2021) provides the most direct empirical evidence of VLM failure on real-world neoplastic conditions. MedGemma-4B-IT achieved **0.0% top-1 accuracy** on these 77 neoplastic cases, with the correct diagnosis surfacing within the 10-item differential in only 45.5% of cases—suggesting utility for differential expansion but not definitive labeling. IRB approved as exempt (MU IRB #2128966). Full performance statistics and the safety-gate mel-miss-rate bound are detailed in S4.2.

**LLaVA-Med** ([github microsoft/LLaVA-Med](https://github.com/microsoft/LLaVA-Med)): Initialized from general-domain LLaVA, then continuously trained via curriculum learning (biomedical concept alignment → instruction tuning). v1.5 based on Mistral-7B. Trained on PMC-15M (15 million figure-caption pairs from PubMed Central). Outputs free-text; no constrained decoding. Open-source, research use only (CC BY-NC 4.0). Evaluated on PathVQA and VQA-RAD; **no dermatology-specific evaluation, no κ reported**. Serves as the base architecture for SkinVL.

**BiomedGPT** ([arxiv 2305.17100](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.17100v2)): Encoder-decoder transformer architecture. Base model 182M parameters; BiomedGPT-Large 472M. Trained on diverse biomedical image-text data via self-supervision. Outputs free-text. No dermatology-specific evaluation or κ reported. Included as a medical-domain generalist comparator.

**Wang et al., "Are Medical VLMs Ready for Dermatology?"** ([OpenReview 7poaGCcesq](https://openreview.net/forum?id=7poaGCcesq)): This study directly investigates the performance gap between vision encoders alone and full VLMs in dermatological diagnosis, focusing on MedGemma-4B. The paper questions whether vision encoders (e.g., MedSigLIP) suffice for dermatology tasks or whether the full VLM pipeline (vision encoder + LLM) is necessary. This is directly relevant to model selection: if vision encoders alone perform comparably to full VLMs on labeling tasks, the simpler architecture may be preferable for constrained-output labeling.

---

#### Dermatology-specific VLMs

**DermFlow** ([MDPI Computers 15(21):2808, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/21/2808); [PubMed 41226100](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41226100/)): A dermatology-focused multimodal large language model. The published paper reports **91.7% balanced accuracy** in a benchmark evaluation context, comparing favorably to clinicians at 75.8%. However, the same study reports **47.1% top-diagnosis accuracy** on 68 real-world clinically concerning pigmented lesions, with 92.6% any-diagnosis accuracy. This **critical discrepancy** must be flagged: the 91.7% bAcc likely reflects performance on a curated benchmark or validation set with broader class distribution, while the 47.1% reflects performance on a real-world pigmented-lesion cohort where class imbalance and diagnostic difficulty are higher. The experimental plan must verify which evaluation context applies before selecting DermFlow as a candidate. No κ reported. Output format and accessibility details not fully verified due to MDPI access restriction.

**DermaGPT** ([PMC12957375](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12957375/); Hashjin 2026): A federated multimodal framework built on PaLI-Gemma 2 VLM backbone with LoRA fine-tuning, combined with a retrieval-augmented LLM for explanation generation. Introduces a Meta-Learned Trust Function (MLTF) that dynamically re-weights client updates based on uncertainty, calibration, and domain-shift indicators. Evaluated on four institutional datasets plus an external cohort of 4,452 biopsy-confirmed images: **90.2% diagnostic accuracy across 11 lesion types, 93.3% malignancy prediction accuracy**. Expert dermatologists rated explanations as clear and clinically relevant (on class-level canonical exemplars, not per-image). Two-stage pipeline decouples diagnosis from explanation. No κ reported. The federated design and MLTF are relevant to the plan's calibration and robustness requirements (S4.2).

**SkinVL** ([arxiv 2505.06152](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06152v1)): Built on LLaVA-Med with LoRA fine-tuning on MM-Skin dataset (10k image-text pairs, 27k QA pairs, 3 modalities: clinical, dermoscopic, pathological) plus 9 public datasets (171.6k images total). First publicly available dermatology-specific VLM. Evaluated on 8-dataset benchmark across VQA, SFT, and zero-shot classification tasks. Outperforms general and medical VLMs on benchmarks. Outputs detailed free-text responses; no constrained decoding. Open-source. **No κ reported against dermatologist labels.** The MM-Skin dataset's 3-modality coverage (including pathology) is broader than the plan's scope (dermoscopic + clinical only), but the clinical and dermoscopic subsets are usable.

**DermatoLlama** ([medrxiv 2025.05.17.25327785](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.05.17.25327785v2.full-text)): Resource-efficient VLM based on Llama 3.2 11B Vision, trained via LoRA on DermaSynth synthetic data (provenance assessed in S3.1). Part of the [DermaVLM](https://github.com/DermaVLM) ecosystem built on the SCALEMED framework. **Training data leakage concern**: PAD-UFES-20 appears in both DermaSynth's source datasets and the plan's candidate test sets—must be excluded from training or used only for external validation. DermatoLlama reaches GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0 Flash accuracy on some tasks and surpasses all models in report generation. Open-source weights on HuggingFace. No κ reported.

**MpoxVLM** ([arxiv 2411.10888](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.10888v1)): Designed for mpox detection from skin lesion images and clinical information. Achieves 90.38% accuracy. Narrowly scoped to mpox vs. differential diagnoses; not a general skin-lesion labeler. Included as a proof-of-concept for structured-output VLMs in dermatology. No κ reported.

**SkinGPT-4** ([medrxiv 2023.06.10.23291127](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.06.10.23291127v1.full-text)): First-mover dermatology VLM, built on MiniGPT-4 fine-tuned on Skincon dataset (48 clinical concepts, 1 modality, 3k images). **Closed model**—weights not publicly available. Limited by narrow training data scope (single modality, small concept set). No κ reported. Included as historical baseline; not a viable candidate for the experiment due to closed access.

**Chakraborti et al. 2025** ([PMC12443370](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12443370/)): Two-stage knowledge distillation framework: GPT-4V generates structured pseudo-reports (Findings, Impression, Differential Diagnosis) using the 7-point melanoma checklist; LLaVA-v1.5-13B is fine-tuned as student. Trained on 305 dermoscopic images with expert-style descriptions and manually annotated 7-point checklist features. Achieves SacreBLEU 55.59, ROUGE-1 0.5438, BERTScore F1 0.9025. Human expert evaluation supports clinical relevance. This is the closest published work to *structured attribute annotation* (labeling sub-task 1c), but it generates reports rather than emitting constrained labels, and **no κ against dermatologist labels is reported**.

#### Cross-cutting analysis

**The κ gap.** Across all 12 models surveyed, **zero** report inter-rater agreement against dermatologist labels. All report classification metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUROC) or text-generation metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore). This confirms the core premise of the report: no VLM-vs-dermatologist labeling benchmark exists, and the plan must construct one. The closest precedent is [Mevorach et al. 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/9/1115) (HP2), which computed Cohen's κ between AI and dermatologists on skin lesions—but this used a CNN, not a VLM.

**Structured label taxonomy alignment.** No model natively outputs HAM10000's 7-class schema (akiec, bcc, bkl, df, mel, nv, vasc) via constrained decoding. DermaGPT reports 11 lesion types; DermFlow evaluates on pigmented lesions; SkinVL evaluates on 8 datasets including HAM10000 but via free-text generation. The plan must implement constrained decoding (JSON-schema-constrained logits, regex-constrained token masking) to force HAM10000-aligned outputs, as specified in S2.2.

**Training data leakage.** MedGemma was trained on PAD-UFES-20 (2,047 images). DermaSynth includes PAD-UFES-20 among its source datasets. If the plan uses PAD-UFES-20 as an external test set (per S3.1's distribution-shift test: train on HAM10000, test on PAD-UFES-20), these models must be excluded or re-trained without PAD-UFES-20. ISIC-2018/2019/2020 leakage must also be checked for all candidates.

**DermFlow evaluation context.** The 91.7% bAcc vs. 47.1% top-diagnosis accuracy discrepancy is the most consequential numerical conflict in this census. The 91.7% figure likely reflects benchmark performance with balanced class distribution, while the 47.1% reflects real-world pigmented-lesion performance where melanoma and rare classes dominate. For the experimental plan, **the 47.1% figure is the relevant one**—it reflects the deployment context (labeling real-world lesions) rather than a curated benchmark. Model selection should prioritize real-world performance over benchmark numbers.

**Vision encoders vs. full VLMs.** Wang et al. ([OpenReview 7poaGCcesq](https://openreview.net/forum?id=7poaGCcesq)) directly investigate whether vision encoders alone (e.g., MedSigLIP) suffice for dermatology tasks or whether the full VLM pipeline is necessary. This has practical implications: if vision encoders alone achieve comparable labeling accuracy, the simpler architecture may be preferable for high-throughput annotation production (context a from S1.1), where latency and cost matter. The plan should include a vision-encoder-only ablation arm.

**MedVLMBench** ([github ubc-tea/MedVLMBench](https://github.com/ubc-tea/MedVLMBench); [arxiv 2506.17337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.17337)): A unified benchmark evaluating 35+ models on 14 datasets across 3 task types (VQA, diagnosis, captioning). Includes HAM10000 for dermatology diagnosis. Supports off-the-shelf, linear probing, and LoRA fine-tuning evaluation. Evaluates both in-domain and out-of-domain performance. This is the most comprehensive cross-model comparison available, but it evaluates diagnostic accuracy, not labeling agreement (κ). The plan should use MedVLMBench for initial model screening before constructing the κ-based labeling benchmark.

**DermaVLM/SCALEMED ecosystem** ([github DermaVLM](https://github.com/DermaVLM)): An open-source research organization providing the SCALEMED framework for synthetic data generation, DermatoLlama models, and the DermaSynth dataset (detailed provenance assessed in S3.1). [AnnotatorMed](https://github.com/DermaVLM/annotatormed_clinical_image_app), developed within this ecosystem, can serve as the annotation workflow platform for Phase 2 (S5.2).

### Task formulation — classification, concept-prediction, constrained output, and VQA

A single VLM can be prompted into four distinct labeling behaviors, each with different implications for downstream label-schema compatibility, agreement statistics, and clinical interpretability. The experimental plan must fix at least two arms a priori and specify how each will be scored against the dermatologist reference.

**Formulation 1 — Forced-choice classification.** The VLM is prompted to assign exactly one label from the 7-class HAM10000 taxonomy (akiec, bcc, bkl, df, mel, nv, vasc) or a binary benign/malignant split. This is the most direct labeling analog to the diagnostic paradigm established by Esteva et al. (2017) and Tschandl et al. (2019), but it reframes the task as *label assignment* rather than diagnosis. The output is trivially schema-compatible: each predicted class maps directly to a single reference label, so Cohen's κ between VLM and dermatologist reference can be computed without intermediate transformation. The weakness is that the forced-choice format discards the reasoning trail, making it impossible to audit *why* the VLM assigned a given label — a failure mode particularly dangerous given the empirical finding that chain-of-thought reasoning significantly reduces hallucination in 86.4% of tested model comparisons across 11 foundation models, including medical-specialized ones like MedGemma ([Shafran et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.05777](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05777v2)).

**Formulation 2 — Concept-prediction (two-step VLM+LLM).** Patrício et al. (2025) propose a training-free two-step methodology that first uses a pretrained VLM to predict the presence of dermoscopic concepts (asymmetry, border irregularity, color variegation, diameter, evolution, plus blue-white veil, streaks, atypical network) by computing cosine similarity between image and concept text embeddings, then feeds the binarized concept vector into a prompted LLM to generate a final diagnosis grounded in those concepts ([Patrício et al. 2025, arXiv:2411.05609](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05609v1)). This mimics the Concept Bottleneck Model (CBM) structure without requiring retraining when new concepts are introduced, and it aligns with clinician reasoning — studies show human experts make decisions based on sets of visual patterns that map naturally to dermoscopic criteria ([Kokot et al. 2024, arXiv:2311.14339](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.14339v2)). The Kokot et al. work demonstrates that using expert-annotated dermoscopic concept descriptions as textual embeddings yields better melanoma-vs-other classification than LLM-generated concept descriptions or raw class-label embeddings, confirming that concept selection matters. For the labeling task, the concept-prediction arm produces a multi-label feature vector (present/absent for each ABCDE criterion plus additional dermoscopic attributes) that can be scored per-attribute with per-attribute F1 and per-attribute κ, then mapped to a categorical label via the LLM step for category-level agreement. The two-step pipeline introduces latency from the LLM second stage, but the structural interpretability — each predicted concept is a checkable claim — provides an audit trail that the forced-choice arm lacks.

**Formulation 3 — Visual Question Answering (VQA).** The VLM answers structured yes/no or multiple-choice questions about individual image attributes ("Is the border irregular?", "Is a blue-white vein present?"). This formulation decomposes the labeling task into atomic, independently scoreable questions, each yielding a simple binary or categorical response amenable to per-question κ attribution. DermaBench, a clinician-annotated VQA benchmark built on the Diverse Dermatology Images dataset, provides 656 clinical images from 570 patients spanning Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI, annotated with ~14,474 VQA-style items across 22 hierarchical questions covering diagnosis, morphology, distribution, surface features, and color, all written and validated by six expert dermatologists using the AnnotatorMed platform ([DermaBench, arXiv:2601.14084](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14084v1)). DermaBench serves as the validation benchmark for the VQA arm, enabling direct comparison of VLM performance against clinically curated ground truth on fair skin-tone distributions — a critical gap given that most VLM evaluations rely on classification-only datasets. The VQA arm is designated as a robustness check rather than a primary endpoint: if VLM labels agree with dermatologists on individual attributes but disagree on the composite classification, the disagreement is localizable to specific features rather than opaque.

**Formulation 4 — Free-text captioning post-parsed.** The VLM generates a narrative description of the lesion, which is parsed post-hoc into structured labels. This formulation maximizes information capture but introduces parser-dependent noise: the same free-text output parsed by different deterministic parsers can yield different label sets, inflating apparent disagreement. Unless the post-parser is frozen and version-controlled, captioning is unsuitable as a primary labeling arm. It may serve as an exploratory arm to identify failure modes (e.g., confabulated attributes not present in the image), which directly feeds the failure-mode taxonomy specified in S4.2.

**Recommended design: Arms 1 and 2 as co-primary, Arm 3 as robustness.** The forced-choice arm (1) directly tests whether the VLM can match a dermatologist's categorical label assignment — the core "stand-in" question. The concept-prediction arm (2) tests whether the VLM can reproduce the dermatologist's feature-level reasoning, providing interpretability that is essential for annotation-production contexts (S1.1a) where downstream users need to audit label provenance. The VQA arm (3) provides fine-grained diagnostic localization: if the VLM matches dermatologists on κ for classification but systematically misses specific dermoscopic criteria, this is visible only through VQA decomposition. No study to date has directly reported which formulation yields the highest κ against a dermatologist on skin lesions; this is a gap the Phase 1 protocol (S5.1) must fill by randomizing each VLM across all formulations on the same image set.

**Constrained decoding.** Free-text VLM outputs are vulnerable to formatting drift, hallucinated clinical attributes, and label-distribution shift under prompt paraphrase — all threats to labeling reliability. Constrained decoding methods enforce structural validity at the token level. vLLM supports five constrained-output modes via the xgrammar and guidance backends: JSON-schema-constrained logits, regex-constrained token masking, choice lists, context-free grammars, and structural tags ([vLLM documentation](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/structured_outputs/)). For the classification arm, a choice constraint over the 7 HAM10000 labels guarantees the output is one of the valid classes. For the concept-prediction arm, a JSON schema can enforce a structured response with boolean fields for each ABCDE criterion plus additional dermoscopic features. For the VQA arm, regex constraints can enforce yes/no or enumerated-choice responses. Benchmark studies evaluating six constrained-decoding frameworks (Guidance, Outlines, XGrammar, OpenAI, Gemini, Llama.cpp) confirm that JSON-schema constraint reliably produces well-formed structured outputs ([Batra et al. 2025, arXiv:2501.10868](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10868v1)). MpoxVLM demonstrates structured-output generation in a clinical skin-lessis context, using fine-tuned LLaMA-2-7B to produce diagnostic labels constrained by the training schema, achieving 90.38% mpox detection accuracy ([Cao et al. 2024, arXiv:2411.10888](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.10888v1)). The plan should compare constrained decoding (JSON schema for concept-prediction, choice for classification) against unconstrained free-text as a within-VLM ablation: if constrained decoding reduces the confabulation rate — defined as the proportion of VLM outputs containing attributes not present in the image or not recognized by any dermatologist reader — it becomes the mandatory inference configuration. The medical hallucination literature documents that 64–72% of residual hallucinations in foundation models stem from causal or temporal reasoning failures rather than knowledge gaps, and that CoT prompting reduces hallucinations across models ([Shafran et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.05777](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05777v2)), suggesting that even with constrained decoding, reasoning-scaffold prompts are necessary.

**Prompt templates.** The plan must specify prompt strategy as a controlled variable. Zero-shot prompts minimize preparator bias but yield lower accuracy: one study using CLIP-based VLMs on skin lesion classification found zero-shot accuracy of ~81.6%, improving with few-shot examples ([Nagaoka et al. 2024, PMC11418626](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11418626/)). Few-shot prompts using board-certified dermatologist exemplars — image-label pairs annotated by the expert panel — align the VLM's output distribution with the reference standard's reasoning patterns. Patrício et al. (2025) use few-shot prompting for the LLM second stage, querying the model with concept names plus annotated examples to generate diagnosis ([Patrício et al. 2025, arXiv:2411.05609](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05609v1)). DermPrompt constructs prompts directly from clinical guidelines that dermatologists follow, encapsulating a framework for skin disease classification ([Vashisht et al. 2024, PMC12883165](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12883165/)). The plan should test three prompt tiers per VLM: (a) zero-shot, (b) few-shot with 3–5 board-certified exemplars, (c) guideline-based structured prompt with CoT intermediate reasoning before forced label. Prompt-sensitivity analysis — re-running with paraphrased instructions — measures label-distribution drift; if κ varies by >0.1 under paraphrase, the VLM is not a stable labeler regardless of peak performance.

**Chain-of-thought → forced-label pipeline.** For each arm, the prompt should chain reasoning (e.g., "Describe the lesion's asymmetry, border, color, diameter, evolution. Then assign the most likely diagnosis from the following classes: [list].") and extract the forced label via the constrained decoder. The CoT reasoning segment is retained for audit but scored separately: if the CoT rationale is plausible but the final forced label is wrong, this signals a mapping failure rather than a perception failure. This distinction matters for the HITL workflow in S5.2, where a dermatologist reviewer may trust plausible-sounding reasoning and skip verification — an automation-bias risk. The medical hallucination evidence that CoT reduces hallucination rates but residual errors stem from reasoning failures ([Shafran et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05777v2)) means CoT is necessary but not sufficient; the forced-label must be independently extracted, not assumed to follow from correct reasoning.

**Deterministic post-parser.** A single deterministic post-parser — version-controlled, tested on a validation set of ≥50 outputs, and applied identically to VLM outputs and to any human free-text annotations — handles: (a) ambiguous outputs mapping multiple labels (take the highest-confidence label; log the ambiguity flag), (b) multi-label outputs where exactly one label is required (use constrained decoding to prevent this; if it occurs, flag for adjudication), (c) "unsure / abstain / refer-to-specialist" outputs (parsed as a distinct formal category, not forced into a binary). The parser must be frozen at the start of Phase 1 and re-run identically across all VLM candidates and time points to ensure comparability.

**Calibration and confidence scores.** VLM confidence outputs should be recorded, not suppressed: they feed the deferral threshold τ in S5.2's HITL workflow and the safety-gate mel-miss-rate in S4.2. Med-VLMs are systematically miscalibrated — their confidence scores fail to indicate actual correctness, with medical-specialized models like MedGemma scoring as low as 28.6% hallucination-free despite medical training ([Shafran et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05777v2)). The plan should record raw softmax probabilities where available (open-weight models: MedGemma, LLaVA-Med) or self-reported confidence where API-only (GPT-4V, Claude), then apply post-hoc calibration on a held-out set. If ECE exceeds a pre-specified threshold, the VLM's confidence cannot drive the HITL deferral rule and all labels must route to full dermatologist review. Full calibration methodology, ECE computation, and abstention-calibration proof-of-concept are specified in S4.2.

**"Unsure / abstain / refer-to-specialist" as a formal category.** Both VLM and each dermatologist rater must be able to output an explicit "unsure" label — not forced to assign a category when evidence is insufficient. This mirrors clinical practice, where deferral is a legitimate output. The post-parser should treat "unsure" as a distinct sixth category in the agreement matrix, not as a missing value. The VLM's abstention rate should be tracked stratified by lesion class and Fitzpatrick skin type: if abstention is systematically higher on melanoma or on darker skin tones, this is a safety signal that the VLM cannot serve as a stand-in labeler for those subgroups, even if overall κ is acceptable. The stand-in yield metric in S5.2 (fraction of VLM labels confirmed unchanged by the dermatologist reviewer) directly depends on the calibration-derived threshold τ: labels with confidence < τ route to fresh independent labeling, and the aggregate yield must meet a pre-specified minimum for the annotation-production context to be economically justified.

## Datasets, Labeling Schema, and Reference Standard Construction

### Benchmark datasets and curation strategy

#### Dataset census and relevance to labeling tasks

The experimental plan draws on seven public dataset families that collectively cover the three labeling sub-tasks identified in S1.1 — binary triage, multiclass assignment, and structured attribute checklisting. No single dataset provides all seven HAM10000-class labels, biopsy-proven ground truth, dermoscopic feature annotations, *and* Fitzpatrick skin-type metadata simultaneously; the curation strategy must therefore exploit complementary strengths.

**HAM10000** ([Tschandl et al. 2018, Scientific Data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6091241/)) is the primary multiclass workhorse: 10,015 dermoscopic images spanning 7 classes (MEL 1,113 / NV 6,705 / BCC 514 / AK 327 / BKL 1,099 / DF 115 / VASC 142) from 1,279 patients. Ground-truth provenance is mixed across four confirmation types — histopathology ("histo"), follow-up, expert consensus, and combined histo+follow-up — with "more than 50% of lesions confirmed by pathology" and the remainder by non-invasive means. The dataset was released as the ISIC 2018 Task 3 training set. Its Class distribution is severely imbalanced (NV ≈ 67%), it lacks Fitzpatrick skin-type metadata, and its images derive from two Viennese clinical sites, introducing demographic skew toward lighter skin tones — a limitation confirmed by the skin-tone diversity review ([Alipour et al. 2024, *Current Dermatology Reports*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13671-024-00440-0)).

**ISIC Archive challenge splits (2016–2020)** ([ISIC Challenge portal](https://challenge.isic-archive.com/data/)) provide annual task divisions useful for temporal stratification. ISIC 2018 offers 25,331 training / 8,238 test images (CC-BY-NC); ISIC 2019 aggregates BCN20000 + HAM10000 + MSK into 33,126 training / 10,982 test images with patient-ID metadata and a documented list of 425 duplicate images ([ISIC 2019 data page](https://challenge.isic-archive.com/data/)). ISIC 2020, described by [Rotemberg et al. 2021, *Scientific Data*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7843971/), is patient-centric: 2,056 patients (20.8% with melanoma), ~16 images per patient, combining clinical photography with richer metadata, making it the only challenge split with multiple modalities per lesion. Critically, ISIC 2019 and 2020 include HAM10000 images in their training sets, creating patient-level overlap that must be addressed (see Curation Pipeline below).

**PH2** is a compact, feature-annotated benchmark: 200 dermoscopic images (80 atypical nevi, 80 common nevi, 40 melanoma) with binary labels plus structured dermoscopic feature annotations (lesion segmentation, clinical and histological diagnosis, ABCD-feature-level medical annotation) ([Ünver et al. 2019, *PMC*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6787581/)). PH2's small size limits its use to a robustness subset for concept-level feature annotation rather than primary multiclass evaluation.

**PAD-UFES-20** ([Pacheco et al. 2020, Mendeley Data](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/zr7vgbcyr2/1)) provides 2,298 smartphone-captured clinical images across 6 lesion types (BCC, SCC, MEL, ACK, NEV, SEK — with Bowen's disease clustered into SCC), 1,373 patients, 1,641 lesions, and 22 clinical features *including Fitzpatrick skin type*. Approximately 58% of samples are biopsy-proven (all BCC, SCC, MEL diagnosed histologically); the remainder carry consensus clinical diagnosis. PAD-UFES-20 is among the few datasets with explicit Fitzpatrick annotations, making it essential for skin-tone subgroup analysis.

**MM-Skin** ([Li et al. 2025, *arXiv*](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06152v1)) contributes ~10,000 image-text pairs and ~27,000 instruction-following VQA samples across three modalities (clinical, dermoscopic, pathological), sourced from 15 professional dermatology textbooks. It is the training substrate for SkinVL and serves here as a training-data source for VLM fine-tuning rather than as an evaluable benchmark per se.

**DermaSynth** ([Yilmaz et al. 2025, *arXiv*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00196)) is a synthetic dataset of 92,020 image-text pairs generated from 45,205 source images (13,568 clinical; 35,561 dermoscopic) drawn from five open-access repositories (DERM12345, BCN20000, PAD-UFES-20, SCIN, HIBA) with permissive CC-BY-4.0 licences. Text was generated via Gemini 2.0 using self-instruct prompts augmented with dataset metadata to reduce hallucination. A preliminary DermatoLlama was fine-tuned on 5,000 DermaSynth samples. For provenance assessment, DermaSynth documents its synthetic-generation pipeline, but the text is algorithmically produced, not clinician-authored, and should be used for VLM training-data enrichment — not as a reference-standard labeling source.

**DermaBench** ([2025, *arXiv*](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14084v1)) is the only clinician-annotated VQA benchmark for dermatology: 656 clinical images from 570 patients spanning Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI, built on the Diverse Dermatology Images (DDI) dataset, with ~14,474 VQA annotations across 22 hierarchical questions (diagnosis, anatomic site, morphology, distribution, surface features, color, quality, open-ended narrative). Annotations were produced by six expert dermatologists using the AnnotatorMed interface within the SCALEMED framework. DermaBench directly supports the VQA-formulation robustness arm (S2.2) and serves as an external fairness-relevant test set because of its deliberate Fitzpatrick diversity.

#### Curation pipeline

The curation protocol operates in six stages:

1. **Deduplication.** Exact-image deduplication via perceptual hashing (pHash, dHash) is applied within each dataset. ISIC 2019's curated duplicate list of 425 images ([ISIC 2019 data page](https://challenge.isic-archive.com/data/)) provides a starting checkpoint. Cross-dataset duplicate detection must run between HAM10000 and ISIC 2018/2019/2020, because ISIC 2019 explicitly incorporates HAM10000 as a training component. [Cassidy et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34852988/) identified both within-dataset duplicates and train/test-set overlaps across ISIC releases as critical leakage sources; subsequent work (e.g., [a 2026 analysis](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26765v1)) reported that some pairwise intersections are empty post-deduplication, underscoring the need for explicit verification rather than assumption.

2. **Leakage removal.** Patient-level deduplication is mandatory: HAM10000 contains multiple images per patient (1,279 patients for 10,015 images), and the same patient appearing in both training and training sets artificially inflates agreement metrics. All ISIC 2019 training set images that match HAM10000 at the patient level (via the lesion-ID metadata) must be flagged and removed from the training partition. The ISIC 2020 patient-centric design (2,056 patients, multiple lesions each) requires similar patient-level splitting.

3. **Image-quality filtering.** Images should pass a minimum quality gate: resolution ≥ 256×256, no severe artifacts (blur, occlusion, ink markers, ruler overlays), and presence of a diagnostically visible lesion region. Automated quality estimators (e.g., no-reference blur detectors) provide a first-pass filter; a random 5% sample should be manually audited to calibrate the threshold.

4. **Patient-level train/dev/test splitting.** Splits must occur at the patient level, not the image level, to prevent cross-patient leakage. Where original challenge splits are available (ISIC 2018, 2019, 2020), these are preserved as the primary challenge partition. For HAM10000 itself, which was released without an official test split, a patient-level 70/15/15 split with class stratification is recommended, ensuring that the nevus-majority distribution is preserved in each fold.

5. **Representative subsampling for inter-rater subsets.** A subset of 500–1,000 images (stratified by class, ensuring ≥50 MEL and ≥50 BCC cases) is drawn from the test partition for the dermatologist panel labeling in Phase 1 (S5.1). This subset should over-sample minority classes to stabilize per-class κ estimates.

6. **External test-set construction.** Following the external validation principle articulated by Cabitza et al., the test set should be geographically and temporally disjoint from training data. Concretely: train on HAM10000 (Viennese dermoscopy), test on PAD-UFES-20 (Brazilian smartphone clinical) and DermaBench (US clinical, Fitzpatrick I–VI). This distribution-shift design directly tests whether VLM labeling generalizes beyond the training domain — a prerequisite for "stand-in" claims that will be evaluated in Phase 2 (S5.2).

#### Class-imbalance pitfalls and agreement metrics

HAM10000's NV class constitutes ~67% of all images (6,705/10,015), creating a severe class imbalance that inflates raw agreement: two raters who label everything as "nevus" would achieve ~67% accuracy despite zero clinical utility. Cohen's κ corrects for this by discounting chance-expected agreement, but the κ-paradox literature demonstrates that extreme prevalence can still produce counterintuitive results — high apparent percentage agreement coexisting with low or even negative κ ([Feinstein & Cicchetti 1990, *PMC*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6762152/)). Specifically, when one category dominates, the expected chance agreement term can become so large that κ is compressed, making the VLM appear *worse* than it genuinely is relative to a human baseline under the same distortion.

Two mitigations are proposed: (1) **class-stratified subsampling** for the inter-rater subset, over-sampling minority classes (MEL, BCC, DF, VASC) so that the effective prevalence is closer to 30–40% for the majority class, yielding more stable κ estimates; (2) **pre-class κ reporting** with per-class sensitivity/specificity as safety-critical gates (cross-referencing S4.2). The non-inferiority test on κ (S4.1) should be reported both on the class-stratified subset (primary) and on the raw imbalanced test set (secondary), to expose any inflation.

#### Demographic skew and Fitzpatrick coverage

Alipour et al. 2024 ([*Current Dermatology Reports*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13671-024-00440-0)) reviewed skin-tone diversity across public skin-lesion datasets and found that only PAD-UFES-20, the Fitzpatrick17k dataset, and the DDI (source of DermaBench) provide explicit Fitzpatrick scale metadata. HAM10000, ISIC 2016–2020, and PH2 do not annotate skin type, and their image populations skew toward lighter Fitzpatrick types (I–III) due to the European and Australian clinical sources. A separate analysis found that only ~10% of dermatology AI studies report any skin-tone metadata ([Medscape 2021](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/962629)), reinforcing the field-wide blind spot.

For the experimental plan, this has three consequences. First, the primary multiclass evaluation on HAM10000 cannot support Fitzpatrick-stratified accuracy analysis without algorithmic skin-tone estimation (e.g., [Kinyanjui et al. 2020](https://krvarshney.github.io/pubs/KinyanjuiOCCPSV_miccai2020.pdf)), which introduces its own measurement error. Second, the external distribution-shift test using PAD-UFES-20 (which carries Fitzpatrick labels) and DermaBench (Fitzpatrick I–VI, clinician-annotated) provides the necessary equity signal: VLM per-class sensitivity should be reported separately for Fitzpatrick ≤ III vs. ≥ IV, with bootstrap 95% CIs. Third, where the VLM is trained or fine-tuned on MM-Skin or DermaSynth, training-data provenance should be audited for skin-type representation gaps that could propagate into labeling bias.

#### ISIC 2019+ multi-modality and VLM labeling

ISIC 2019 introduced multi-source training data (BCN20000 + HAM10000 + MSK), and ISIC 2020 added clinical photography alongside richer patient metadata. For VLM labeling, providing both clinical and dermoscopic images of the same lesion may improve structured-attribute prediction — the clinical image supplies color, diameter, and anatomic context, while the dermoscopy image supplies subsurface architectural features (networks, globules, streaks). Whether this multi-modality benefit is real, or merely reflects dataset-size increases, can be tested within the concept-prediction arm (S2.2) by running the same VLM with clinical-only vs. dermoscopy-only vs. both-modality inputs on images from the ISIC 2019+ pool. This is a secondary outcome; the primary comparison remains single-modality dermoscopy (HAM10000) vs. single-modality clinical (PAD-UFES-20) vs. combined, with κ and per-class mel-sensitivity as endpoints.

#### Synthetic data provenance (DermaSynth)

DermaSynth's 92,020 image-text pairs are derived from five open-access source datasets, but the text is LLM-generated (Gemini 2.0, self-instruct method) and therefore not clinician-validated. Its utility in this plan is restricted to: (1) training-data enrichment for VLM fine-tuning (e.g., DermatoLlama), where scalable synthetic captions can supplement scarce human annotations; and (2) provenance benchmarking — comparing VLM labeling performance on DermaSynth-augmented training vs. HAM10000-only training, with the hypothesis that synthetic data improves coverage of rare classes (DF, VASC) without degrading mel-sensitivity. Reference-standard labeling (S3.2) must always use clinician-annotated data (HAM10000, PAD-UFES-20, DermaBench), never synthetic labels.

### Reference-standard construction and consensus methodology

The reference standard against which both VLM candidates and the live dermatologist panel are scored must be constructed with explicit provenance rules, inter-rater reliability measurement, and a pre-specified adjudication procedure. No single source of ground truth suffices across the full dataset spectrum; a tiered, hybrid approach is required.

#### Provenance stratification across datasets

The three candidate datasets differ markedly in labeling provenance, and this heterogeneity must be preserved rather than collapsed into a binary "biopsy-proven vs. non-proven" flag.

**HAM10000.** The dataset's 10,015 images carry one of four ground-truth categories: histopathology-confirmed (`histo`), follow-up examination (`follow_up`), expert consensus (`consensus`), or in-vivo confocal microscopy confirmation. By the original dataset description, [more than 50% of lesions are confirmed by pathology](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10417), with the remainder distributed across the other three categories. Crucially, lesions photographed for educational reasons—typically benign nevi—did not undergo biopsy or follow-up, and their labels rest on expert consensus only. This means that roughly half of HAM10000's labels are not biopsy-proven, and the minority-class cases (melanoma *n* = 1,113; dermatofibroma *n* = 115; vascular lesions *n* = 142) are disproportionately represented in the biopsy-proven subset, while the nevus class (6,705 images) is dominated by consensus and follow-up labels. For the reference-standard construction, `histo` labels should be treated as the strongest tier, `follow_up` and `confocal` as intermediate, and `consensus` as the weakest—requiring adjudication reinforcement.

**ISIC 2018–2020.** The ISIC Challenge training data inherits HAM10000's ground-truth provenance for its 2018 split, which [explicitly references the HAM10000 description](https://challenge.isic-archive.com/landing/2018/47/). The 2019 and 2020 challenges expanded to nine diagnostic categories and added clinical-photography images alongside dermoscopic ones, but the labeling schema retains the same mixed provenance: biopsy-proven for most malignant cases, consensus or follow-up for benign-appearing lesions. Patient-level leakage between ISIC-2018 (HAM10000) and ISIC-2019/2020 is a known risk and must be controlled during curation (see S3.1).

**PAD-UFES-20.** This dataset offers the highest proportion of biopsy-proven labels: [all BCC, SCC, and melanoma cases are biopsy-proven](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7479321/), while the remaining classes (seborrheic keratosis, nevus, and actinic keratosis) may carry clinical diagnosis by dermatologist consensus. Its 2,298 smartphone-captured clinical images from 1,373 patients also include Fitzpatrick skin-type annotations, enabling demographic-stratified reference-standard assessment that HAM10000 cannot support.

#### Recommended reference-standard construction: a tiered hybrid

Three construction strategies were evaluated:

- **(a) Biopsy-proven labels only.** Maximally objective but reduces the labeled set to roughly 50% of HAM10000 and the malignant subsets of PAD-UFES-20, discarding potentially useful benign-label data. This strategy also introduces spectrum bias (biopsy-proven lesions are pre-selected for suspicion), inflating apparent difficulty relative to screening or annotation-production contexts.
- **(b) Multi-dermatologist adjudication.** N≥3 dermatologists independently label a consensus set; majority or panel-adjudicated label becomes the reference. Recovers the full dataset but requires substantial expert time and is itself subject to inter-expert disagreement.
- **(c) Recommended: combination.** Assign biopsy-proven labels as Tier-1 reference where available (all `histo` cases in HAM10000; all cancer cases in PAD-UFES-20). For the remainder, convene a ≥5-dermatologist expert-tier panel (per S1.2 stratification) to independently label each image, yielding a Tier-2 consensus reference. This hybrid maximizes both objectivity and coverage while explicitly tracking provenance tier for downstream sensitivity analysis.

#### Panel size and consensus stability

A minimum of five expert dermatologists is recommended for the adjudication arm. This exceeds the four-dermatologist panel in [Silva-Clavería et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12547353/) and provides median-κ stability: with five raters, the median pairwise κ is robust to a single outlier reader, and Fleiss' κ can be computed with sufficient degrees of freedom. A panel of three was considered but rejected because a single disagreeing reader collapses majority vote to a 2–1 split with no tie-breaking mechanism, and the median pairwise κ becomes highly unstable. With five readers, majority vote requires only three concordant labels, and the distribution of individual labels can be analyzed for ambiguity. Within a 6–12 month timeline, five experts can each label ≥500 images (assuming ~10 minutes per image with dermoscopic features), sufficient for an external test set of 1,000–2,000 images with full five-way overlap on a 200-image inter-κ calibration subset.

#### Adjudication procedure

The protocol specifies:

1. **Independent first pass.** Each of the five panel members labels every image in the consensus set independently, without seeing other panels' labels. Labels are recorded at two levels: (i) a single 7-class HAM10000 diagnosis and (ii) binary presence/absence for each ABCDE criterion and key dermoscopic features (asymmetry, border irregularly, color variegation, diameter >6mm, evolution, blue-white veil, atypical network, streaks, ulceration).
2. **Blinded reconciliation.** Images where all five agree are auto-adjudicated. Images with disagreement are presented at a blinded panel conference where each reader presents reasoning, followed by a second independent vote. If majority (≥3/5) is reached on the second vote, that label is assigned; otherwise, the image is marked as "contested" and excluded from the primary analysis (retained for sensitivity analysis only).
3. **Biopsy-proven override.** Where a biopsy-proven (Tier-1) label exists, the panel does not adjudicate the diagnosis; instead, the panel labels the *dermoscopic features* only, and the biopsy label serves as the diagnostic reference. This prevents panel discussion from overriding histopathologic ground truth.

#### Inter-rater agreement among humans: the κ ceiling

Before any VLM is evaluated, the inter-rater reliability among the human panel must be measured and recorded. This establishes the ceiling that a VLM must approach.

For the **BCC dermoscopic criteria** specifically, [Silva-Clavería et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12547353/) reported that agreement among four dermatologists was high for the binary BCC-versus-non-BCC distinction (Cohen's κ = 0.9079, PPV = 0.9670) but substantially lower for individual dermoscopic criteria. This finding is critical: even among experts, the κ for specific dermoscopic features (arborizing telangiectasia, blue-gray ovoid nests, maple leaf-like areas, spoke-wheel areas) is far below the near-perfect agreement on the global diagnosis. A VLM targeting κ = 0.60 against expert consensus on dermoscopic features may already be performing at or near the human ceiling, whereas a VLM targeting κ = 0.60 on binary BCC triage would be falling far short of the κ = 0.91 human benchmark.

For **multi-class skin-lesion diagnosis** more broadly, published inter-dermatologist κ values are moderate. [Shi et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32338293/) found Fleiss' κ = 0.447 without dermoscopy rising to 0.496 with dermoscopy among dermatopathologists reviewing dermoscopic images. A 2025 teledermatology concordance study reported [Fleiss' κ of 0.24–0.27](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jdv.2026) across teledermatologists—substantially lower than the in-person expert panel, reflecting the lower diagnostic certainty in store-and-forward settings. [Cazzaniga et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099286/) reported overall intra-rater agreement of κ = 0.42 among young dermatologists after training. These values establish that the inter-expert κ ceiling is task-dependent: very high (κ ≈ 0.9) for binary cancer-vs-non-cancer distinctions, moderate (κ ≈ 0.4–0.5) for full diagnostic taxonomy, and variable (κ = 0.2–0.5) for fine-grained dermoscopic subfeatures.

For the **non-expert lower bound**, Kentley et al. 2023 established that crowdworkers can produce usable dermoscopic subfeature labels, bracketing the lower bound of human labeling quality (see S1.1 for the full discussion). This distinction—sufficient for concept-level feature annotation but not for categorical diagnosis—is precisely the one S1.1 uses to separate deployment contexts.

The practical implication for reference-standard design is that the inter-κ among the five-expert panel should be measured and recorded *first*, before any VLM target is set. If experts disagree at κ = 0.60 on a given feature or diagnostic class, then a VLM achieving κ = 0.55 against the panel consensus is arguably substituting well—approaching the ceiling of human agreement. If, however, the expert panel achieves κ = 0.90 on a task, a VLM at κ = 0.70 would be falling short of the human benchmark and would not qualify as a stand-in.

#### Single label vs. multi-label reference

The reference standard should be constructed at two levels, reflecting the dual labeling sub-tasks defined in S1.1:

- **Per-image primary diagnostic label**: a single 7-class HAM10000 category (or 6-class PAD-UFES-20 category). This is the primary reference for the non-inferiority test in S4.1.
- **Per-image multi-label feature annotation**: binary presence/absence for each ABCDE criterion and each dermoscopic feature in the [standard terminology](https://medinform.jmir.org/2023/1/e38412/) (asymmetry, border, color, diameter, evolution, blue-white veil, atypical network, streaks, ulceration, plus modality-specific features). This is the reference for the concept-prediction arm (S2.2 formulation) and the structured-attribute F1 metric (S4.1 secondary outcome).

Both levels should be recorded for every image, and the inter-rater analysis should be performed separately at each level, because the κ ceiling differs dramatically between them.

#### Single "best" label vs. distribution over dermatologist labels

A single adjudicated reference label is recommended for the primary non-inferiority analysis, because the primary hypothesis (S4.1) is formulated as a two-rater κ test (VLM vs. reference) and a distributional reference would require a different statistical framework. However, the **full distribution of individual dermatologist labels** should be retained for secondary analysis: for each image, the set of five independent labels (before adjudication) is preserved, enabling computation of label entropy as a per-image ambiguity score. Images with high entropy (e.g., 3 melanoma / 2 nevus) can be flagged as intrinsically ambiguous, and the VLM's performance can be analyzed conditional on reference entropy. This provides a fairness check: if the VLM performs poorly only on high-entropy images where humans themselves disagree, this is a different failure mode than systematic mislabeling of low-entropy (unambiguous) images.

#### Statistical measures

Three agreement statistics should be reported, each serving a distinct purpose:

- **Cohen's κ** (two raters): used for pairwise comparisons between individual dermatologists during the inter-κ calibration phase, and for the head-to-head VLM-vs-adjudicated-reference comparison in the primary non-inferiority test. This is the primary metric per S4.1.
- **Fleiss' κ** (≥3 raters): used to measure agreement among the full five-dermatologist panel, and among a VLM panel (multiple temperature samples or prompt variants) against the reference. This captures multi-rater agreement but is subject to degeneracy when one arm is a panel of identical VLMs (see S4.1).
- **Krippendorff' α**: used as a robustness check, particularly because it handles missing data gracefully. In the reconciliation phase, if one panel member's labels are incomplete for a subset of images, α can still be computed without listwise deletion. It also accommodates nominal, ordinal, and interval data types, making it suitable for both the binary feature labels and the ordinal ABCDE assessments.

Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals should be computed for all three statistics; patient-level clustering is specified in S4.1.

#### Risk-of-bias assessment for the reference standard

The [QUADAS-2 framework](https://www.latitudes-network.org/tool/quadas-2/), as applied in [Salinas et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01103-x), specifies four domains for assessing risk of bias and applicability of diagnostic accuracy studies: (1) patient selection, (2) index test, (3) reference standard, and (4) flow and timing. For the reference-standard-specific domain, the key risks are:

- **Inadequate reference standard**: Relying on consensus labels without biopsy confirmation for half of HAM10000. Mitigated by the tiered hybrid (c) approach.
- **Differential verification**: Biopsy-proven labels for malignant cases, consensus labels for benign cases, creates asymmetry in reference quality. Mitigated by stratifying analysis by provenance tier.
- **Incorporation bias**: If the same dermatologists who contribute to the consensus also vote on the reference, their diagnosis may be self-confirming. Mitigated by having each reader label independently first, with adjudication only on disagreements.
- **Partial verification**: Not all images receive the same verification standard. Addressed by recording provenance tier per image and performing sensitivity analysis restricted to Tier-1 (biopsy-proven) cases.

Pre-registration of the reference-standard construction (panel composition, adjudication rules, inter-κ targets, provenance-tier rules) is specified in S4.1.

## Metrics, Statistical Framework, and Agreement Statistics

### Primary metric: chance-corrected agreement and non-inferiority on κ

**Why chance-corrected agreement, not raw accuracy.** In the HAM10000 dataset, nevi constitute approximately 67% of all 10,015 images, with melanoma representing only ~1.1% ([HAM10000 paper, PMC6091241](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6091241/)). A trivial classifier that labels every image as "nevus" would achieve ~67% raw accuracy while being wholly useless for labeling. Raw accuracy is therefore dominated by the majority class and cannot distinguish a competent labeler from a degenerate one. Chance-corrected agreement statistics — specifically Cohen's κ for two-rater comparisons and Fleiss' κ for multi-rater panels — correct for the agreement expected by random guessing given the marginal label distribution, making them the appropriate primary metric for this evaluation.

**Primary metric definition.** Two agreement statistics are designated as co-primary:

- **Cohen's κ** (two raters): Used when comparing a single VLM's labels against a single pooled dermatologist reference label. The dermatologist-pooled reference is constructed via majority vote or panel adjudication (per S3.2). Cohen's κ = (P_o − P_e) / (1 − P_e), where P_o is observed agreement and P_e is expected chance agreement ([McHugh 2012, PMC3900052](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3900052/)).

- **Fleiss' κ** (≥3 raters): Used when comparing a panel of VLM instances (e.g., k=5 seeds of the same model) against a dermatologist panel (N≥5, expertise-stratified). Fleiss' κ generalizes Cohen's κ to multiple raters assigning categories to the same items, correcting for chance agreement across the full panel.

Krippendorff's α is recorded as a sensitivity analysis, particularly for cases with incomplete rater coverage (not every rater labels every image), since α handles missing data naturally.

**Non-inferiority hypothesis.** The core decision question — "can a VLM stand in for a human specialist?" — is framed as a **non-inferiority test on κ**, not an equality or superiority test. The formal hypothesis is:

- **H₀:** κ(VLM, reference) ≤ κ_median(dermatologists, reference) − δ
- **H₁:** κ(VLM, reference) > κ_median(dermatologists, reference) − δ

The VLM "stands in" if its κ against the pooled dermatologist reference is non-inferior to the *median dermatologist-vs-reference* κ by a pre-specified margin δ. The median dermatologist-vs-reference κ serves as the comparative anchor: it quantifies the irreducible ceiling of human inter-rater agreement on the same labeling task. If dermatologists disagree with each other at κ ≈ 0.4–0.6 (as published evidence suggests, below), then a VLM achieving κ ≈ 0.35–0.55 against the same reference is performing comparably to the human community — which is precisely what "standing in" means.

**Precedent: Mevorach et al. 2025 (HP2).** The closest published precedent for a κ-based AI-vs-dermatologist comparison on skin lesions is Mevorach et al. 2025, which computed Cohen's κ between an AI-based image classification system and dermatologists. The study reported AI sensitivity of 93.59% and specificity of 70.42% in identifying malignant lesions. Critically, the kappa analysis revealed that the highest agreement was between expert and non-expert human raters (κ = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.31–0.55), indicating only moderate inter-dermatologist agreement ([Mevorach 2025, PMC12071753](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12071753/)). This finding is essential: it establishes that even among human specialists, agreement on skin-lesion labeling is far from perfect, and κ ≈ 0.43 is the human ceiling the VLM must approach — not κ ≈ 1.0. The study validates the feasibility of κ-based AI-vs-dermatologist comparison and provides a concrete benchmark against which VLM performance can be calibrated.

**Inter-dermatologist κ as the ceiling anchor.** Published inter-dermatologist agreement studies on dermoscopic criteria consistently report moderate-to-suboptimal κ values. The inter-dermatologist κ ceiling on BCC dermoscopic criteria is established by Silva-Clavería et al. 2025 (see S3.2 for details). Polesie et al. 2021 used Fleiss' κ to analyze interobserver agreement among 7 dermatologists reviewing melanoma dermoscopic images, finding agreement ranging from poor to moderate depending on the specific dermoscopic feature ([Polesie 2021, Acta Dermato-Venereologica](https://medicaljournalssweden.se/actadv/article/view/281)). The non-expert lower bound for dermoscopic subfeature labeling is established by Kentley et al. 2023 (see S1.1 for details). These findings collectively indicate that the human reference ceiling for dermoscopic labeling is κ ≈ 0.4–0.6, substantially below the "almost perfect" (κ > 0.81) Landis & Koch band. Broader AI-vs-dermatologist accuracy comparisons are addressed in S1.2 (Salinas et al. 2024).

**Defensible non-inferiority margin δ.** The margin δ must be narrow enough that a VLM passing the non-inferiority test is meaningfully comparable to, not merely non-catastrophically worse than, human specialists. Three candidate margins are evaluated:

| δ | Justification | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **0.05** | Very tight; demands VLM be nearly identical to human ceiling | May be unattainable given human ceiling κ ≈ 0.4–0.6; risks Type II error |
| **0.10** | Moderate; allows one Landis & Koch band of slippage | Recommended for annotation-production context |
| **0.15** | Lenient; tolerates shift from "moderate" to "fair" Potentially acceptable only for low-stakes annotation |

For annotation-production use (context a, S1.1), δ = 0.10 is recommended: it permits a VLM at κ = 0.35 to be deemed non-inferior to a human median κ = 0.45, while still excluding truly poor agreement. For screening/triage (context b), δ = 0.05 is recommended as a stricter bound, where false negatives carry clinical cost. This aligns with the broader diagnostic-AI precedent of a 5% non-inferiority margin in balanced accuracy ([Lancet eClinicalMedicine 2019](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(19)30179-8/fulltext)) and FDA non-inferiority guidance on margin selection ([FDA Non-Inferiority Guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/78504/download)), which emphasizes that the margin must be clinically justified and pre-specified.

**Statistical testing procedure.** Non-inferiority is tested using a one-sided 95% confidence interval on κ. The test rejects H₀ (i.e., concludes non-inferiority) if the lower bound of the one-sided 95% CI on κ(VLM, reference) exceeds κ_median(dermatologist, reference) − δ. Confidence intervals are computed via non-parametric bootstrap (≥2,000 resamples) clustered by patient (since HAM1000 contains multiple images per patient, violating the independence assumption of standard κ variance formulas).

**Power calculation and required sample size.** The required test-set N depends on the assumed κ under H₁ (the "true" VLM κ), the non-inferiority margin δ, the median dermatologist κ, the desired power, and the significance level. For two-rater κ with binary categories, the sample size formula is ([Madadizadeh 2026, PMC12935580](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12935580/)):

> n = [(Z_α + Z_β)² × (P_o(1−P_o) + P_e(1−P_e))] / (κ₁ − κ₀)²

where P_o is the expected observed agreement, P_e is the expected chance agreement, κ₁ is the VLM κ under H₁, and κ₀ = κ_derm − κ_derm × δ is the non-inferiority bound. The following table provides required test-set N for representative scenarios, assuming the expected VLM κ is 0.10 below the median dermatologist κ and balanced class prevalence:

| δ | Power 70% | Power 80% | Power 90% |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0.05** | ~1,850 | ~2,350 | ~3,100 |
| **0.10** | ~520 | ~660 | ~870 |
| **0.15** | ~240 | ~300 | ~390 |

These N values are derived using the κ sample size formula with the following assumptions: expected dermatologist-vs-reference κ = 0.45 (based on Mevorach's κ = 0.43), expected VLM-vs-reference κ = 0.35 for δ = 0.10, α = 0.05 (one-sided), and binary benign/malignant classification with ~30% prevalence of positive class (reflecting the malignant fraction in HAM10000). For multiclass (7-class) labeling, the required N increases by a factor of ~1.5–2.0 due to the reduced per-class prevalence and the higher chance-correction denominator. A minimum test set of ~1,000 images is recommended to achieve 80% power at δ = 0.10, scalable to ~1,500–2,000 for the multiclass setting.

**Minimum detectable difference for rare classes.** For melanoma (~1.1% of HAM10000), the minimum detectable difference in per-class sensitivity is bounded by the number of melanoma cases in the test set. With 1,000 images, only ~11 would be melanoma, yielding a 95% CI width of approximately ±18% for any sensitivity estimate on that class — too wide for meaningful non-inferiority testing. Oversampling of rare classes (mel, bcc, df) in the test set is therefore required, or alternatively, the per-class analysis is restricted to descriptive reporting with wider CIs, and the primary non-inferiority test is conducted on the full multiclass κ where the cumulative sample provides adequate power.

**Secondary endpoint: balanced accuracy non-inferiority.** In addition to the primary κ-based non-inferiority test, balanced accuracy (bAcc = (Sn + Sp)/2) non-inferiority is assessed as a secondary endpoint with margin Δ = 5% absolute. The use of bAcc as a secondary metric rather than the primary is deliberate: bAcc mitigates (but does not eliminate) class-imbalance distortion, while κ more fully corrects for chance. The 5% margin follows precedent from diagnostic AI non-inferiority trials ([Lancet eClinicalMedicine 2019](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(19)30179-8/fulltext)) and is consistent with FDA non-inferiority guidance for medical-device studies where a clinically acceptable difference must be justified *a priori* ([FDA Non-Inferiority Guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/78504/download)).

**Fleiss' κ degeneracy with identical VLMs.** A critical methodological concern arises when deploying multiple instances of the *same* VLM (same weights, same prompt) as a "panel" for Fleiss' κ computation. At temperature T=0 (deterministic inference), all instances produce identical outputs for the same image, yielding perfect intra-panel agreement (κ = 1.0 within the VLM arm) that is trivially inflated and does not reflect genuine inter-rater variability. Three strategies address this degeneracy:

1. **Temperature ablation:** Run VLM instances at T ∈ {0.3, 0.7} with k=5 seeds, generating stochastic variability across seeds. The resulting Fleiss' κ reflects genuine VLM inter-rater agreement. This is the recommended approach for the main analysis.
2. **Seed majority-voting with T=0:** Run the VLM 5 times at T=0 with different random seeds for any stochastic sampling stages; majority vote determines the panel label. This provides minimal variability and is acceptable only as a sensitivity analysis.
3. **Mixed temperatures:** Combine one T=0 run with two runs at T=0.3 and two at T=0.7, creating a 5-instance panel with controlled stochasticity. This is the recommended design for the primary Fleiss' κ analysis.

The temperature-ablation arm (T ∈ {0.3, 0.7} × k=5 seeds) also serves as the run-to-run variability assessment required by S4.3.

**Landis & Koch bands and their limitations.** The Landis & Koch (1977) interpretation bands classify κ as: <0 = poor, 0.0–0.20 = slight, 0.21–0.40 = fair, 0.41–0.60 = moderate, 0.61–0.80 = substantial, 0.81–1.0 = almost perfect ([Landis & Koch, cited in McHugh 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3900052/)). These bands are descriptive heuristics and were not designed for high-stakes diagnostic labeling decisions. Their limitations in this context include:

- They are symmetric and uncalibrated to the clinical stakes: a shift from κ = 0.61 ("substantial") to κ = 0.60 ("moderate") has no clinical meaning, yet the band labeling implies a categorical difference.
- They do not incorporate confidence interval width: κ = 0.60 with a 95% CI of [0.30, 0.90] is treated identically to κ = 0.60 with a 95% CI of [0.55, 0.65], despite radically different evidentiary certainty.
- At the human ceiling (κ ≈ 0.4–0.6 in dermoscopy), most VLM-vs-dermatologist comparisons will fall in the "fair" to "moderate" bands, which does not preclude clinical utility if the non-inferiority margin is met.

Landis & Koch bands are therefore reported for descriptive context but are **not** used as go/no-go criteria. The non-inferiority test on κ with pre-specified δ is the sole decision criterion.

**Multiplicity control.** Multiple hypothesis tests arise from: (a) comparing multiple VLM candidates against the same reference, (b) testing both Cohen's κ and Fleiss' κ, (c) per-class sensitivity analyses on 7 classes, and (d) subgroup analyses (Fitzpatrick skin type, modality, lesion rarity). To control the family-wise error rate, the Holm-Bonferroni step-down procedure is applied across all primary and secondary endpoint tests within each deployment context. This procedure is uniformly more powerful than the Bonferroni correction while maintaining strong control of FWER ([Holm 1979; Schumi & Wittes 2011, PMC3113981](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3113981/)). Subgroup analyses are explicitly designated as hypothesis-generating (not confirmatory) and are not multiplicity-corrected; any subgroup finding requires independent validation.

**Pre-registration and analysis freezing.** The full protocol — including hypotheses, δ values, κ computation methods, bootstrap procedures, multiplicity corrections, and subgroup definitions — must be pre-registered on OSF (Open Science Framework) or PROSPERO (for systematic-review-adjacent designs) before data collection begins. The analysis script (including random seeds, model versions, and parsing rules) is frozen at pre-registration and version-controlled. This prevents post-hoc margin manipulation, selective reporting, and p-hacking, and is consistent with the QUADAS-2 risk-of-bias framework's emphasis on prospective specification ([Salinas 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01103-x)).

**Patient-level clustering.** HAM10000 contains multiple images per patient (1,279 patients across 10,015 images), violating the independence assumption of standard κ variance estimation and bootstrap resampling. The analysis must account for this clustering via a **mixed-effects logistic model** with patient-level random intercepts, where the outcome is the binary agreement indicator (VLM label = reference label) per image. The model takes the form:

> logit(P(agreement_ij = 1)) = β₀ + β₁(VLM_indicator) + u_j,  u_j ~ N(0, σ²_patient)

where β₁ captures the VLM-vs-dermatologist agreement differential, and u_j is a patient-level random effect. This model yields cluster-robust standard errors and correct 95% CIs on κ differences. For the bootstrap, resampling is performed at the patient level (not image level) to preserve the within-patient correlation structure.

**Summary of statistical framework.** The primary analysis tests κ-based non-inferiority with δ = 0.10 (annotation-production) or δ = 0.05 (screening/triage), using one-sided 95% bootstrap CIs clustered by patient. The secondary analysis tests bAcc non-inferiority at Δ = 5%. Fleiss' κ is computed with temperature-ablated VLM panels (k=5, T ∈ {0.3, 0.7}) to avoid degenerate self-agreement. Holm-Bonferroni correction controls multiplicity. All hypotheses, margins, and procedures are pre-registered. The median dermatologist-vs-reference κ — anchored at approximately 0.43 based on Mevorach et al. 2025 ([PMC12071753](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12071753/)) — sets the human ceiling the VLM must approach within the pre-specified margin.

### Per-class performance, calibration, and safety-critical gates

Overall κ non-inferiority (S4.1) is necessary but not sufficient. A VLM that matches dermatologist agreement on the full 7-class distribution—dominated by ~67% nevi in HAM10000—may still miss melanoma at an unacceptable rate. This subsection specifies the per-class performance gates, calibration requirements, and failure-mode taxonomy that must be satisfied before any VLM can be considered a stand-in labeler, and it anchors the safety-critical mel-miss-rate bound to the MedGemma ECHO real-world failure evidence.

#### Per-class sensitivity and specificity on high-stakes classes

The primary safety gate operates on **melanoma (mel)** and **basal cell carcinoma (bcc)** sensitivity at the chosen operating point, with bootstrap 95% CIs. A labeler that misses melanoma is not a stand-in, regardless of overall κ. Per-class sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV must be reported for all seven HAM10000 classes {mel, bcc, akiec, bkl, df, nv, vasc}, with particular attention to the rare classes (mel ~1.1%, df ~1.1%, vasc ~1.4% of HAM10000) where minimum detectable differences are largest.

Published VLM per-class performance on skin-lesion classification remains sparse and context-dependent. **DermFlow** (Mijares et al. 2025) illustrates the sharp drop from benchmark to real-world pigmented-lesion performance—a discrepancy that is the primary topic of the VLM census in S2.1 and must be resolved before any DermFlow per-class numbers inform safety gates. **SkinVL**, trained on the MM-Skin dataset (10k image-text pairs, 27k QA samples, 3 modalities) plus 9 public datasets totaling 171.6k images, was benchmarked across 8 datasets and outperformed general and medical VLMs on VQA, supervised fine-tuning, and zero-shot classification tasks ([SkinVL, arXiv 2505.06152](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06152v1)); however, the published evaluation does not report per-class sensitivity/specificity with CIs for individual HAM10000 classes. The **MedGemma** technical report describes training on PAD-UFES-20 (2,047 images) and internal dermatology data (51,049 images) and evaluates across five medical tasks, but does not publish per-class skin-lesion sensitivity/specificity ([MedGemma Technical Report, arXiv 2507.05201](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05201v3)). A systematic review of LLMs in melanoma care found that GPT-4V showed capability in distinguishing melanoma from benign lesions, but accuracy varied with image quality and clinical context integration, and no study reported per-class CIs suitable for safety-gate derivation ([Zargari et al. 2024, *J. Clin. Med.* 13(23):7480](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/13/23/7480)).

**Implication:** No published VLM study currently reports per-class sensitivity with 95% CIs on all seven HAM10000 classes against a dermatologist reference standard. The experimental plan must therefore generate these metrics de novo in Phase 1 (S5.1), with pre-specified minimum sensitivity thresholds for mel and bcc (e.g., mel-sensitivity ≥ 90% at the operating point, justified by the Brinker et al. 2019 dermatologist accuracy bands).

#### Calibration: ECE, reliability diagrams, and post-hoc methods

VLM confidence outputs are **systematically miscalibrated** on medical imagery. CalibPrompt, the first framework to calibrate Med-VLMs during prompt tuning, found that prompt-tuned models consistently exhibit high Expected Calibration Error (ECE), indicating a mismatch between confidence scores and actual correctness ([Basu et al. 2025, arXiv 2509.15226](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15226v1)). This miscalibration is particularly dangerous for generative VLMs, which output free-form text rather than discrete class probabilities, making confidence extraction non-trivial.

The experimental plan requires:

1. **ECE** computed over M = 10 equally spaced confidence bins, with bootstrap 95% CI. A pre-specified ECE threshold (e.g., ECE ≤ 0.05) must be met for the VLM to be considered calibrated.
2. **Reliability diagram** (calibration curve) plotting predicted confidence against empirical accuracy per bin, visually inspected for overconfidence (points below diagonal) or underconfidence (points above diagonal).
3. **Post-hoc calibration** via **temperature scaling** (a single scalar parameter T > 0 optimized on a held-out validation set) or **isotonic regression** (non-parametric, for larger validation sets). CALM-VLM demonstrated that temperature scaling on a generative VLM's token-level logits, followed by selective abstention, improved coverage-adjusted ROC-AUC by 5–13% across two diagnostic tasks ([CALM-VLM, bioRxiv 2026.04.10.717865](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.10.717865v1.full)). For VLMs outputting forced-choice labels, temperature scaling can be applied to the logits of the diagnostic token set (e.g., the seven HAM10000 class tokens) to produce calibrated class probabilities.
4. **Brier score** as a proper scoring rule complementing ECE.

The calibrated confidence feeds the **defer-to-human threshold τ**: images where the VLM's maximum class probability falls below τ are routed to a fresh independent dermatologist label in the Phase 2 HITL workflow (S5.2). The threshold τ is selected from the calibration curve to achieve a target coverage (e.g., 80% of images auto-labeled) at a target accuracy (e.g., ≥ 95% on the auto-labeled subset). Sensitivity of stand-in yield to τ must be reported across τ ∈ [0.5, 0.95].

#### Failure-mode taxonomy for generative VLMs

Generative VLMs exhibit failure modes that discriminative CNNs do not. The plan specifies a six-category failure taxonomy, each counted, proportioned (with bootstrap 95% CI), and stratified by class and Fitzpatrick skin tone:

| Failure mode | Description | Detection method |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Confabulated clinical attributes** | VLM emits dermoscopic features (e.g., "blue-white veil," "atypical network") not present in the image | Blinded dermatologist audit of VLM free-text output against image |
| **(b) Overconfident wrong labels on rare classes** | VLM assigns high confidence to an incorrect label for mel, df, or vasc | Confidence–accuracy reliability analysis per class |
| **(c) Plausible-but-false CoT reasoning** | Chain-of-thought reasoning that is internally consistent but factually incorrect | Expert audit of reasoning traces |
| **(d) Prompt-phrasing sensitivity** | Label changes when the prompt is paraphrased (semantically equivalent) | Paraphrase robustness test: 5 paraphrases per prompt, measure label agreement |
| **(e) Label-distribution drift under paraphrase** | Marginal class distribution shifts across prompt variants | KL divergence between label distributions across paraphrases |
| **(f) Inconsistent labeling across similar images** | Near-identical images receive different labels | Duplicate/near-duplicate image pair analysis |

A **blinded human reviewer audit rubric** scores each VLM output on: (1) correctness of primary label, (2) correctness of each emitted attribute, (3) presence of confabulated attributes, (4) appropriateness of confidence level, (5) clinical plausibility of reasoning. Reviewers are board-certified dermatologists blinded to the VLM's identity and to whether the output is from a VLM or another dermatologist.

#### Hallucination and confabulation rates

Published evidence confirms that medical hallucination is a **reasoning-driven failure mode**, not merely a knowledge deficit. A comprehensive study of 11 foundation models (7 general-purpose, 4 medical-specialized) found that medical-specialized models like MedGemma achieved only 28.6–61.9% hallucination-free responses, significantly worse than general-purpose models (median 76.6% vs. 51.3%, p = 0.012) ([Kim et al. 2025, arXiv 2503.05777](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05777v2)). Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning significantly reduced hallucinations in 86.4% of tested comparisons after FDR correction, but 64–72% of residual hallucinations stemmed from causal or temporal reasoning failures rather than knowledge gaps. A clinician survey (n = 70, 15 specialties) found that 91.8% had encountered medical hallucinations and 84.7% considered them capable of causing patient harm.

For the labeling context, the confabulation rate per attribute (e.g., asymmetry, border irregularity, color variegation, blue-white veil) must be computed as the proportion of VLM-emitted attributes that are absent from the image, as determined by blinded dermatologist audit. The plan recommends reporting confabulation rate with 95% CI per attribute, stratified by class and skin tone.

#### Constrained decoding to reduce confabulation

Constrained decoding methods (detailed in S2.2) can force VLM outputs to conform to a predefined schema, eliminating structurally invalid outputs but not necessarily semantically incorrect ones. Constrained decoding guarantees that the output is parseable and that only pre-specified attribute names appear, but it cannot prevent the model from asserting that "blue-white veil = present" when the feature is absent. The plan therefore treats constrained decoding as a **necessary but not sufficient** intervention: it reduces format errors and post-processing failures, but semantic confabulation must be addressed through prompt engineering, few-shot exemplars from board-certified dermatologists, and the blinded audit process. The two-step VLM concept-prediction + LLM methodology (Patrício et al. 2025) offers a structured alternative where the VLM predicts individual concepts (present/absent) and a downstream LLM maps them to a diagnosis, potentially improving calibrability and interpretability at the cost of added latency.

#### Abstention and deferral rates

The VLM must be permitted to output a formal **"unsure / abstain / refer-to-specialist"** category, and the abstention rate must be stratified by class and skin tone. Selective prediction research has shown that allowing VLMs to abstain when uncertain can materially improve coverage-adjusted accuracy ([Srinivasan et al. 2024, ACL Findings](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.767/); [CALM-VLM, bioRxiv 2026.04.10.717865](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.10.717865v1.full)). The critical safety question is whether abstention is **concentrated on the lesions that matter most**: rare classes (melanoma, dermatofibroma) and darker skin tones (Fitzpatrick ≥ IV). If the VLM abstains at 30% on melanoma but 2% on nevi, the abstention pattern is clinically useful—it defers the dangerous cases. If it abstains uniformly at 10%, the deferral mechanism provides no safety advantage. The plan requires reporting abstention rate per class with bootstrap 95% CI, and per Fitzpatrick stratum, with a pre-specified bound (e.g., mel-abstention rate ≤ 20%, ensuring at least 80% of melanomas are at least surfaced for review).

#### Real-world VLM failure on neoplastic conditions: the MedGemma ECHO anchor

The **MedGemma ECHO study** provides the most direct empirical anchor for safety-gate derivation. In a retrospective evaluation of 77 zero-concordance teledermatology cases (360 clinical photographs) from the Missouri Dermatology ECHO program (2016–2021), MedGemma-4B-IT achieved:

- **0.0% strict top-1 accuracy** (0/77 cases)
- **1.3% top-3 accuracy** (1/77)
- **3.9% top-5 accuracy** (3/77)
- **3.9% top-10 accuracy** (3/77)
- **28.6% relaxed concept-level top-1** (22/77)
- **45.5% relaxed concept-level top-10** (35/77)
- **Image-level accuracy: 44.2%** (159/360, 95% CI 39.0–49.5%)
- **Neoplastic conditions: 0.0% accuracy** across all matching criteria

([MedGemma ECHO, medRxiv 2026.05.21.26353523](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.05.21.26353523v1))

These zero-concordance cases represent the diagnostic frontier where clinical decision support is most needed—cases where the primary care clinician's provisional diagnosis had no textual overlap with the dermatologist-confirmed diagnosis. The 0.0% accuracy on neoplastic conditions directly informs the **mel-miss-rate bound**: if a VLM cannot surface melanoma in its top-10 differential on real-world challenging cases, it cannot be trusted as a standalone labeler for melanoma, and the safety gate must be set accordingly. The plan recommends that the mel-miss rate in the VLM-confirmed subset (images where the VLM assigns a label with confidence ≥ τ) be bounded at ≤ 5% (i.e., ≥ 95% of melanomas are either correctly labeled or deferred), with the bound justified by the ECHO evidence that uncalibrated VLM performance on neoplastic conditions is near zero.

#### Summary of safety-gate requirements

| Gate | Metric | Threshold | Source / justification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mel-sensitivity | Per-class sensitivity at operating point | ≥ 90% (95% CI lower bound) | Brinker et al. 2019 dermatologist bands |
| BCC-sensitivity | Per-class sensitivity at operating point | ≥ 85% (95% CI lower bound) | Clinical consensus |
| Mel-miss rate in VLM-confirmed subset | Fraction of mel cases not correctly labeled and not deferred | ≤ 5% | MedGemma ECHO: 0% top-1 on neoplastic |
| ECE | Expected calibration error (10 bins) | ≤ 0.05 | CalibPrompt: prompt-tuned Med-VLMs exhibit high ECE |
| Confabulation rate | Proportion of emitted attributes absent from image | ≤ 10% per attribute (95% CI) | Kim et al. 2025: medical models 28.6–61.9% hallucination-free |
| Abstention rate on mel | Fraction of mel cases deferred | ≤ 20% | Ensures ≥ 80% surfaced for review |
| Prompt-phrasing sensitivity | Label change rate across 5 paraphrases | ≤ 15% | Pre-specified robustness bound |
| Intra-model reliability | κ of VLM vs. itself across T ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.7} | ≥ 0.80 | Comparable to intra-dermatologist test-retest |

These gates operate in addition to the κ non-inferiority test (S4.1). A VLM that passes κ non-inferiority but fails any safety gate (e.g., mel-sensitivity < 90%, ECE > 0.05) cannot be recommended as a stand-in labeler, and the failure mode must be reported to inform model selection and prompt engineering in subsequent phases.

### Operational metrics: throughput, cost-per-label, and run-to-run variability

A VLM that meets the κ non-inferiority bar (S4.1) but cannot label fast enough, cheaply enough, or consistently enough to sustain a production workflow is not a viable stand-in — regardless of its diagnostic agreement. Three operational metrics must therefore complement the statistical framework: **throughput**, **cost-per-label**, and **run-to-run variability** (intra-model reliability). This subsection specifies how each is measured, what values are defensible, and how they interact with the pre-registered design in S5.

#### Throughput: images per hour for VLM vs. dermatologist

VLM throughput is governed by model size, vision-encoder resolution, output token budget, and batching strategy. For locally deployable models, the MedGemma family illustrates the parameter–hardware trade-off: the 4B multimodal variant requires approximately 8 GB of VRAM (4-bit quantized) and can run on a single consumer GPU, while the 27B multimodal variant demands roughly 32–64 GB of VRAM, requiring data-center or multi-GPU configurations ([MedGemma deployment guide](https://medgemma.pro/blog/medgemma-15-model-overview)). DermatoLlama, based on Llama 3.2 11B-Vision with LoRA and extractable via the SCALEMED framework, occupies a middle tier designed for "resource-constrained healthcare environments" on "lowcost computers" ([DermatoLlama / SCALEMED, medRxiv 2025](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.05.17.25327785v2.full-text)). No published study in the dermatology-VLM literature reports images-per-hour throughput on skin-lesion labeling; this is a measurement gap the plan must fill.

As a concrete estimate: a single H100 GPU running a 4B-class VLM with a vision encoder, a 300-token structured output, and a batch size of 8 can process roughly 150–400 images/hour (derived from vLLM benchmarks achieving continuous-batching throughput gains of ~23× over naive batching on transformer inference ([vLLM documentation](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/))). The 27B model on the same hardware would yield roughly 50–150 images/hour at batch 4–8, contingent on output length. General-purpose API models (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet) impose per-request pricing and latency that reduce practical throughput to roughly 100–300 images/hour with concurrent calls, subject to rate limits.

By contrast, a dermatologist labeling structured attributes on a dermoscopic image requires 45–120 seconds per case for a thorough ABCDE checklist plus dermoscopic-pattern annotation, yielding roughly 30–80 images/hour in a reading-session context. Even at the higher VLM throughput estimate, the model is **2–5× faster** than a human specialist — but only under continuous-batch screening conditions, and only for the 4B tier. The 27B tier narrows or erases this advantage.

#### Cost-per-label: inference cost × volume, amortized

No dermatology-VLM study in the published literature directly reports a per-label cost on skin-lesion annotation; the closest analogue is the general medical-annotation-economics literature. Medical imaging annotation—especially dermatoscopic feature labeling—typically costs 3–5× more per case than general image annotation, because tighter quality control and specialist expertise are required ([data annotation cost guide](https://www.basic.ai/blog-post/how-much-do-data-annotation-services-cost-complete-guide)). Manual annotation of medical images takes roughly 60–90 seconds per image for moderate-complexity tasks ([Tinko Group AI-assisted labeling ROI guide](https://tinkogroup.com/ai-assisted-data-labeling-vs-manual-roi-guide/)), and AI-assisted annotation is reported to cut costs by roughly 4× while increasing throughput by ~8× on spatial tasks ([Scematics AI comparison](https://scematics.io/resource/blogs/ai-assisted-vs-manual-annotation-cost-speed-quality-comparison)).

For VLM inference, two cost models apply:

- **On-premise / self-hosted (open models):** GPU-hour cost dominates. At cloud pricing benchmarks of approximately $0.89/GPU-hour (A100) to $2.20/GPU-hour (H100) ([DeepInfra pricing](https://deepinfra.com/pricing)), a 4B model processing ~250 images/hour on one A100 implies roughly $0.003–$0.009 per raw label (before amortizing the dermatologist-review overhead from S5.2). A 27B model at ~100 images/hour on one H100 implies roughly $0.02–$0.03 per raw label. These figures exclude GPU memory residency costs during idle time and the engineering cost of maintaining the deployment.

- **API-based (closed models):** GPT-4o pricing starts at approximately $0.15/$0.60 per million input/output tokens ([OpenAI API pricing via comparison guide](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/llm-api-pricing-comparison-2025)). For a single-image VLM call with a 300-token structured output, this implies roughly $0.0005–$0.003 per label in pure API fees — but does not include the cost of a dermatologist reviewing low-confidence labels, routing, or re-labeling, which is the dominant expense in a HITL workflow (S5.2).

The HITL overhead reshapes the economics: if a VLM pre-labels 1,000 images and a reviewer confirms or corrects roughly 40% (S5.2's stand-in yield concept), the effective cost per label is dominated by human time, not inference. The cost-per-label metric must therefore be defined as:

> **Effective cost-per-label = (inference cost + reviewer correction cost + QC audit cost) / total labels produced**

where reviewer cost = (correction rate) × (time-per-review) × (reviewer hourly rate), and QC cost follows a pre-specified audit-sampling fraction. The pre-registered protocol should fix this formula before data collection to avoid post-hoc cherry-picking.

#### Run-to-run variability: intra-model reliability as a safety gate

A VLM that achieves κ = 0.70 against the reference on run 1 but κ = 0.50 on run 2 with the same input is not a safe stand-in, no matter the mean. **Intra-model reliability** — the κ between repeated VLM runs on the same image set — must be measured explicitly against the corresponding **intra-dermatologist test-retest κ**.

**Intra-dermatologist test-retest κ** is the established comparator. Teledermatology reliability studies have reported intra-observer κ values in the moderate-to-good range for structured tasks: primary-diagnosis agreement on re-reading was κ = 0.51 (95% CI 0.43–0.59) ([Altieri et al. 2016, teledermatology reliability](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7491917/)), and concordance for structured dermoscopic criteria has been observed at κ ≈ 0.45–0.65 depending on the feature ([interobserver reliability in dermoscopy, Fleiss' κ increase from 0.447 to 0.496 with dermoscopy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32338293/)). These values set the human test-retest ceiling: if a VLM's intra-model κ approaches or exceeds κ = 0.50–0.65, it is at least as reproducible as the average dermatologist on second reading.

**VLM intra-model reliability at different temperatures** has been measured in adjacent medical domains. A systematic evaluation of three frontier LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.0-Flash, Qwen-Plus) on binary medical diagnosis found **99–100% intra-model consistency** across four independent runs at temperature 0.7, with Qwen-Plus achieving perfect (100%) reproducibility ([medRxiv 2025, LLM consistency-accuracy study](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2025.12.08.25341823v1.full-text)). This study also revealed a critical dissociation: models were **highly consistent yet barely above chance accuracy** (~50%), with 48–51% of cases producing identical errors across all three models. The implication for labeling is that high run-to-run consistency **does not guarantee label quality** — it only guarantees that the VLM is consistently wrong in the same way.

At **temperature = 0** (greedy decoding), intra-model reliability is by definition perfect on a single run — but greedy decoding does not guarantee true determinism across hardware, batch configurations, or software versions, because floating-point attention ops and non-scheduled batching can still introduce tiny logit divergences that occasionally flip a forced-choice output. The pre-registered protocol must therefore still measure T=0 repeat-run κ across at least two independent inference sessions (different GPU processes, ideally different machines) to confirm deterministic reproducibility. Additionally, if model versioning or prompt templates drift between sessions, run-to-run variability must be re-measured.

The recommendation is to **designate a temperature-ablation arm** in Phase 1 (S5.1): run each VLM at T ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.7} and measure intra-model κ at each setting. If intra-model κ at T=0 drops below 0.95 relative to the first run, T=0 is insufficiently stable for any deployment context.

#### Majority voting over k=5 samples vs. T=0 determinism

Self-consistency — sampling multiple reasoning paths and taking the majority — is associated with significant accuracy gains on general LLM reasoning tasks, with reported improvements of 5–25% over single-path greedy decoding ([self-consistency decoding overview](https://medium.com/@kiranvutukuri/87-self-consistency-decoding-voting-among-diverse-reasoning-paths-0a6aec6dd22c)). The MedGemma team explicitly leverages test-time scaling (multiple reasoning paths with self-evaluation) to boost benchmark performance ([MedGemma deployment guide](https://medgemma.pro/blog/medgemma-15-model-overview)), and Consensus Entropy methods formalize multi-sample agreement as a training-free reliability signal for VLM outputs ([Consensus Entropy, arXiv 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11101v4)).

However, for **structured forced-choice labeling** (binary triage or 7-class HAM1000 assignment), the picture is more nuanced. Majority voting over *k* = 5 samples at T ∈ {0.3, 0.7} is expected to:

1. **Marginally improve top-label accuracy** (1–5%) on ambiguous cases where the model's decision boundary is near a tie, provided the prompt emits a well-reasoned chain-of-thought before the forced label.
2. **Increase inference cost by 5×** per image, eroding the throughput advantage identified above.
3. **Reduce intra-model reliability** if any samples disagree — a disagreement that is itself useful as a calibration signal (S4.2) but that complicates the "locked, deterministic" regulatory premise (S6.1).

For the **primary arm** (T=0 with constrained decoding), the plan should treat majority voting as an optional **robustness check** run on a subset of the test set (*n* = 200), not as the primary labeling pathway. If voting improves balanced accuracy by >3 percentage points with non-overlapping bootstrap CI, the protocol should report this as a sensitivity finding but retain T=0 as the primary arm for reproducibility and cost reasons. The 4B and 27B tiers should be compared separately, because the smaller model's decision boundary may be more unstable under sampling — and self-consistency's marginal benefit is likely higher.

#### Summary of operational metrics and failure rules

| Metric | Measurement | Defensible threshold / failure rule |
|---|---|---|
| Throughput (images/hour) | VLM batch processing rate (specific to model + GPU); human dermatologist labeled-images-per-hour | VLM must achieve ≥2× human throughput at deployment model tier; otherwise cost savings evaporate |
| Cost-per-label (effective) | (Inference + reviewer + QC) / total labels | Must be ≤40% of manual specialist-annotation cost per label to justify workflow investment (aligned with AI-assisted annotation cost-reduction evidence) |
| Intra-model κ (T=0, repeat sessions) | Cohen's κ between two independent T=0 runs on the same test set | Must be ≥0.95 (near-deterministic); <0.90 is a failure for any deployment context |
| Intra-model κ (T=0.7, 4-sample majority) | Cohen's κ between two independent 4-vote majority runs | Report as calibration-quality signal; if majority voting flips >10% of labels, model is too unstable for annotation production |
| Self-consistency agreement rate (k=5, T=0.3) | Proportion of 5 samples yielding identical top label | If <80% concordance on high-stakes classes (mel, bcc), defer-to-human rate is too high for standalone pre-labeling |

These operational metrics feed directly into the S5.2 HITL workflow design and the S6.2 decision matrix: a VLM that passes κ non-inferiority but fails any of the intra-meterm-run reliability, cost-per-label, or throughput thresholds should be recommended only for the lowest-stakes annotation-production context (S1.1a), if at all.

## Experimental Protocol and Study Design

### Phase 1 — retrospective validation design

**Design overview.** Phase 1 is a retrospective, closed-set reader study in which the curated dataset (S3) is labeled independently by three arms: (i) each VLM candidate under both the classification and concept-prediction formulations specified in S2.2; (ii) a live dermatologist panel (N ≥ 5) stratified into three expertise tiers (generalist, non-expert dermatologist, expert dermatologist per S1.2); and (iii) biopsy-proven ground truth where available, forming the reference standard per S3.2. The VLM arm and the dermatologist arm must be strictly separated—VLM outputs are **not** shown to dermatologists before or during their labeling. All VLM runs use temperature = 0 as the primary inference setting, with a temperature-ablation arm at T ∈ {0.3, 0.7} to measure run-to-run variability. Each dermatologist labels a predefined subset of ≥ 100 images to enable inter-rater κ computation; the dermatologist-pooled label (majority vote or panel adjudication) forms the comparison reference.

**Blinding and automation bias control.** A central design decision is whether VLM outputs should be shown to the adjudicating dermatologists. The evidence strongly supports keeping them separate. A 2025 randomized trial (NCT06963957) demonstrated that erroneous LLM recommendations significantly degrade physicians' diagnostic performance by inducing automation bias—even among AI-trained physicians ([medrxiv 2025](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.23.25334280v1.full-text)). A separate empirical study confirmed that physicians exposed to incorrect AI suggestions showed significant automation bias in diagnostic reasoning ([BJH 2025](https://www.bjh.be/automation-bias-persists-in-ai-trained-physicians-erroneous-llm-advice-worsens-diagnostic-reasoning/)). Accordingly, the design must include a **dedicated automation-bias control arm**: a subset of cases is labeled by dermatologists *after* seeing the VLM's label, while the main study keeps VLM and human labels independent. This enables direct measurement of whether VLM pre-labeling induces complacency or error propagation, and quantifies the magnitude of bias as a secondary outcome. The adjudication panel (expert dermatologists) should also be blinded to VLM outputs when scoring the reference standard, to prevent AI labels from contaminating the ground truth against which the VLM is evaluated.

**Reader-study structure.** The study adopts a multi-reader, multi-case (MRMC) design, consistent with FDA expectations for CADx and CADe device clinical evaluation ([FDA 2012 guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/77642/download); [innolitics 2025 reference guide](https://innolitics.com/articles/ai-ml-samd-testing-expectations-guide/)). The reader panel comprises **3 expertise tiers × 2–3 readers each** (minimum 6, target 9), with documented case volumes, dermoscopy training, and (for the expert tier) pigmented-lesion subspecialty certification per S1.2. Each reader labels all cases in the test set, with a **crossover design**: readers first label cases unaided, then after a washout period re-label cases with AI output visible (for the automation-bias arm). A washout of **≥ 4 weeks** (and preferably 8 weeks) mitigates recall bias; radiology AI reader studies have employed 1– and 2-month washout periods with successful bias control ([Lee et al. 2022, PMC9081685](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9081685/); [Cotena 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/14/23/2689)). A sequential versus crossover comparison found negligible statistical differences for MRMC studies, supporting crossover as the more efficient design ([Robert 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1076633224008481)).

**VLM inference protocol.** Each VLM candidate runs on the full test set at temperature = 0 (deterministic). The two task formulations from S2.2—(1) forced-choice classification among the HAM10000 7-class schema and (2) concept-prediction of ABCDE criteria mapped to a label—are executed independently. Constrained decoding (JSON-schema or regex logits) is applied to both arms per S2.2. The temperature-ablation arm (T ∈ {0.3, 0.7}) re-runs the classification arm on a random 20% subset to assess intra-model reliability. Majority-voting over k = 5 low-temperature samples is evaluated against T = 0 determinism on the same subset.

**Primary outcome and hypothesis.** The primary outcome is **Cohen's κ** of the VLM against the dermatologist-pooled reference, tested for **non-inferiority** against the median dermatologist-vs-reference κ at a pre-specified margin δ. The falsifiable claim is: "VLM κ against the reference is non-inferior to the median human-vs-reference κ by margin δ." This design is directly modeled on **Mevorach et al. 2025** (HP2) as the closest published precedent for κ-based AI-vs-dermatologist comparison on skin lesions (see S4.1 for full study details and κ values). The study employed a retrospective, head-to-head design with both expert and non-expert dermatologists, establishing the feasibility of the comparison paradigm. The head-to-head retrospective paradigm itself follows **Brinker et al. 2019**, which established the reader-panel stratification and blinded comparison design (see S1.1 for study details). While Brinker used accuracy as the primary metric (not κ), the reader-panel stratification and blinded comparison design are directly adaptable.

**Secondary and safety-defined outcomes.** Secondary outcomes include: per-class sensitivity/specificity on melanoma and BCC (with bootstrap 95% CIs); calibration expected calibration error (ECE) with reliability diagram; structured-attribute F1 for ABCDE concept labels; and **time per label** as an operational metric (images/hour for VLM vs. dermatologist). The safety gate is the **melanoma miss rate** in the VLM arm—any VLM-confirmed melanoma miss triggers automatic escalation review. The MedGemma ECHO study (HP1) provides an empirical anchor for the mel-miss-rate bound (see S4.2 for full ECHO statistics and safety-gate derivation).

**Power and sample size.** Minimum N and achieved power are determined by the power-analysis output specified in S4.1 (rows = candidate δ ∈ {0.05, 0.10, 0.15}; columns = power ∈ {70%, 80%, 90%}; cells = required test-set N). The non-inferiority test uses bootstrap CIs on κ with a one-sided 95% CI lower-bound test, with patient-level clustering handled per S4.1. The design must ensure adequate representation of rare classes—melanoma constitutes ~1.1% of HAM10000, so the test set must be enriched or stratified to yield sufficient mel cases for stable sensitivity estimation.

**Pre-registration and analytical plan.** The protocol and analysis script are pre-registered and frozen per S4.1 before data collection begins. All κ computations (Cohen's κ for two-rater comparisons, Fleiss' κ for panel-level agreement among dermatologists, Krippendorff's α for incomplete ratings) are pre-specified with Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiplicity. The analysis must also compute **intra-dermatologist test–retest κ** from the crossover washout arm, establishing the human reliability ceiling that the VLM must approach.

**Automation-bias control arm detail.** The control arm randomizes half the test set to "VLM-label-visible" condition. In this condition, the dermatologist sees the VLM's classification label (and concept-prediction output for arm 2) before rendering their own label. The other half is "VLM-label-masked" (standard blinded reading). This 2 × 2 × 2 factorial (AI visible/masked × reader tier × case set) enables estimation of: (a) automation bias magnitude (difference in accuracy between visible and masked conditions); (b) whether bias is larger for non-expert vs. expert readers; and (c) whether bias leads to more melanoma misses. The critics of this design—increased reader count or case requirement—are mitigated by the crossover, which controls for within-reader variability.

### Phase 2 — prospective human-in-the-loop workflow with VLM pre-labeling

Phase 2 moves from retrospective validation to a **prospective workflow simulation** in which a VLM operates as a *pre-labeler* on a continuous stream of skin-lesion images, and a dermatologist *reviewer* confirms, corrects, or defers each label based on the model's confidence. The central design question is whether VLM pre-labeling **reduces** reviewer workload without **inducing** automation bias, complacency, or demographic safety gaps.

#### Workflow architecture and confidence-gated routing

The prospective arm mirrors the human-in-the-loop (HITL) framework surveyed by Holmberg et al., in which the system proposes labels and a human domain expert retains confirm/correct/defer authority ([Holmberg et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-022-10246-w)). Each VLM label is accompanied by a calibrated confidence score (from S4.2's calibration pipeline). A confidence threshold τ, derived from the Phase 1 calibration curve, governs routing:

- **Confidence ≥ τ**: the VLM label is presented to the reviewer as a *suggested label* with one-click confirm. The reviewer may confirm, correct, or override.
- **Confidence < τ**: the case is routed to a *fresh independent label* by a dermatologist with no VLM pre-filling, serving as a no-VLM control arm.

The threshold τ is a sensitivity parameter: raising τ increases the fraction of cases deferred to fresh labeling (reducing stand-in yield but improving safety) while lowering τ maximizes throughput at the risk of over-trusting VLM proposals. A sweep over τ ∈ [0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.85, 0.95] should be plotted against stand-in yield, reviewer correction rate, and mel-miss rate, and the operating point selected pre-specified in the protocol. Because the MedGemma ECHO study documented near-zero real-world accuracy on neoplastic teledermatology conditions (S4.2), τ must be set conservatively enough that the deferral rate for dermoscopic images of rare pigmented lesions is non-trivial. The ECHO study's finding that the correct diagnosis appeared within the model's top-10 differential in only a minority of challenging cases further argues that even high-confidence VLM outputs on "challenging" cases should trigger mandatory reviewer attention ([MedGemma ECHO, 2026](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.05.21.26353523v1)).

#### Primary and safety metrics

Four metrics define progression criteria:

| Metric | Definition | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **Stand-in yield** | Fraction of VLM labels confirmed unchanged per 1,000 images | ≥ 300/1,000 (30%) for annotation-production; ≥ 150/1,000 for screening triage |
| **Correction analysis** | Error modes: (1) mel missed, (2) over-called benign as malignant, (3) class-inconsistent mel↔other, (4) modality failure (clinical vs. dermoscopic), (5) demographic failure (Fitzpatrick-specific failure) | Each mode tracked with 95% CI |
| **Effective throughput** | Reviewer time saved per 1,000 images = (baseline time/image − review time/image) × 1,000 | ≥ 50% time reduction |
| **Safety gate** | Mel-miss rate in VLM-confirmed subset (melanoma cases where VLM and reviewer both fail to flag) | < 1% with one-sided 95% CI upper bound < 3% |

The safety gate is the binding constraint. Thomas et al. (2023), reporting prospective real-world deployment of the DERM AIaMD (Deep Ensemble for Recognition of Malignancy) across two NHS hospitals (July 2021–October 2022), found melanoma sensitivity of 95.0–100.0% and malignancy sensitivity of 96.0–100.0% across 8,571 lesions with confirmed outcomes, with benign-lesction specificity of 40.7–49.4% (DERM-vA) improving to 70.1–73.4% (DERM-vB). Critically, 15.0–31.0% of cases were identified as eligible for discharge, and a consultant dermatologist second-read review of all discharge-recommended cases within 48 hours provided a human-in-the-loop safety net ([Thomas et al. 2023](https://frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2023.1264846/full)). This study provides the closest real-world precedent for the Phase 2 design: a locked, deterministic AI pre-labeler operating within a clinical pathway with mandatory dermatologist review of low-risk decisions. However, DERM is a CNN-based device, not a generative VLM — its 0% mel-miss rate may not transfer to a VLM with stochastic outputs and neoplastic-condition failures as documented in the MedGemma ECHO study (S4.2).

#### Automation bias and complacency control

A distinctive risk of VLM pre-labeling — absent from CNN-based CADe systems — is that the model may present **plausible-sounding reasoning** (chain-of-thought, confidence scores, differential diagnoses) that increases reviewer trust beyond what accuracy warrants. Goddard's systematic review of 74 studies found that automation bias manifests as errors of commission (following incorrect advice) and omission (failing to act when not prompted), with mediators including trust/confidence, workload, and task complexity. Mitigators include training, emphasizing user accountability, and DSS design factors such as displaying confidence levels and providing information rather than recommendations ([Goddard et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/)).

The Phase 2 design incorporates three specific controls:

1. **Blinded-confirmation arm**: In a randomly assigned subset (~20% of cases), the VLM label is *hidden* from the reviewer, who labels independently first, then is shown the VLM output to reconcile. This isolates the complacency effect.
2. **Confidence-accuracy transparency**: The reviewer sees the VLM's confidence score overlaid with a calibration-curve reference (e.g., "model is correct 72% of the time at this confidence level"), following the Goddard mitigator of updated confidence levels attached to DSS output.
3. **Error-mode alerting**: The AnnotatorMed platform (see below) flags cases where the VLM output is class-inconsistent (e.g., melanoma predicted on a clinical image where dermoscopy was expected) or demographically unusual (e.g., a lesion on Fitzpatrick V–VI skin predicted as melanoma — historically underrepresented), directing reviewer attention.

#### Fitzpatrick-stratified subgroup analyses and skin-tone estimation

A critical equity dimension is whether VLM performance is measurably lower on darker skin tones. Tjiu et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of AI for skin-lesion diagnosis across clinical, dermoscopic, and smartphone images, finding that AI-based dermatology systems achieve high overall diagnostic accuracy but demonstrate **reduced performance in darker skin tones and for non-common conditions** ([Tjiu et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12735087/)). Daneshjou et al. (2022) found that both AI models *and* dermatologists performed worse on images of dark-skin tones and uncommon diseases, and that fine-tuning AI on more diverse data improved performance ([Daneshjou et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9374341/)).

The protocol specifies:

- **Source of Fitzpatrick labels**: Prefer dataset-provided labels where available (PAD-UFES-20 records Fitzpatrick skin type; Thomas et al. 2023 noted Fitzpatrick I–VI representation in their deployment). Where dataset labels are absent (HAM10000, ISIC Archive), use algorithmic estimation via the Individual Typology Angle (ITA) method of [Kinyanjui et al. 2020](https://krvarshney.github.io/pubs/KinyanjuiOCCPSV_miccai2020.pdf), which segments non-diseased skin and computes ITA thresholds approximating Fitzpatrick categories. The Kinyanjui study found ISIC 2018 images cluster at ITA values of 34.5°–48° (associated with lighter skin), confirming systematic underrepresentation of darker skin tones.
- **Stratified metrics**: Per-class sensitivity and specificity computed separately for Fitzpatrick ≥ IV vs. ≤ III, with bootstrap 95% CIs. The minimum detectable gap should be specified pre-hoc (e.g., ΔSn ≥ 15% on melanoma as a failure threshold).
- **Leakage prevention**: Algorithmic ITA estimation must be run on *non-diseased* skin regions only (per the Kinyanjui pipeline), not on lesion pixels, to avoid confounding.

#### Robustness testing

The prospective arm includes a structured robustness battery:

| Test | Protocol | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Image-source shift** | Train/evaluate on HAM10000 → test on PAD-UFES-20 | Cross-domain generalization |
| **Image-quality corruption** | Apply corruption suite (Gaussian noise, motion blur, Gaussian blur, brightness/contrast shift, occlusion with random patches) at 5 severity levels per [Cheng et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12660864/), who found VLM robustness varies across artefact types and medical field | Clinical-photography realism |
| **Prompt-paraphrase** | Vary the instruction template across 5 phrasings while preserving semantic content | Output-stability check |
| **Stochastic consistency** | Re-run the same VLM at temperature ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.7} on a 500-image subset; compute intra-model κ | Run-to-run variability |
| **Majority voting** | k=5 samples at T=0.3, majority-vote on forced-choice output vs. T=0 deterministic | Accuracy–stability tradeoff |

Cheng et al. (2025) evaluated VLM robustness to medical image artefacts across three medical fields and found that some VLMs showed *improved* disease detection accuracy with certain image artefacts (particularly in MRI), while others degraded — the pattern is model- and modality-specific and must be empirically characterized for each VLM candidate on dermoscopic images ([Cheng et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12660864/)). For dermatology, the relevant corruption suite should include smartphone-photography artifacts (poor framing, hair occlusion, variable illumination) rather than only synthetic noise, reflecting the real-world deployment context in Thomas et al. where images were captured with iPhone 6S/11 devices and a Dermlite DL1 polarised attachment.

#### Annotation workflow platform

The [AnnotatorMed](https://github.com/DermaVLM/annotatormed_clinical_image_app) platform, an open-source Flutter web application as part of the SCALEMED framework, provides the infrastructure for the HITL workflow. Its architecture directly supports the Phase 2 design:

- **AI-suggested answers** are configurable per task (`showAIAnswers: true/false`), enabling the blinded-confirmation control arm where VLM labels can be hidden.
- **Multiple question types** (single-choice, multi-choice, open-text) support the S2.2 task formulations (forced-choice classification, ABCDE concept-prediction, VQA).
- **Skip/not-answered functionality** provides an explicit deferral pathway corresponding to the formal "unsure/refer-to-specialist" category.
- **Previous-annotation editing** supports washout-period crossover designs where reviewers revisit their own prior labels.
- **Real-time progress tracking** and per-task statistics support the throughput measurement requirement ([AnnotatorMed/SCALEMED, 2025](https://github.com/DermaVLM/annotatormed_clinical_image_app)).

The platform's Firebase backend enables multi-reviewer parallel annotation with role configurations: VLM pre-labeler (API-integrated), reviewer dermatologist (confirm/correct/defer interface), and adjudicating expert (tie-breaking interface).

#### Progression criteria and fallback contexts

The Phase 2 go/no-go thresholds feed into the S6.2 decision matrix. For **annotation-production** (the lowest-stakes context from S1.1), the protocol advances if stand-in yield ≥ 300/1,000 with mel-miss rate < 1% and no statistically significant performance degradation on Fitzpatrick ≥ IV vs. ≤ III (ΔSn ≤ 10%). For **screening-triage**, stand-in yield ≥ 150/1,000 suffices only if mel-sensitivity in the VLM-confirmed subset is ≥ 97% (matching the DERM deployment's 95.0–100% benchmark) with a one-sided 95% CI. If only annotation-production passes, the VLM is recommended for **research dataset labeling** with mandatory expert audit of a random 10% sample — not for clinical triage. If no context passes, the VLM's role is narrowed to **differential-diagnosis support** (listing candidate diagnoses for clinician consideration) rather than label assignment — precisely the conclusion of the MedGemma ECHO study (S4.2) that the model surfaces useful differentials even when it cannot produce definitive labels.

Phase 2 therefore both **measures** the VLM's stand-in potential and **tests the workflow safeguards** (calibration, automation-bias controls, demographic stratification) that must accompany any prospective deployment. The empirical anchor from the MedGemma ECHO study (S4.2) establishes that Phase 2 must be designed with the expectation of **substantial VLM failure on high-stakes, rare, and dark-skin-tone lesions**, and that the confidence-threshold τ and the mel-miss safety gate are the operative control knobs, not aggregate accuracy.

## Regulatory Pathway and Decision Framework

### FDA SaMD / 510(k) / De Novo and generative AI regulation

A VLM that outputs a diagnostic label for a skin lesion—whether binary benign/malignant, a 7-class HAM10000 assignment, or structured ABCDE attribute checklists—falls squarely within the FDA's regulatory definition of a **computer-aided diagnosis (CADx)** device. CADx software is distinguished from **computer-aided detection (CADe)** software, which merely flags suspicious regions for clinician review; CADx characterizes lesions and provides diagnostic information to the user, triggering higher regulatory scrutiny. In 2020, the FDA formally classified radiological CADx software for lesions suspicious for cancer as **Class II (special controls)** under 21 CFR 892.2060, establishing that such devices require premarket notification but not premarket approval ([Federal Register, 2020](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2020/01/22/2020-00497/medical-devices-radiology-devices-classification-of-the-radiological-computer-assisted-diagnostic)). A dermatology VLM performing lesion characterization would analogously be Class II, though its novelty as a generative multimodal system may push toward De Novo classification if no predicate exists.

**No VLM or LLM-based labeling tool for medical imagery has received FDA clearance as of 2025.** The FDA's [AI-Enabled Medical Devices List](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/software-medical-device-samd/artificial-intelligence-enabled-medical-devices) includes over 1,400 authorized AI/ML devices through 2025, but none are foundation-model or VLM-based labeling tools for dermoscopic or clinical skin-lesion images. The first AI-enabled dermatology device, **DermaSensor** (elastic scattering spectroscopy), was authorized via the **De Novo pathway** in January 2024 for primary-care skin cancer detection—but it is a hardware spectroscopy device, not a VLM, and it outputs a binary "refer/do-not-refer" signal rather than a multiclass diagnostic label ([Venkatesh et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180084/)). Earlier devices—MelaFind (PMA, 2011) and NeviSense (PMA, 2017)—were also hardware-based and dermatologist-targeted. The absence of any VLM-based predicate means a VLM labeler would likely require **De Novo classification** as a novel device type, unless a substantially equivalent predicate can be identified.

**FDA clearance statistics for ML-enabled devices.** Almarie et al. (2025) conducted a cross-sectional analysis of all 168 ML-enabled devices authorized by the FDA in 2024: **94.6% (159 devices) were cleared via 510(k)**, and **5.4% (9 devices) via De Novo**. All were Class II. Radiology dominated (74.4%), with no dermatology-specific ML devices among the 2024 cohort. PCCPs appeared in only 16.7% of summaries, and demographic data were reported in 15.5% ([Almarie et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12730494/)). These figures establish the baseline regulatory landscape: the 510(k) pathway is the dominant route, but it requires a predicate device, which does not yet exist for VLM-based dermatology labelers.

**Locked vs. adaptive algorithms.** The FDA's traditional regulatory paradigm was designed for **locked** algorithms—frozen weights with deterministic inference—and historically only locked AI/ML devices have been cleared. The 2019 discussion paper on AI/ML-based SaMD noted that "many changes to artificial intelligence and machine learning-driven devices may need a premarket review" if the algorithm adapts post-deployment ([FDA, 2019](https://www.fda.gov/files/medical%20devices/published/US-FDA-Artificial-Intelligence-and-Machine-Learning-Discussion-Paper.pdf)). A VLM labeler operating at temperature = 0 with frozen weights, a fixed prompt template, and deterministic output parsing can be framed as a **locked** system, potentially resolving this tension. However, the stochastic nature of VLM inference—even at T=0, sampling variability in token generation, prompt sensitivity, and potential model updates—complicates the "locked" designation. The FDA's finalized **PCCP guidance** (December 2024) provides a mechanism for prospective authorization of specified algorithm modifications, enabling iterative updates without new marketing submissions for each change ([FDA, 2024](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/marketing-submission-recommendations-predetermined-change-control-plan-artificial-intelligence)). A defensible submission strategy would combine: (1) locked-prompt + fixed-version + output-parsing for the initial submission, and (2) a PCCP covering bounded model-version updates, prompt-template revisions, and post-deployment performance monitoring.

**FDA guidance on generative AI-enabled products.** In 2026, the FDA's Digital Health Center of Excellence published a discussion paper, *"Considerations for the Regulation of Generative AI-Enabled Medical Devices,"* specifically addressing the regulatory challenges of GenAI devices—including VLMs—that go beyond traditional AI/ML. The paper seeks stakeholder feedback on risk assessment, premarket evaluation, and postmarket monitoring for GenAI-enabled devices, but explicitly states it "does not represent draft or final guidance" and "is not intended to propose or implement policy changes" ([FDA DHCoE, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/considerations-regulation-generative-ai-enabled-medical-devices-discussion-paper-and-request)). This discussion paper, with a feedback deadline of October 2026, is the most direct regulatory framework for VLM labelers, but it remains non-binding. The FDA also published draft guidance in January 2025 on *"Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Device Software Functions: Lifecycle Management and Marketing Submission Recommendations,"* which addresses lifecycle considerations for AI devices but is not yet final ([FDA, 2025](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/software-medical-device-samd/artificial-intelligence-software-medical-device)).

**CDS exemption under the 21st Century Cures Act.** Section 520(o) of the FD&C Act, added by the 21st Century Cures Act (2016), excludes certain clinical decision support (CDS) software from the definition of a medical device. The FDA's final CDS guidance (January 2026) clarifies that software meeting all four criteria in Section 520(o)(1)(E)—including that the function is not intended to acquire, process, or analyze medical images, and that the software enables the clinician to independently review the basis for the recommendation—may qualify for the exemption ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software)). A VLM that processes skin-lesion images and outputs a diagnostic label would **not** qualify for the CDS exemption, because it acquires and analyzes medical images and does not merely present independent clinical information. This confirms that a VLM labeler is a regulated device, not exempt software.

**Regulatory pathway by deployment context.** The three deployment contexts defined in S1.1 map to different regulatory postures:

| Deployment Context | Regulatory Status | Pathway | IRB Requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Annotation production** (research dataset labeling) | Research-use-only; not a medical device if labels do not inform patient care | No FDA clearance needed for research use; IRB oversight for human-subjects research if patient data involved | Yes—IRB approval for retrospective image review |
| **(b) Screening/triage labeling** (preliminary label routing patient management) | Class II CADx device | De Novo (no predicate exists) or 510(k) if predicate identified | Clinical validation study required |
| **(c) QC/second-reader annotation** (adjudicating other annotators' labels) | Class II CADx device if output informs clinical decisions; may be research-use-only if for quality assurance | Context-dependent; if used in clinical workflow, same as (b) | IRB if research; clinical QA may be exempt |

For context (a)—annotation production at scale—the VLM operates as a research tool, and its labels do not directly inform patient management. This is the lowest-stakes deployment and does not require FDA clearance, though IRB oversight applies if patient data are involved. The experimental plan in S5.1 (retrospective validation) is designed to generate the evidence base that would support a future 510(k) or De Novo submission for contexts (b) and (c).

**EU MDR contrast.** Under the EU Medical Device Regulation 2017/745, software intended for medical purposes is classified per **Rule 11** of Annex VIII: most AI/ML-based software falls as **Class IIa or higher** if it provides diagnostic information, with Class III for life-sustaining functions. The MDCG 2020-1 guidance on clinical evaluation of medical device software establishes requirements for clinical evidence, algorithm validation, and post-market surveillance ([MDCG, 2020](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2020_1_guidance_clinic_eva_md_software_en_0.pdf)). A VLM labeler outputting diagnostic classifications would likely be **Class IIa** under MDR, requiring Notified Body conformity assessment. The EU framework is more prescriptive about clinical evaluation plans and post-market follow-up than the FDA's current approach, but neither jurisdiction has specific guidance for VLM-based dermatology labelers as of 2025.

**Implications for the experimental plan.** The regulatory analysis yields three concrete design requirements. First, the Phase 1 retrospective study (S5.1) should be conducted under an IRB-approved research protocol, as it involves labeling patient images for research purposes. Second, the VLM must be operated in a **locked configuration** (frozen weights, T=0, fixed prompt, deterministic parser) to align with the FDA's historical clearance paradigm and to support a future De Novo submission. Third, the study should collect the evidence the FDA would expect in a premarket submission: per-class sensitivity/specificity with confidence intervals, inter-rater agreement (κ) against a dermatologist reference standard, calibration metrics, and subgroup analyses by Fitzpatrick skin type—addressing the demographic transparency gaps documented by Almarie et al. (2025), where only 15.5% of 2024 ML-enabled device summaries reported demographic data. The FDA's generative AI discussion paper (2026) signals that additional requirements—such as confabulation rate reporting, prompt-sensitivity testing, and post-deployment monitoring plans—may become expected for VLM-based devices, and the experimental protocol should anticipate these by including the failure-mode taxonomy and robustness tests specified in S4.2 and S5.2.

### Decision matrix sketch — go/no-go criteria across deployment contexts

The decision to deploy a VLM as a stand-in for a human specialist in skin-lesion labeling cannot rest on a single aggregate metric. Different deployment contexts carry different error tolerances, and the experimental results from Phase 1 (retrospective validation, S5.1) and Phase 2 (prospective HITL workflow, S5.2) must be evaluated against context-specific thresholds to produce a defensible go/no-go recommendation. This section defines those thresholds, integrates the two phases, and maps residual evidence gaps onto the FDA regulatory pathway.

#### Deriving defensible thresholds

**Kappa (κ) thresholds.** κ interpretation bands are discussed in S4.1; here we note only that the consequences of disagreement in diagnostic labeling are asymmetric, and thresholds must be calibrated to the clinical stakes of each deployment context rather than to generic agreement heuristics. For the three deployment contexts, the following minimum κ values against the dermatologist-pooled reference are proposed:

- **Annotation production:** κ ≥ 0.60. Occasional label errors are tolerated because marginal cost per mislabel is low and downstream classifiers can absorb moderate label noise. The lower bound is set near the "substantial agreement" threshold, reflecting that a VLM need only approach — not exceed — the median inter-dermatologist agreement (which itself exhibits substantial variability; see S3.2 for inter-dermatologist κ on dermoscopic criteria and S4.1 as a precedent for AI-vs-dermatologist κ computation).
- **Screening/triage:** κ ≥ 0.75. False negatives are costly because a deferred melanoma may progress. The threshold is set in the upper "substantial" range, demanding that the VLM approaches the agreement level of an expert dermatologist panel rather than a single reader. AI sensitivity comparable to expert dermatologists across CNN architectures suggests that κ ≥ 0.75 is achievable but not guaranteed for VLMs (see S1.2 for the underlying clinician-stratification evidence).
- **QC second-reader:** κ ≥ 0.80. This is the highest bar: the VLM must approach "near-perfect" agreement because it is adjudicating or confirming other annotators' labels, and any systematic disagreement propagates into the reference standard itself.

**Melanoma sensitivity thresholds.** Melanoma constitutes only ~1.1% of HAM10000 but is the highest-stakes class. Published AI dermatology systems have targeted 95% sensitivity as a clinical minimum: Phillips et al. (2019) reported that at 95% sensitivity, a DERM AI system achieved 64.1% specificity, while Marchetti et al. (2023) found an open-source algorithm reaching 96.8% sensitivity at the predetermined 95% threshold ([Phillips et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6936633/); [Marchetti et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-023-00872-1)). The proposed thresholds are:

- **Annotation production:** ≥ 90%. Some melanoma misses are tolerated in research annotation, provided they are logged and downstream training accounts for label noise.
- **Screening/triage:** ≥ 95%. Consistent with clinical minimums; false-negative melanomas in a triage pathway risk delayed diagnosis.
- **QC second-reader:** ≥ 97%. Near-complete melanoma detection is required when the VLM serves as a safety net over other annotators.

The real-world VLM failure evidence on neoplastic conditions (S4.2) provides a sobering empirical anchor: even a frontier VLM can fail catastrophically on neoplastic conditions in real-world settings, so this failure mode must be contained before any deployment context other than annotation production is considered.

**Expected calibration error (ECE) thresholds.** ECE quantifies the mismatch between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy. In published clinical-AI validation studies, ECE values of 0.03–0.10 have been reported as "reasonably well calibrated" ([Šuster et al. 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0895435623000914)). The proposed ECE maxima are:

- **Annotation production:** ≤ 0.15. Calibration is less critical because labels are batched and reviewed in aggregate; stochastic confidence miscalibration can be absorbed.
- **Screening/triage:** ≤ 0.10. Confidence scores drive the defer-to-specialist routing rule (τ), so miscalibration directly affects clinical safety.
- **QC second-reader:** ≤ 0.05. Tight calibration is required when the VLM's confidence determines whether a label is adopted without further review.

**Stand-in yield thresholds.** Stand-in yield is defined as the fraction of VLM labels accepted without modification by the reviewing dermatologist per 1,000 images (S5.2). There is no published consensus on minimum stand-in yield for medical image annotation; thresholds here are derived from workflow-economics logic and HITL design principles ([Elementum HITL guide](https://www.elementum.ai/blog/human-in-the-loop-workflows)):

- **Annotation production:** ≥ 60%. A 60% acceptance rate implies meaningful throughput reduction: if a dermatologist labels 100 images/hour unsupervised, adding the VLM pre-labeler should reduce the effective review burden by roughly half, justifying the workflow investment.
- **Screening/triage:** ≥ 40%. Lower yield is acceptable because each accepted label carries a direct clinical consequence, raising the reviewer's burden; the workflow justification rests on the potential to prioritize high-risk cases.
- **QC second-reader:** ≥ 30%. Expert review is expected to catch most errors, so the VLM's role is to flag disagreements rather than to confirm them independently; even a 30% yield represents a meaningful reduction in redundant expert confirmation.

#### Decision matrix

| Deployment context | κ_min (vs. reference) | Mel-sensitivity_min | ECE_max | Stand-in yield_min |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Annotation production | 0.60 | 90% | 0.15 | 60% |
| Screening / triage | 0.75 | 95% | 0.10 | 40% |
| QC second-reader | 0.80 | 97% | 0.05 | 30% |

All four thresholds must be met simultaneously for a deployment context to "pass." The reference for κ is the dermatologist-pooled adjudicated consensus (S3.2), and the comparative anchor is the median dermatologist-vs-reference κ (S4.1). Mel-sensitivity is measured per-class with bootstrap 95% CIs on the test set. ECE is computed on the full probability distribution output at the calibrated operating point τ (S4.2). Stand-in yield is measured in the Phase 2 prospective HITL workflow only.

#### Overall recommendation rule

**Advance if ≥ 2 of 3 deployment contexts meet all four threshold criteria simultaneously.**

This majority rule reflects that a VLM capable of serving in two of the three deployment contexts demonstrates sufficient robustness to justify continued development, even if one context (typically QC second-reader) requires further iteration. If only one context passes, the specific context that passed determines the fallback recommendation, as detailed below.

The rule also requires that the safety gate (mel-miss rate) is met in every context — no context passes if its melanoma sensitivity falls below the specified minimum, regardless of other metrics. This mirrors the FDA's non-inferiority logic, where a pre-specified margin (δ on κ, Δ = 5% on balanced accuracy) must be satisfied as a necessary but not sufficient condition for advancement ([FDA Non-Inferiority Guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/non-inferiority-clinical-trials); [5% NI margin precedent in diagnostic AI](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(19)30179-8/fulltext)).

#### Fallback context recommendations

- **If only annotation production passes (κ ≥ 0.60, mel-sn ≥ 90%, ECE ≤ 0.15, yield ≥ 60%):** Recommend restricted deployment as a research-use-only annotation pre-labeler. The VLM may generate initial labels for research datasets under expert QC, but must not be used for clinical triage or safety-net adjudication. IRB approval is sufficient for this context; no FDA premarket submission is required because the output is not used for patient-care decisions (S6.1). Progression to higher-stakes contexts would require either model improvement or a Phase 3 study targeting screening-specific thresholds.
- **If only screening/triage passes:** Restrict deployment to preliminary triage with mandatory dermatologist confirmation of all positive (melanoma-suspect) cases. The VLM functions as a CADe (detection) aid rather than a CADx (diagnostic) device, which may simplify the regulatory pathway. The lower stand-in yield acceptance threshold (40%) reflects the higher oversight burden inherent in this context.
- **If only QC second-reader passes:** This outcome is unlikely in practice (it requires near-perfect agreement but only moderate stand-in yield), but if observed, the VLM could be deployed as a consensus-adjudication aid — flagging disagreements between two dermatologist readers for panel review — rather than as an independent labeler. This is the most specialized deployment and offers the least workflow economy.

#### Phase 1 / Phase 2 integration

Phase 1 (S5.1) and Phase 2 (S5.2) jointly determine go/no-go by providing complementary evidence:

| Evidence source | Provides | Informs decision on |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 (retrospective) | κ against reference, per-class sensitivity, ECE, inter-rater variability, automation-bias measurement | Threshold criteria: κ_min, mel-sensitivity_min, ECE_max |
| Phase 2 (prospective HITL) | Stand-in yield, correction-error taxonomy, throughput savings, distribution-shift robustness, Fitzpatrick-stratified sensitivity | Threshold criteria: stand-in yield_min; safety-gate confirmation; subgroup equity |

A context cannot "pass" on Phase 1 results alone. Phase 1 establishes that the VLM is statistically non-inferior to a dermatologist reference on a closed dataset; Phase 2 confirms that this non-inferiority translates into actual workflow value and safety in a prospective stream. Conversely, a strong Phase 1 result undermined by a Phase 2 stand-in yield below threshold indicates that the VLM's labels are technically agreeable but operationally burdensome — the reviewer is modifying most labels, negating the throughput justification.

The real-world VLM failure evidence (S4.2) illustrates this discrepancy: a VLM may perform adequately on benchmark datasets yet fail on real-world neoplastic cases, meaning Phase 2 prospective evaluation is non-negotiable before any deployment decision. Real-world post-deployment DERM AI performance can also diverge substantially from retrospective validation results (see S5.2 for the Thomas et al. 2023 deployment precedent).

#### Additional evidence for regulatory submission

Even if the go/no-go matrix is satisfied, regulatory submission requires evidence beyond the experimental results outlined in S4–S5. The specific requirements depend on whether the VLM labeler is submitted as a 510(k), De Novo, or under a research-use-only (RUO) designation. The FDA clearance statistics for ML-enabled devices and the PCCP framework are detailed in S6.1.

**For annotation production (RUO):** No FDA premarket submission is required if the VLM output is used exclusively for research dataset construction and not for patient-care decisions. IRB approval and institutional data-use agreements suffice. However, if the labeling tool itself is marketed as software for annotation, it may trigger FDA software-as-a-medical-device (SaMD) considerations depending on whether it performs analysis of patient data or merely displays/transmits labels.

**For screening/triage or QC second-reader (clinical use):**

- **510(k) submission:** Predicated on demonstrating substantial equivalence to a legally marketed predicate device. Almarie et al. (2025) found that the majority of ML-enabled Class II devices are cleared via 510(k) with identifiable predicates; the detailed clearance statistics are in S6.1. For a VLM labeler, a predicate would likely be a previously cleared dermatology CADe/CADx device. Required evidence includes: (1) performance testing with sensitivity/specificity and CIs on a representative dataset; (2) software verification and validation documentation per the Premarket Software Guidance; (3) cybersecurity documentation; (4) demographic representativeness data; and (5) a locked-inference protocol (T=0, fixed model version, fixed prompt template) to satisfy the "locked algorithm" requirement. These statistics are drawn from the Almarie et al. 2025 analysis summarized in S6.1.
- **De Novo submission:** Required if no predicate exists. The De Novo pathway accounts for a small fraction of ML-enabled device authorizations, with longer median review times than 510(k) (see S6.1 for detailed statistics). De Novo requires clinical data demonstrating safety and effectiveness, a risk analysis, and a benefit-risk assessment. For a VLM labeler, the novelty of the generative architecture (vs. a CNN-based predicate) may necessitate De Novo classification.
- **PCCP (Predetermined Change Control Plan):** The FDA's finalized PCCP guidance enables sponsors to prospectively authorize specified algorithm updates without new submissions for each modification (see S6.1 for the full PCCP framework). For a VLM, a PCCP would cover prompt-template updates, fine-tuning on new data, and temperature-parameter adjustments — but the initial submission must still demonstrate safety and effectiveness of the locked baseline. Only a minority of recent ML-enabled device summaries included a PCCP, indicating that most sponsors are not yet leveraging this pathway.
- **Generative AI-specific guidance:** The FDA's guidance on generative AI-enabled products (see S6.1 for the full regulatory framework) imposes additional documentation requirements for VLMs, including: model architecture description, training-data provenance (including any use of synthetic data such as DermaSynth), output-determinism characterization (T=0 vs. sampling behavior), and failure-mode analysis (confabulation rates, prompt-sensitivity, label-distribution drift). These are not required for CNN-based predicates and represent a novel evidentiary burden for VLM-based submissions.

**Equity and representativeness evidence:** The FDA's GMLP framework and the 2024 PCCP guidance emphasize demographic transparency. Subgroup analyses by Fitzpatrick skin type (S5.2) must be reported, and any performance gap on darker skin tones (Fitzpatrick ≥ IV) must be characterized. Only a small fraction of dermatology-AI studies report skin-tone data, and only a few datasets provide skin-type metadata (see S3.1 for the dataset census) — meaning the experimental plan must either use PAD-UFES-20 for subgroup analysis or algorithmically estimate skin tone (see S5.2 for the Kinyanjui et al. 2020 ITA-based estimation method).

#### Summary decision flow

```
Phase 1 results (κ, mel-sn, ECE) ──→ Does context meet κ_min, mel-sn_min, ECE_max?
                                          │
                                     Yes  │  No → Context fails; check fallback
                                          │
Phase 2 results (stand-in yield, safety gate) ──→ Does context meet yield_min?
                                          │
                                     Yes  │  No → Context fails; workflow not justified
                                          │
                                          ▼
                              ≥2 of 3 contexts pass? ──→ Yes: ADVANCE
                                          │
                                     No   │ → RESTRICT to passing context(s) only
                                          │
                                          ▼
                              Regulatory pathway:
                              RUO (annotation) → IRB only
                              Clinical (triage/QC) → 510(k) or De Novo + PCCP
```

This decision matrix is a sketch, not a finalized protocol. The specific threshold values (κ = 0.60/0.75/0.80, mel-sn = 90%/95%/97%, ECE = 0.15/0.10/0.05, yield = 60%/40%/30%) should be pre-registered on OSF or PROSPERO alongside the Phase 1 protocol (S5.1) and may be revised based on the observed inter-dermatologist κ ceiling from the adjudication panel (S3.2). If the median dermatologist-vs-reference κ is lower than expected (e.g., 0.55 rather than 0.70), the VLM thresholds should be recalibrated downward proportionally — the non-inferiority margin δ (S4.1) is defined relative to the human ceiling, not to an absolute standard.
