# Experimental Plan for Testing Whether a Vision–Language Model Can Replace Dermatologists in Skin-Lesion Image Labeling

## Executive summary

This study should answer a deliberately narrow question: **can a modern vision–language model produce structured labels for skin-lesion images that are non-inferior to specialist dermatologists under a prespecified labeling protocol?** The intended use should initially be **research/dataset annotation and possibly pre-labeling**, not autonomous patient diagnosis. A retrospective benchmark showing expert-level classification would not, by itself, establish safety or authorization for clinical diagnosis. Current FDA guidance for AI-enabled device software emphasizes total-product-lifecycle risk management, while IMDRF guidance treats clinical evaluation as a distinct requirement for software used in medical decisions. [[1]](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/artificial-intelligence-enabled-device-software-functions-lifecycle-management-and-marketing)

The decisive evaluation should **not rely primarily on familiar public benchmarks**. HAM10000 contains 10,015 dermoscopic images and remains valuable for development, but it is heavily reused in machine-learning research. [[2]](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018161) More importantly, contamination is demonstrable for some modern candidates: the official MedGemma documentation cites PAD-UFES-20 and SCIN among its dermatology training resources, while PanDerm was pretrained on more than two million dermatology images drawn from public and proprietary sources. [[3]](https://huggingface.co/google/medgemma-27b-it) Therefore, the confirmatory endpoint should use a **locked, multicenter proprietary cohort**, ideally acquired after model release or otherwise verified to be absent from pretraining, with a separate prospective silent-validation cohort.

A practical design is a three-stage study:

- **Development:** public datasets for taxonomy mapping, prompt design, classifier training and parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

- **Locked retrospective reader study:** approximately **3,000 unique lesions**, including at least **450 pathology-confirmed malignant lesions**, from at least three institutions, each independently read by three dermatologists.

- **Prospective silent external validation:** roughly **1,000 consecutive lesions** from a new site and/or later time window, with model outputs hidden from treating clinicians.

The recommended co-primary endpoints are **multiclass labeling performance** and **malignant-lesion sensitivity**. A defensible initial non-inferiority margin is **5 percentage points**, subject to clinical justification before database lock. The model should be declared capable of replacing specialist labeling only if both endpoints are non-inferior, calibration is adequate, major subgroup safety signals are absent, and external prospective validation succeeds. A model that falls short of this bar may still be useful in a **human-in-the-loop workflow**, where it generates preliminary labels and dermatologists verify malignant, uncertain, out-of-distribution and abstained cases.

The candidate panel should combine medical and generalist VLMs with specialized vision baselines: google/medgemma-1.5-4b-it, google/medgemma-27b-it, Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, meta-llama/Llama-4-Scout-17B-16E-Instruct, PanDerm, and microsoft/BiomedCLIP-PubMedBERT_256-vit_base_patch16_224. MedGemma is especially relevant because its SigLIP vision encoder was medically pretrained on de-identified dermatology and other medical images; Qwen3-VL and Llama 4 provide strong general-purpose multimodal controls; PanDerm provides a dermatology-specific foundation-model baseline; and BiomedCLIP supplies a contrastively pretrained biomedical VLM baseline. [[4]](https://huggingface.co/google/medgemma-1.5-4b-it)

The study should be preregistered and reported under **STARD-AI**, whose 2025 guideline specifically calls for transparent description of AI index tests, dataset practices, bias, fairness and evaluation methodology; an author correction was published in July 2026 and should be incorporated when preparing the manuscript. [[5]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03953-8)

## Study objective and hypotheses

**Primary objective.** Determine whether a frozen VLM can replace a board-certified dermatologist for **structured image-label annotation** of skin lesions, when both are judged against an independent reference standard and receive the same prespecified information.

The target task should be fixed before model development. A sensible primary schema is a clinically interpretable multiclass taxonomy—melanoma, melanocytic nevus, basal-cell carcinoma, squamous-cell carcinoma/Bowen disease, actinic keratosis, benign keratosis, dermatofibroma, vascular lesion and an “other/uncertain” category—plus a clinically important derived binary endpoint, **malignant versus non-malignant / biopsy-or-refer versus routine**. HAM10000 and BCN20000 demonstrate that such multiclass dermoscopic taxonomies are feasible, although their class definitions are not perfectly identical and therefore require explicit harmonization. [[6]](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018161)

For every VLM response, require a machine-readable schema such as:

{
  "diagnosis": "<fixed label>",
  "malignant_probability": 0.00,
  "confidence": 0.00,
  "abstain": false,
  "image_quality": "<adequate|limited|uninterpretable>"
}

Free-text explanation can be retained as a secondary research output, but should not determine the primary label. Self-reported verbal “confidence” should likewise not be treated as a calibrated probability unless it is empirically validated; when possible, probabilities should derive from constrained class likelihoods or a calibrated classification head.

**Primary hypotheses.** Let Dclass be model minus dermatologist performance on multiclass accuracy or macro-F1, and Dsens the corresponding difference in malignant-lesion sensitivity. Using a proposed initial non-inferiority margin Δ=0.05:

H0,class:Dclass≤−0.05,  H1,class:Dclass>−0.05

H0,sens:Dsens≤−0.05,  H1,sens:Dsens>−0.05.

The replacement claim requires **both** null hypotheses to be rejected. The 5-point margin is a design recommendation, not a universal clinical standard; it should be justified prospectively by dermatologists, biostatisticians and the intended downstream use. Because these are co-primary requirements forming an intersection-union decision rule, success should require both one-sided tests to pass rather than accepting a favorable aggregate result.

Secondary hypotheses should assess: equivalence within ±5 points; model–reference agreement by Cohen's κ; AUROC and AUPRC; calibration; performance with versus without clinical metadata; model abstention; cross-site transportability; and subgroup performance. Fairness analyses are particularly important because the Diverse Dermatology Images study was expressly constructed across Fitzpatrick groups and demonstrated that dermatology AI can behave differently across skin tones and disease subsets. [[7]](https://aimi.stanford.edu/datasets/ddi-diverse-dermatology-images)

A useful **decision rule** is stricter than “statistically significant accuracy.” Declare the VLM a viable substitute for specialist labeling only if it:

- passes both co-primary non-inferiority endpoints;

- retains performance on a fully external site/time cohort;

- exhibits clinically acceptable calibration and malignant false-negative rates;

- shows no prespecified severe subgroup failure;

- maintains a low invalid-output rate and effective abstention mechanism; and

- reproduces under a frozen checkpoint, prompt, preprocessing pipeline and decision threshold.

Otherwise, classify it as an **assistant/pre-labeler rather than a specialist replacement**.

**Proprietary cohort composition.** The confirmatory subset should contain approximately 3,000 **unique lesions**, with no patient or lesion represented in development data. At least 450 malignant lesions should have pathology confirmation, and sampling should deliberately include darker skin tones, rare classes, acral/nail/mucosal sites, poor-quality photographs, multiple devices and all participating sites. A useful structure is a prevalence-representative core plus an enriched safety cohort. PPV, NPV and calibration should be reported on the prevalence-representative core—or appropriately weighted—because deliberate malignancy enrichment changes prevalence-dependent metrics.

**Gold-standard hierarchy.** For excised lesions, use the pathology diagnosis with verified lesion–specimen linkage; difficult melanocytic lesions should be eligible for dermatopathology rereview. For non-excised lesions considered benign, use a predeclared combination of longitudinal stability, clinically documented resolution, or independent expert consensus rather than silently equating a single clinician's label with ground truth. HAM10000 itself illustrates why heterogeneous reference methods need to be documented explicitly: its labels arose from pathology, follow-up, expert consensus and confocal microscopy. [[2]](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2018161)

Crucially, the dermatologists who serve as the **human comparator should not simultaneously define the reference standard from their test reads**. Each test case should instead have three independent blinded dermatologist readings, while a separate adjudication panel—preferably including a senior dermatologist and dermatopathologist—resolves reference-standard ambiguities using pathology, longitudinal evidence and all permissible source records.

**De-identification and provenance.** In U.S. covered settings, HHS identifies **Expert Determination** and **Safe Harbor** as the two HIPAA Privacy Rule routes for de-identification. [[13]](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/de-identification/index.html) Imaging-specific ETL should additionally remove EXIF/DICOM identifiers, embedded names or identifiers and unnecessary timestamps; assess visible faces, tattoos or other identifying features; retain re-identification keys only inside the originating institution; and maintain a provenance record linking each derived image to its source, consent/waiver status, lesion identifier, acquisition modality and gold-standard evidence.

## Models and baselines

The experiment should test architectures that answer different scientific questions: does medical pretraining help, does scale help, does general multimodal capability transfer to dermatology, and does a specialized image encoder outperform a generative VLM?

PanDerm should be treated as a **vision foundation baseline rather than a conversational VLM**. Its value is scientific: if a specialized non-generative encoder matches or exceeds generative VLMs, the language component may not be necessary for structured labeling. PanDerm's publication reports pretraining on more than two million real-world images and reader studies showing dermatology-specific utility, making it a particularly strong domain baseline. [[18]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03747-y)

**Prompting and adaptation arms.** For every generative VLM, evaluate the same four progressively more adapted conditions:

| Arm | Input and training |
| --- | --- |
| Zero-shot image-only | Image + fixed label definitions + constrained JSON output |
| Zero-shot image+metadata | Add only prespecified age, sex, anatomical location and modality; no pathology/history unavailable to human readers |
| Few-shot | Fixed exemplars chosen solely from training data, identical across all test cases |
| Adapted | Supervised instruction fine-tuning or LoRA/QLoRA on training data using class-balanced sampling |

The **confirmatory inference configuration must be deterministic** where technically possible: temperature 0 / greedy decoding, frozen system prompt, fixed image preprocessing, pinned checkpoint revision and a schema validator. Invalid labels should be recorded as failures or abstentions according to a rule written before test unlock, not manually repaired after seeing outcomes.

For probability-based analyses, constrain the output space to the allowed classes and recover normalized class likelihoods where the serving framework exposes them. If a model cannot provide meaningful class probabilities, add a prespecified calibrated classification head to a frozen representation and treat free-text confidence as secondary.

Known pretraining overlap must be recorded in a **contamination matrix**. For example, PAD-UFES-20 and SCIN should not be promoted as pristine MedGemma test sets because they are listed in MedGemma documentation. [[11]](https://huggingface.co/google/medgemma-27b-it) In general, public benchmark absence from a model card does not prove absence from pretraining, so the strongest safeguard is newly collected, institution-controlled test data.

## Experimental design and statistical analysis

The study should use a locked, multi-reader/multi-case design rather than comparing a published VLM score against a published dermatologist score.

| --- |

**Splitting and sampling.** All partitions must be grouped first by patient and then lesion; every photograph of one lesion belongs to one split. Do not perform naïve random image splitting. Use public data primarily for development; a reasonable internal development allocation is 70/15/15 when an official split does not exist, but the proprietary **confirmatory test must remain untouched**. Cross-site validation should use leave-one-site-out experiments during development and, ideally, one completely untouched institution or temporal period for final validation.

The locked 3,000-lesion cohort can be stratified into approximately 2,000 cases reflecting the target labeling population and 1,000 deliberately enriched cases covering malignancy, rare diagnoses, darker skin tones, hard anatomical sites and acquisition failures. This supports both realistic performance estimation and sufficiently precise safety analyses.

**Blinded human reads.** Recruit six to ten board-certified dermatologists, ideally mixing general dermatologists and dermoscopy/skin-cancer specialists. Randomly assign each case to three readers. Readers should be blinded to pathology, final chart diagnosis, other readers and VLM output. The model receives only the fields prespecified for its arm. Case order should be randomized. If image-only and image-plus-metadata conditions are read by the same clinician, use a counterbalanced crossover or adequate washout to minimize recall.

The reader study should preserve each raw human label. Reference-standard adjudication occurs separately. This avoids the circular comparison “model versus consensus of the same people counted as the human comparator.”

### Evaluation framework

| Metric | Primary purpose | Recommended analysis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Accuracy** | Overall exact label correctness | Patient-level bootstrap CI; report alongside prevalence and class mix |
| **Macro-F1 / balanced accuracy** | Prevent common classes from dominating multiclass results | Co-primary candidate for structured labeling; per-class bootstrap |
| **Sensitivity / specificity** | Safety-critical malignant detection and one-vs-rest classes | Primary malignant sensitivity; report specificity at locked threshold |
| **AUROC** | Threshold-independent discrimination | Binary malignant AUROC and macro one-vs-rest multiclass AUROC; paired DeLong test for appropriate binary comparisons |
| **AUPRC** | Discrimination when malignancy/classes are uncommon | Report with class prevalence |
| **Calibration** | Whether predicted probability corresponds to observed risk | Brier score, reliability curve, calibration intercept/slope and ECE |
| **Cohen's** κ | Agreement beyond chance | Model–gold and individual dermatologist–gold; use multi-rater extension/average pairwise agreement for the human panel |
| **F1** | Precision–recall balance | Micro, macro and per-class values; macro preferred for imbalance |
| **Confusion matrix** | Identifies clinically meaningful error transitions | Raw and row-normalized matrices, plus malignant false negatives highlighted |
| **Decision-threshold performance** | Operationalizes “biopsy/refer” rule | Threshold chosen on validation data only; report sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV and workload |
| **Selective-risk / coverage** | Tests benefit of abstention | Error versus retained-case fraction and abstention rate |
| **Subgroup metrics** | Bias/transportability | Repeat sensitivity, specificity, AUC, F1 and calibration by site, skin tone, sex, age, location, modality and image quality |

Thresholds must **never be optimized on the locked test set**. A clinically defensible primary rule is to choose, on the validation set, the threshold that reaches a target malignant sensitivity—for example 95%, if clinicians judge that target appropriate—and then maximize specificity under that constraint. Argmax multiclass prediction and a Youden-index threshold can be secondary analyses.

Calibration should be fitted only on development/validation data. Calibration slope near 1 and intercept near 0 are desirable, but the protocol should avoid using arbitrary pass/fail cutoffs unless clinically justified.

### Statistical analysis plan

The primary analysis should compare the VLM with dermatologists **on the same cases**. For binary paired outcomes, use McNemar-type discordance analysis where appropriate. For AUROC, use paired DeLong analysis for standard binary comparisons; for macro/multiclass metrics and most subgroup contrasts, use patient-level bootstrap estimates. A mixed-effects regression model is preferable for the full reader study: fixed effect for rater type (VLM versus dermatologist), with case and human reader effects and site terms/interactions to account for repeated evaluations.

Use approximately **2,000 stratified bootstrap replicates**, resampling at the patient—not image—level, preserving within-patient image correlation. Report 95% confidence intervals for all major metrics and effect differences. Exploratory subgroup analyses should have prespecified strata and use false-discovery-rate control, such as Benjamini–Hochberg, rather than selectively highlighting significant subgroups.

For non-inferiority, calculate

D=PVLM−PDerm.

The endpoint passes if the lower one-sided 97.5% confidence bound exceeds −0.05. As a secondary analysis, perform two one-sided tests for equivalence using a ±0.05 performance margin. A separate agreement margin, such as ±0.05 in κ, can be prespecified for secondary equivalence analysis, but should not substitute for accuracy and sensitivity.

**Planning sample-size calculation.** A conservative simple-binomial calculation for malignant sensitivity near p=0.90, one-sided α=0.025, 90% power and Δ=0.05 is:

npositive≈p(1−p)(z0.975+z0.90)2Δ2

≈0.9(0.1)(1.96+1.282)20.052≈379.

Allowing for exclusions, clustering and reference-standard uncertainty suggests targeting **at least 450 malignant test lesions**. The proposed total of 3,000 unique lesions gives additional precision for multiclass and subgroup analyses. This formula is only a planning approximation: the final sample size should be recalculated by simulation from pilot estimates of VLM–human discordance, reader variance, site heterogeneity and repeated images, because the actual study is paired and multi-reader/multi-case rather than a single independent proportion.

The prospective silent phase should then repeat the locked endpoint definitions on roughly 1,000 consecutive external cases. It should not be used to retune the model; failure should trigger a new development/version cycle rather than post hoc threshold adjustment.

## Error analysis, safety, ethics and regulation

**Error analysis should be designed before results are known.** Every malignant false negative should receive blinded root-cause review by at least two dermatologists; also audit a random sample of false positives, correct predictions and abstentions. Errors should be assigned to prespecified categories: intrinsic visual ambiguity; wrong gold label; melanoma/nevus or SCC/AK confusion; skin-tone effects; acral/nail/mucosal location; blur or compression; hair, ink, rulers or occlusion; modality shift; rare diagnosis; metadata conflict; out-of-distribution condition; and model-output/schema failure.

Run targeted robustness experiments by removing rulers/text/borders, changing crops, withholding metadata and introducing plausibly perturbed metadata. A model whose label flips because a nonclinical artifact or minor prompt wording changes should be flagged even when its aggregate accuracy remains high. Model explanations or saliency maps may help generate hypotheses, but should not be treated as proof that the model is reasoning causally from the lesion.

**Bias.** DDI exists precisely because conventional dermatology datasets inadequately characterize performance across skin tones: its 656 images were sampled across Fitzpatrick I–VI and matched in part to support such comparisons. [[7]](https://aimi.stanford.edu/datasets/ddi-diverse-dermatology-images) SCIN adds broader common-condition and self-captured-image diversity, but its crowdsourced labels are different in epistemic quality from pathology-confirmed cancer labels. [[12]](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2826506) Accordingly, subgroup performance should be reported rather than “controlled away.” Prespecify analyses by validated skin-tone measure where available, age, sex, anatomical site, imaging modality, institution, device class and image quality. A clinically chosen sensitivity-gap threshold—for example a 10-point gap as a safety trigger—can mandate investigation even when subgroup tests lack statistical significance.

**Privacy and research ethics.** Obtain local IRB/HREC review and a consent or waiver determination appropriate to each site. HIPAA de-identification should follow Safe Harbor or Expert Determination where applicable. [[13]](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/de-identification/index.html) Public release of derived metadata should be separately risk-assessed: “de-identified for research processing” does not automatically imply that every combination of rare condition, age, dates and anatomical attributes is safe for unrestricted publication.

**Human-in-the-loop deployment.** Even a positive retrospective result should first support a workflow in which the VLM generates a proposed label and dermatologists verify it. Require manual review for malignant predictions, low-confidence cases, OOD inputs, abstentions and poor-quality images. FDA transparency principles specifically emphasize performance of the **human–AI team** and the information users need to understand the system. [[20]](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/software-medical-device-samd/transparency-machine-learning-enabled-medical-devices-guiding-principles) This also guards against automation bias: replacing the human immediately after a benchmark win removes the very safety layer most likely to catch distribution shifts.

**Regulatory scope.** A model used only to prepare a research dataset is different from software intended to diagnose or direct patient management. Once the intended use enters clinical decision-making, applicable device-software rules must be assessed. FDA's January 2025 AI-enabled device draft guidance recommends lifecycle documentation and risk management for AI device software, and FDA lists a final August 2025 guidance for predetermined change-control plans when future model modifications are contemplated. [[21]](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/artificial-intelligence-enabled-device-software-functions-lifecycle-management-and-marketing) IMDRF's final SaMD clinical-evaluation framework remains a relevant international reference. [[22]](https://www.imdrf.org/documents/software-medical-device-samd-clinical-evaluation)

FDA's Good Machine Learning Practice page now points to the IMDRF final set of ten GMLP principles, reinforcing lifecycle quality rather than one-time benchmark performance. [[23]](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/software-medical-device-samd/good-machine-learning-practice-medical-device-development-guiding-principles) Thus a continuously updated model should not be silently substituted for the frozen model tested in this experiment; version changes require predefined change management, validation and monitoring.

For EU deployment, assess both medical-device law and the **AI Act** under the then-current implementing guidance. The Commission describes the AI Act as a risk-based framework and states that AI constituting, or acting as a safety component of, products subject to third-party conformity assessment can fall into the high-risk category, including medical devices. [[24]](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) As of August 2026, implementation details have continued to evolve, and the Commission has issued updated high-risk-system guidance; therefore, legal classification should be performed against current intended use rather than assumed from the research protocol. [[25]](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-ai-high-risk-systems)

**Clinical claim discipline.** A positive result would support the claim:

“Under this fixed labeling taxonomy, input specification and study population, this frozen model was statistically non-inferior to the prespecified dermatologist comparator for retrospective image annotation and reproduced that performance on the external silent cohort.”

It would **not** automatically justify:

“The VLM can independently diagnose patients in clinical practice.”

The latter claim introduces consequences of history-taking, lesion selection, full-body context, management recommendations and downstream treatment that an isolated-image labeling experiment does not measure.

## Reproducibility, resources, timeline, and key references

**Reproducibility package.** Before the locked test set is opened, preregister the protocol, statistical analysis plan, hypotheses, non-inferiority margins, primary taxonomy, subgroup definitions, decision threshold method, abstention handling and replacement rule. Freeze and checksum:

dataset manifest → image preprocessing → prompt template → tokenizer → checkpoint revision → fine-tuning adapter → inference engine → decoding parameters → calibration transform → evaluation code.

Release, where licenses and privacy permit, the source code, prompt files, taxonomy mapper, split manifests, model/adaptor configuration, software environment, evaluation scripts and a synthetic or public-data integration test. For protected proprietary images, publish cryptographic hashes, data dictionary, cohort flow and split-generation code rather than the images themselves. This creates a reproducible test without violating data-use agreements.

STARD-AI should be the primary reporting checklist because it was created specifically for AI diagnostic-accuracy studies and explicitly covers dataset practice, the AI index test, bias, fairness and generalizability. [[26]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03953-8) For probability-prediction components, TRIPOD+AI is an additional useful framework; the updated statement applies to prediction models using regression or machine-learning methods. [[27]](https://www.tripod-statement.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/TRIPODAI_250319.pdf)

**Compute planning.** Exact memory and runtime depend on image resolution, quantization, serving engine and fine-tuning method, so these figures should be treated as engineering estimates rather than study endpoints.

| 4–8B LoRA/QLoRA adaptation | One 48–80 GB GPU; budget tens of GPU-hours for multiple seeds/configurations |
| MedGemma 27B | Prefer one 80 GB accelerator with memory-efficient loading or two GPUs for headroom |
| Llama 4 Scout | Meta reports the quantized Scout model can fit on a single H100; its MoE model has 17B active and 109B total parameters. [[17]](https://ai.meta.com/blog/llama-4-multimodal-intelligence/) |
| PanDerm downstream probing | Far below original pretraining cost; one GPU is sufficient for frozen-feature/linear-probe studies |
| Full experimental sweep | Reserve approximately four 80 GB-class GPUs for parallel inference/tuning plus 0.5–2 TB protected image/artifact storage |

The point is **not to reproduce foundation-model pretraining**. PanDerm, for example, already embodies dermatology-scale pretraining over more than two million images; the experiment should evaluate or adapt released weights rather than retrain an equivalent model. [[18]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03747-y) BiomedCLIP similarly supplies a released checkpoint derived from 15 million PubMed Central figure-caption pairs. [[19]](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/BiomedCLIP-PubMedBERT_256-vit_base_patch16_224)

A realistic **24-week schedule** is:

| Period | Milestone | Deliverable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weeks 0–4 | Finalize intended use, taxonomy, sites, DUA/IRB pathway, power assumptions | Protocol v1, data specification, provisional SAP |
| Weeks 3–8 | Ingest, de-identify, deduplicate, harmonize and link reference standards | Versioned cohort manifest and data-quality report |
| Weeks 7–12 | Public-data baselines, prompts, linear probes and LoRA experiments | Development benchmark report and selected model configuration |
| Week 13 | Freeze model, prompts, thresholds, exclusions and statistical plan | Signed model/data lock; preregistration |
| Weeks 14–18 | Locked 3,000-case VLM inference and dermatologist reader study | Immutable model and reader outputs |
| Weeks 18–20 | Adjudication, primary statistics, calibration and MRMC analysis | Primary results package |
| Weeks 20–22 | Error, fairness, robustness and cross-site analyses; begin/complete silent external cohort as volume permits | Safety/failure-mode report |
| Weeks 23–24 | Final analysis, reproducibility release and deployment/regulatory gap review | Technical report, STARD-AI checklist, code/config release and implementation recommendation |

If prospective case accrual cannot yield 1,000 cases within 24 weeks, the retrospective replacement decision should explicitly remain **provisional** until the prespecified prospective sample is completed; the prospective sample should not be reduced merely to meet the calendar.

The central methodological choice is therefore straightforward: **use public datasets to develop the model, but decide specialist substitutability on a blinded, contamination-resistant, multicenter human-reader study with an independent gold standard and a prospective external confirmation**. Model–dermatologist agreement alone is insufficient; the decision must simultaneously consider diagnostic correctness, malignant sensitivity, calibration, subgroup safety, abstention behavior and transportability. Only a model that passes that complete locked protocol should be considered a credible replacement for specialist *labeling*—and even then, clinical deployment should begin with human oversight rather than assuming that an annotation study establishes autonomous diagnostic safety.
