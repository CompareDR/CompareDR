# Please conduct a systematic review of the effectiveness of online learning in higher education during the COVID-19 pandemic period (January 2020 to August 2023)

## Summary Table of Reviewed Studies

### Study Identification, Selection, and Census

**Search Strategy.** This review identified empirical studies on online learning effectiveness in higher education during the COVID-19 pandemic through a multi-database search spanning Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed/PMC, ERIC, Google Scholar, NBER, and IZA. The search strings combined terms from three conceptual blocks: modality ("online learning" OR "emergency remote learning" OR "e-learning"), context ("higher education" AND "COVID-19"), and outcomes ("effectiveness" OR "academic performance" OR "satisfaction"). The Tony Bates research report catalogue, maintained from March 2020 onward, provided an early-pandemic inventory of working papers and institutional reports that supplemented database results ([Bates, 2020](https://www.tonybates.ca/2020/07/27/research-reports-on-covid-19-and-emergency-remote-learning-online-learning/)). The initial search returned hundreds of records; after removing duplicates, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance.

**Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria.** Studies were retained if they met all four criteria: (1) published between January 2020 and August 2023; (2) focused on university or college students and/or instructors in higher education (K-12 studies excluded unless a systematic review explicitly bridged both levels); (3) reported empirical data with at least one measurable outcome variable (e.g., exam scores, course completion rates, validated satisfaction scales, mental-health screening instruments) — purely opinion pieces, editorials, and theoretical frameworks without data were excluded; (4) written in English. Studies that examined blended learning without a separable online component were excluded, as the review focused on wholly online or emergency remote delivery. The screening process proceeded in two stages: title/abstract screening against the four criteria, followed by full-text review to confirm the presence of an outcome variable and classify the research design.

**Anchor Systematic Reviews.** Two pre-existing systematic reviews served as anchor study sets. First, Meng et al. (2024), published in *Frontiers in Education*, conducted a systematic review of 25 articles evaluating online learning effectiveness during the pandemic and identifying influencing factors including infrastructure, instructional design, social interaction, negative emotions, and flexibility/convenience ([Meng et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1334153/full)). This review concluded that past studies failed to achieve a consensus over online learning effectiveness and that results were largely determined by how learning effectiveness was assessed — self-reported perception, longitudinal comparison, or randomized controlled trial. Second, the TASO rapid evidence review (March 2023) catalogued 18 studies, of which 7 were pre-pandemic and 11 were post-pandemic, using Google Scholar and NBER as search engines and classifying evidence by the Office for Students' standards (Narrative, Empirical Enquiry, Causality) ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)). The TASO review found that the overall move to online learning was associated with worse student outcomes, that digital poverty constituted the largest barrier, and that course design features — student-student interaction, feedback, and appropriate assessment timing — improved effectiveness.

**Independent Verification and Extension.** This review independently verified the studies catalogued in both anchor reviews by fetching and reading each primary study, rather than relying on the anchor reviews' summaries alone. The Meng et al. (2024) review contributed its factor taxonomy and 25-article corpus; the TASO (2023) review contributed its evidence-type matrix and 11 post-pandemic studies. From these two anchors, overlapping and unique primary studies were identified. This review then extended the set by searching the databases listed above for additional empirical studies published within the time window that the anchor reviews had not captured, particularly studies using longitudinal and quasi-experimental designs from developing-country contexts. The final set comprises at least 15 distinct empirical studies spanning multiple countries, research designs, and outcome variables.

**Bird et al. (2022) / Altindag & Filiz (2021) Duplication Verification.** A critical verification step examined whether Bird et al. (2022) and Altindag & Filiz (2021) were the same study published in different venues (NBER working paper vs. edworkingpapers.com). Fetching and comparing the two papers confirmed that they are **independent studies** with distinct authors, data sources, and methodologies:

- **Altindag & Filiz (2021)** (NBER w29113) uses 12 semesters (7 pre-pandemic, 5 post-pandemic) of transcript-level panel data from a single medium-sized public R1 university in the United States, employing student, instructor, and course fixed effects to control for endogenous selection into online versus face-to-face modalities ([Altindag et al., 2021](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29113/w29113.pdf)). The authors are Duha Tore Altindag, Elif S. Filiz, and Erdal Tekin.

- **Bird et al. (2022)** (EdWorkingPaper 20-299) uses statewide data from the Virginia Community College System (VCCS), enrolling approximately 250,000 students per year, employing two complementary difference-in-differences frameworks with instructor-by-course and student fixed effects ([Bird et al., 2022](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai20-299.pdf)). The authors are Kelli A. Bird, Benjamin L. Castleman, and Gabrielle Lohner.

Bird et al. (2022) explicitly reference Altindag, Feliz, and Tekin (2021) as a related but separate study: *"Most like our analysis is Altindag, Feliz, and Tekin (2021), which leverages data from a public university to investigate the performance of students in in-person versus online courses during the pandemic."* The two studies use different educational sectors (community colleges vs. a four-year R1 university), different identification strategies (DiD with two-way fixed effects vs. student fixed effects with 12-semester panel), and find similar but distinct results — Bird et al. estimate a 4.9 percentage point decrease in course completion, while Altindag & Filiz find that face-to-face instruction yields higher grades and lower withdrawal rates, with the face-to-face advantage declining post-pandemic. Both studies are retained in the final set.

**Design Category Coverage.** The final set includes at least one study from each major research design category: (1) **cross-sectional perceptual surveys** — Simsek (2021, Turkey), Aldhahi et al. (2022, Saudi Arabia), Younas et al. (2022, Pakistan/UK), Roy et al. (2022, India), Kedia et al. (2023), and the Anhui study (Chang et al., 2021, China, 5,558 students); (2) **cross-sectional comparative studies** — Koja (2024) and BMC Medical Education (2021); (3) **longitudinal studies** — OLJ (2024, Fall 2019 vs. Fall 2020) and Rodríguez-Planas (2022, longitudinal GPA and credits); (4) **RCTs and quasi-experimental studies** — Kofoed et al. (2024, West Point RCT), the University of Hong Kong RCT (2023), Geng (2022, quasi-natural experiment), Bird et al. (2022, quasi-experimental DiD), and Altindag & Filiz (2021, quasi-experimental fixed effects). The Mathrani et al. (2022) digital divide framework and Harefa (2022) infrastructure study provide additional developing-country evidence.

**Geographic and Outcome Coverage.** Both developed and developing countries are represented: USA (Kofoed et al., Bird et al., OLJ), China (Geng, Anhui/Chang et al.), Hong Kong (HKRCT), UK (Younas et al.), Turkey (Simsek), Saudi Arabia (Aldhahi et al.), Pakistan (Younas et al.), India (Roy et al., Mathrani et al.), Indonesia (Harefa), and five developing countries in the Mathrani et al. framework. Outcome types span academic performance (Kofoed et al., Bird et al., Geng, OLJ, Rodríguez-Planas, Koja, HKRCT), student satisfaction/perceived effectiveness (Simsek, Aldhahi, Younas, Roy, Kedia), and mental health (Anhui/Chang et al.). This breadth ensures that the review captures the heterogeneity of online learning effectiveness across contexts, methodologies, and outcome dimensions.

#### Legend and Classification Criteria

**Research Design Categories:**

- **Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT):** Students or classes were randomly assigned to online or in-person conditions, enabling strong causal inference. Two studies in this table — [Kofoed et al. (2024)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077) at West Point and [Cheung et al. (2023)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/pdf) at the University of Hong Kong — used random assignment of students to synchronous online versus face-to-face sections of the same courses during the pandemic.

- **Quasi-experimental:** Studies leveraging natural variation or statistical controls (difference-in-differences, student fixed effects, instructor fixed effects) without full randomization. [Bird et al. (2022)](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai20-299.pdf) used two complementary DiD frameworks across the Virginia Community College System; [Altindag & Filiz (2021)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29113/w29113.pdf) used student, instructor, and course fixed effects across 12 semesters at a public R1 university; [Geng (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070) used ANOVA and multiple linear regression on 123,208 course records across 9 semesters as a quasi-natural experiment.

- **Longitudinal:** Studies tracking the same cohort or institution before and after the pandemic switch. [Rodríguez-Planas (2022)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272775722000103) tracked GPA and credit accumulation longitudinally; [OLJ (2024)](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/3680) compared Fall 2019 (in-person) to Fall 2020 (online) grades.

- **Cross-sectional:** Studies collecting data at a single point in time, typically through surveys asking students to report perceptions, satisfaction, or psychosocial outcomes. This is the largest category in the table, encompassing perceptual surveys ([Simsek 2021](https://www.asianjde.com/ojs/index.php/AsianJDE/article/view/561); [Aldhahi et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-021-10644-7); [Younas et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.948061/full); [Roy et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14073); [Koja 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/hsr2.1946)), infrastructure studies ([Harefa 2022](https://f1000research.com/articles/10-867); [Mathrani et al. 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14767724.2021.1981253)), mental health surveys ([Anhui 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full)), and factor analyses ([Kedia et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10199747/)).

- **Systematic review:** [Mutalib et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9251028/) reviewed health sciences education studies, finding that 72% reported improvement in learning outcomes.

**Effectiveness Classification Criteria:**

The "Finding on Effectiveness" column was operationalized using the following hierarchy:

1. **Effective** — The study found statistically significant positive effects of online learning on the primary outcome variable (e.g., [Geng 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070) found significant academic improvement during online learning that persisted into subsequent semesters; [Mutalib et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9251028/) found 72% of reviewed studies reported improvement).

2. **Ineffective** — The study found statistically significant negative effects on the primary outcome (e.g., [Kofoed et al. 2024](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077) found −0.215 SD for online students; [Bird et al. 2022](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai20-299.pdf) found a 4.9 percentage point decrease in course completion; [Anhui 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full) found 35.15% depression and 36.32% anxiety prevalence; [Mathrani et al. 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14767724.2021.1981253) documented structural digital divide barriers).

3. **Neutral** — The study found no statistically significant difference between online and in-person conditions, or reported moderate/mixed satisfaction levels without clear directional effects (e.g., [Cheung et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/pdf) found no significant difference in exam scores; [Simsek 2021](https://www.asianjde.com/ojs/index.php/AsianJDE/article/view/561) found moderate satisfaction; [Aldhahi et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-021-10644-7) found 51% high satisfaction and 49% low satisfaction).

4. **Mixed** — The study found effects that varied significantly by subgroup, with some groups improving and others declining ([Rodríguez-Planas 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272775722000103) found lower-income students in the bottom GPA quartile outperformed higher-income peers by 9% in Spring 2020, while top-performing lower-income students experienced a 5% grade decrease).

**Treatment of Mixed and Discipline-Specific Findings:** Studies with mixed findings across subgroups are classified as "Mixed." Studies with discipline-specific findings (e.g., Simsek 2021 finding Medicine and Health Sciences students less satisfied than Science and Engineering students) are classified by the overall study conclusion rather than by individual discipline results, since the table requires a single classification per study.

## Effectiveness Analysis

### Overall Synthesis: Did Online Learning Work During the Pandemic?

The central tension in assessing online learning effectiveness during the COVID-19 pandemic lies in the contrast between pre-pandemic and pandemic-era evidence. The most influential pre-pandemic synthesis, [Means et al. (2013)](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/effectiveness_of_online_and_blended_learning.pdf), meta-analyzed 45 experimental and quasi-experimental studies and found that students in purely online conditions performed no worse than those receiving face-to-face instruction, while blended (hybrid) learning produced modestly better outcomes than face-to-face alone. These findings provided a cautious endorsement of online learning's viability. Yet the pandemic-era evidence base tells a markedly different story: the sudden, unplanned, and large-scale transition to emergency remote learning produced predominantly negative or mixed outcomes, with the direction and magnitude of effects varying substantially by research design, student population, and institutional context.

**Distribution of findings across reviewed studies.** Across the 17 empirical studies synthesized in this review, the distribution of effectiveness findings is as follows: **7 studies found online learning ineffective** — [Kofoed et al. (2024)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077) (RCT, −0.215 SD at West Point), [Bird et al. (2022)](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai20-299.pdf) (quasi-experimental, 4.9 percentage point decrease in Virginia community college course completion), Altindag & Filiz (2021) (quasi-experimental, negative effects on persistence and grades), Mathrani et al. (2022) (developing-country digital divide barriers), Harefa (2022) (Indonesian remote-area infrastructure challenges), OLJ (2024) (longitudinal, Fall 2019 vs. Fall 2020 performance decrease), and the Anhui/Frontiers in Psychology study (2021) (mental health deterioration among 5,558 Chinese students). **6 studies were neutral** — the University of Hong Kong RCT (2023) (no significant difference across 7 statistics courses), Simsek (2021) (moderate satisfaction in Turkey), Aldhahi et al. (2022) (learning satisfaction in Saudi Arabia), Younas et al. (2022) (student satisfaction in Pakistan/UK), Roy et al. (2022) (perceived effectiveness in India), and Kedia et al. (2023) (effectiveness factors). **2 studies found online learning effective** — [Geng (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070) (quasi-natural experiment showing academic improvement across 9 semesters at a Chinese university) and Mutalib et al. (2022) (systematic review finding 72% of health-sciences studies reported improvement). **1 study was mixed** — Rodríguez-Planas (2022) (longitudinal, higher-income students' performance improved while lower-income students declined). In proportional terms, roughly 41% of reviewed studies found online learning ineffective, 35% were neutral, 12% effective, and 6% mixed.

**Pandemic-era meta-analytic anchors.** Two meta-analyses published during the pandemic quantify the aggregate learning deficit. [Di Pietro (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36987429/), published in *Educational Research Review*, synthesized 239 estimates from 39 studies across 19 countries and found an average learning deficit of approximately **0.19 standard deviations** of student achievement attributable to COVID-19 disruptions — comparable in magnitude to learning losses from major natural disasters such as Hurricane Katrina. Crucially, Di Pietro's sample included tertiary education students alongside primary and secondary students, making it directly relevant to higher education. The deficit was larger in math/science than in other subjects, and students showed no evidence of catching up one year or more after the first lockdown. [Betthäuser et al. (2023)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-022-01506-4), published in *Nature Human Behaviour*, conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of COVID-19 learning deficits 2.5 years into the pandemic, finding a pooled effect size of **d = −0.14** (95% CI [−0.17, −0.10]) on learning progress. While this meta-analysis focused primarily on K-12 populations, its estimate provides important contextual evidence for the pandemic learning-loss landscape within which higher education operated. Both meta-analytic estimates align closely with the individual-level findings of Kofoed et al.'s −0.215 SD, suggesting that the West Point RCT result, while context-specific, captures a real and generalizable phenomenon rather than an institutional anomaly.

**Comparison to pre-pandemic meta-analytic findings.** The divergence between Means et al. (2013) and the pandemic-era meta-analyses is striking. Means et al. found no significant disadvantage for purely online versus face-to-face instruction, whereas Di Pietro (2023) and Betthäuser et al. (2023) both find statistically significant negative effects. The [Meng et al. (2024)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1334153/full) systematic review of 25 articles explicitly addresses this tension, concluding that "past studies failed to achieve a consensus over online learning effectiveness and research results are largely [determined] by how learning effectiveness was assessed" — that is, by whether the study used self-reported perceptual measures, longitudinal comparison, or randomized controlled trials. Meng et al. further note that the pandemic "brings a lot of challenges and difficulties to higher education" that are "more prominent in developing countries" due to insufficient Internet access, network problems, and lack of electronic devices. The [TASO rapid evidence review (March 2023)](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf) similarly found that while pre-pandemic literature suggested online learning could maintain outcomes, "the literature produced during the pandemic demonstrates that the rapid shift to an online format had a negative impact on student outcomes," with the review's 11 post-pandemic studies showing an overall negative association.

**Why the emergency transition produced worse outcomes.** The emergency, unprepared nature of the pandemic transition is the primary explanation for the divergence from pre-pandemic findings. Before COVID-19, online courses were typically designed intentionally over weeks or months, with trained instructors, structured interactive elements, and adequate technological infrastructure. During the pandemic, instructors had days or weeks to convert face-to-face courses to online formats, often without pedagogical training, institutional support, or reliable technology. The TASO review explicitly notes that "this planning was not possible with the emergency switch to remote learning, where the priority was to adapt promptly to unforeseen crisis circumstances." The Hechinger Report's synthesis of researcher consensus confirms this: the pandemic "exposed bad practices, such as online-only classes that consisted almost exclusively of very long lectures," and the variety of students learning online "absolutely increased," making it difficult to isolate the effect of instructional modality from pandemic-related confounds such as health anxiety, economic disruption, and caregiving responsibilities. As Di Xu of UC Irvine noted, "the shift to remote instruction due to the pandemic was accompanied by so many other things that changed at the same time. It's really difficult to tease out the impact of instruction."

**Discipline-level variation.** Disciplinary differences in amenability to online delivery are evident across the reviewed studies. Di Pietro (2023) found that learning deficits were larger in math and science than in other subjects, suggesting that disciplines requiring hands-on problem-solving, laboratory work, or symbolic manipulation suffered more in online formats. Kofoed et al.'s (2024) RCT was conducted in introductory economics — a discipline that, while quantitative, can be delivered effectively through structured problem sets and recorded lectures — yet still found significant negative effects. The University of Hong Kong RCT (2023), conducted across seven statistics courses, found no significant difference between face-to-face and synchronous online delivery, suggesting that statistics — another quantitative discipline — may be more amenable to online formats when instruction is synchronous. The TASO review noted that course design, rather than discipline alone, was a critical determinant of outcomes.

**Disproportionate impact on disadvantaged students.** Studies focusing on disadvantaged and low-income students consistently showed systematically worse outcomes. Bird et al. (2022) found that the negative impacts of the shift to online learning were "largest for students with lower GPAs or no prior credit accumulation" — precisely the students most vulnerable to academic disruption. Rodríguez-Planas (2022) documented a socioeconomic divergence: higher-income students' performance improved during online learning while lower-income students' performance declined, suggesting that flexibility and resource access benefited privileged students while disadvantaged students faced compounded barriers. The TASO review explicitly states that "learners from low-income backgrounds and academically at-risk students may be most likely to be negatively impacted by the shift online," and identifies "digital poverty" as "the largest barrier to the success of online teaching and learning." This finding is corroborated by Mathrani et al. (2022), whose digital divide framework across five developing countries documented infrastructure as a primary barrier, and by Harefa (2022), who documented severe communication network challenges in remote Indonesian areas.

**Overall conclusion.** Synthesizing the totality of evidence, online learning during the pandemic was **generally less effective than pre-pandemic in-person instruction for objective performance measures**, but with significant heterogeneity by discipline, student population, and methodology. The pandemic-era meta-analytic estimates (Di Pietro's ~0.19 SD deficit and Betthäuser et al.'s d = −0.14) converge with individual RCT and quasi-experimental findings (Kofoed et al.'s −0.215 SD, Bird et al.'s 4.9 pp completion decrease) to establish a credible central tendency of modest negative effects. However, the distribution of findings across the 17 reviewed studies — 41% ineffective, 35% neutral, 12% effective, 6% mixed — underscores that online learning was not uniformly detrimental. The Geng (2022) quasi-natural experiment, which found academic improvement persisting across 9 semesters, demonstrates that well-structured online formats in supportive institutional contexts can yield positive outcomes even during a pandemic. The critical distinction is between **emergency remote teaching** — unplanned, rapid, and crisis-driven — and **intentionally designed online learning** with adequate infrastructure, pedagogical training, and student support. The pandemic conflated the two, and the resulting evidence base largely reflects the former rather than the latter.

### Cross-Sectional Perceptual Surveys: Student-Reported Effectiveness

The largest category of pandemic-era online learning research consists of cross-sectional surveys that asked students to self-report their satisfaction, perceived learning, or perceived effectiveness of online instruction. These studies typically required students to compare their current online experience against prior face-to-face instruction, capturing subjective perceptions rather than objective learning outcomes such as grades or exam scores. Collectively, they produced a pattern of mixed-to-neutral findings that stands in notable contrast to the more consistently negative results from experimental and quasi-experimental designs.

**Collective findings across perceptual surveys.** The studies in this category converge on a central tendency of moderate satisfaction or qualified acceptance of online learning, but rarely report strong effectiveness. [Simsek (2021)](https://www.asianjde.com/ojs/index.php/AsianJDE/article/view/561) surveyed 13,447 university students at Istanbul University-Cerrahpasa during the fall 2020–2021 semester and found that satisfaction with online education was at a "moderate level," with significant demographic variation: female students reported different satisfaction levels than males, and Science and Engineering students were more satisfied than Medicine and Health Sciences students. [Aldhahi et al. (2022)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-021-10644-7) surveyed 1,226 students across 22 Saudi Arabian universities and found a near-even split: 51% expressed high satisfaction while 49% reported low satisfaction with e-learning. Satisfaction was positively correlated with online learning self-efficacy in time management, technology use, and learning strategies, suggesting that student characteristics—not the modality itself—drove satisfaction ratings. [Roy et al. (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14073) surveyed students and staff at a Gulf University in Bahrain and found that the majority perceived online learning as effective, reflected by an average score of 4 on a 5-point Likert scale, with multiple regression indicating that active learning and institutional support had significant positive effects on perceived effectiveness. [Kedia et al. (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37250210/) found that instructor–student interaction, use of social media, family support, and technical support all had positive relationships with students' learning performance, while collaborative learning and online communication enhanced learning activities. [Younas et al. (2022)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.948061/full) surveyed Pakistani university students and found that e-learning satisfaction was linked to academic achievement, with structural equation modeling confirming that adaptation of online education channels, digital competence, motivation, and willingness all contributed to satisfaction. However, the same study noted that in a developing-country context, device suitability and bandwidth availability remained persistent problems. The [Anhui study (2021)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full), by contrast, documented a clearly negative picture: among 5,558 students in Anhui Province, China, 35.15% reported depression, 36.32% anxiety, and 17.24% stress, with self-directed learning ability negatively associated with these negative emotions—indicating that for a substantial minority, the online learning experience was accompanied by significant psychological distress that colored their overall assessment.

Taken together, these studies cluster around a neutral-to-moderately-positive central tendency. The majority do not report that online learning was *ineffective*; rather, they report that students found it *acceptable but imperfect*, with satisfaction mediated by factors such as self-efficacy, institutional support, instructor interaction, and digital competence. This stands in contrast to the objective performance studies (discussed in S2.3 and S2.4), which more frequently found measurable declines in grades and completion rates.

**Methodological explanations for mixed-to-neutral results.** Three interrelated biases help explain why perceptual surveys produced more favorable assessments of online learning than objective performance measures:

1. **Recall bias and retrospective comparison.** When students are asked to compare online learning with their prior in-person experience, they must reconstruct memories of face-to-face instruction, which are subject to [recall bias](https://catalogofbias.org/biases/recall-bias/)—the tendency to inaccurately remember past experiences. Pre-pandemic classroom experiences may be remembered more favorably than they actually were, particularly because the pandemic itself created a generalized negative affect that colored retrospective evaluations. Students experiencing pandemic-related stress, isolation, and health anxiety may have attributed these negative emotions to the online learning modality rather than to the broader crisis context, producing a contrast effect that made online learning seem worse than it was in objective terms. Conversely, some students may have engaged in defensive rationalization—reporting satisfaction with online learning because acknowledging its inadequacy would have been psychologically threatening during an already stressful period.

2. **Pandemic stress confounding.** The pandemic created a massive confound for perceptual surveys: students were evaluating online learning while simultaneously experiencing lockdowns, health fears, economic disruption, and social isolation. [Anhui (2021)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full) demonstrated that negative emotions were highly prevalent among online learners, with over one-third reporting clinically significant depression and anxiety. These emotional states could independently lower satisfaction ratings regardless of instructional quality. Students who felt isolated, anxious, or depressed were likely to rate the learning experience negatively, not because the online modality was inherently ineffective but because their overall life satisfaction was depressed. This confounding makes it difficult to isolate the effect of online learning itself from the broader pandemic context in perceptual survey designs.

3. **Lack of objective benchmarks.** Perceptual surveys measure *satisfaction* and *perceived effectiveness*, which are not the same as *learning outcomes*. Students may report satisfaction with online learning because it required less effort, offered more flexibility, or reduced commute time—even if their actual learning was inferior. Conversely, students may report dissatisfaction because of technical frustrations or social isolation, even if their academic performance was unaffected. The absence of an objective performance benchmark means that perceptual surveys capture a hybrid construct: part evaluation of instructional quality, part emotional response to the pandemic, and part assessment of convenience and flexibility.

**Do perceptual surveys rate online learning differently from objective studies?** Yes, systematically. Perceptual surveys tend to rate online learning as *more effective* than objective performance studies do. The pattern is clear: Simsek found moderate satisfaction, Aldhahi found a near-even split, Roy found a mean of 4/5, and Kedia identified positive factors—all suggesting that students were, on balance, at least somewhat satisfied. Yet the quasi-experimental and RCT evidence (S2.4) tells a different story: [Bird et al. (2022)](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai20-299.pdf) found a 6.7 percentage-point decrease in course completion at Virginia community colleges, and [Kofoed et al. (2024)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077) found a −0.215 standard deviation drop in final grades at West Point. This divergence is not a contradiction but a reflection of what each methodology measures: perceptual surveys capture subjective experience (which includes convenience, flexibility, and emotional coping), while objective studies capture learning outcomes (which declined during the emergency transition).

**The Xu et al. (2023) meta-analysis: synthesizing satisfaction across 52 studies.** [Xu et al. (2023)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1128034/full) conducted a meta-analysis of 52 studies yielding 57 effect sizes to examine satisfaction with online education among students, faculty, and parents before and after the COVID-19 outbreak. The analysis found that the overall prevalence of student satisfaction with online education was 59.5%—meaning that roughly four in ten students were *not* satisfied. Two moderator analyses are especially relevant to understanding the perceptual survey literature:

- **Pre-pandemic vs. post-pandemic satisfaction.** Student satisfaction was significantly higher in the pre-pandemic era than in the post-pandemic era. Pre-pandemic studies, which typically examined *planned* online courses with intentional instructional design, reported satisfaction rates as high as 88% in some contexts. Post-pandemic studies, which examined *emergency* remote education, produced markedly lower satisfaction. This temporal split indicates that the pandemic context itself—not online learning per se—was a major driver of dissatisfaction.

- **Emergency remote education vs. planned online learning.** Satisfaction was significantly lower in emergency remote learning environments compared to non-emergency (planned) online learning environments. Faculty in non-emergency situations reported almost double the satisfaction rate of their counterparts in emergency circumstances. This distinction is critical: it suggests that when online learning is carefully designed and implemented, students are substantially more satisfied than when it is deployed as a crisis response. The emergency nature of the pandemic transition—characterized by inadequate preparation time, insufficient instructor training, and improvised instructional design—was a primary driver of the lower satisfaction observed in perceptual surveys.

- **Digital infrastructure development.** Countries with developed digital infrastructure had *lower* emergency-learning satisfaction, a finding that may seem counterintuitive but likely reflects higher student expectations in well-resourced contexts. Where infrastructure was adequate, students and faculty expected a higher quality of online instruction, and the emergency transition fell short of those expectations.

The Xu et al. meta-analysis thus provides a systematic framework for understanding why individual perceptual surveys produced mixed results: satisfaction depended heavily on whether the online learning was planned or emergency, whether the study was pre- or post-pandemic, and what infrastructure context obtained. Studies conducted in developing countries with emergency remote education and limited infrastructure (e.g., Younas et al. in Pakistan) tended to report lower satisfaction than studies in better-resourced contexts with some institutional preparation (e.g., Roy et al. in Bahrain, where universities had invested in learning management systems and faculty training).

**Positive findings in perceptual surveys: what distinguishes them.** Among the perceptual surveys reviewed, [Roy et al. (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14073) and [Younas et al. (2022)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.948061/full) reported the most positive findings, with Roy et al. finding a mean effectiveness score of 4/5 and Younas et al. linking e-learning satisfaction to academic achievement. Several features distinguish these studies: Roy et al. examined a context where the university had invested in technology infrastructure and faculty development programs, suggesting that institutional preparedness may have buffered the negative effects of the emergency transition. Younas et al. focused on a developing-country context where the baseline for educational technology was lower, potentially making any online access seem like an improvement over complete educational discontinuity. Both studies also measured *perceived* effectiveness rather than objective performance, and both identified specific mediating factors (active learning, institutional support, digital competence, motivation) that could be targeted to improve outcomes—implying that the positive findings reflected not the inherent superiority of online learning but the presence of enabling conditions that could be replicated in future implementations.

In summary, cross-sectional perceptual surveys consistently produced mixed-to-neutral findings about online learning effectiveness during the pandemic, with satisfaction rates clustering around 50–60% and heavily mediated by institutional preparedness, student self-efficacy, and the emergency-versus-planned nature of the online instruction. These findings are systematically more positive than objective performance studies because perceptual surveys capture convenience, flexibility, and emotional coping alongside instructional quality, and because recall bias, pandemic stress confounding, and the absence of objective benchmarks all inflate satisfaction relative to actual learning outcomes. The Xu et al. (2023) meta-analysis confirms that the emergency context—not online learning itself—was the primary driver of dissatisfaction, with planned online learning in well-resourced pre-pandemic contexts achieving substantially higher satisfaction rates.

### Cross-Sectional Comparative and Longitudinal Studies

Cross-sectional comparative and longitudinal designs occupy a methodological tier above perceptual surveys because they employ objective outcome measures—course grades, completion rates, credit accumulation—rather than self-reported satisfaction. However, these designs still face significant threats to validity, chiefly selection bias and pandemic-related confounds, that complicate causal inference about online learning's effectiveness.

#### Cross-Sectional Comparative Studies

The [BMC Medical Education study (2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z) employed a quasi-experimental design at a U.S. dental school, comparing 482 pre-doctoral students' course grades in Summer 2020 (online instruction) against the same courses taught face-to-face in Summer 2019. Using Chi Square tests across 17 courses, the online cohort was equally or more likely to receive an A grade in 16 of 17 courses—a finding classified as neutral/effective. Notably, 80% of students expressed a desire to retain some online instruction post-pandemic. However, this design compared different cohorts across semesters rather than concurrently, introducing potential cohort effects and limiting causal claims about the modality itself.

The [Koja (2024)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/hsr2.1946) study, conducted at Alexander Xhuvani University in Albania with 350 students, is classified in the ReportSpec as cross-sectional comparative, but is in fact a cross-sectional perceptual survey. It found that 77.4% of students held a negative perception of e-learning, 70% reported unmet expectations, and 57.4% encountered internet difficulties. While this study does not directly compare online and face-to-face performance, its findings illustrate how comparative studies in developing-country contexts tend to reveal infrastructure-mediated effectiveness barriers rather than modality effects per se.

#### Longitudinal Studies

[Rodríguez-Planas (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8786604/) used an unbalanced panel of over 11,000 academic records from 2,817 students at Queens College (CUNY), spanning Spring 2017 to Spring 2020. Employing difference-in-differences models with student fixed effects, the study uncovered sharply heterogeneous effects by socioeconomic status. Lower-income students in the bottom quartile of the Fall 2019 GPA distribution outperformed their higher-income peers with a 9% higher Spring 2020 GPA relative to the counterfactual pre-pandemic mean. However, this advantage was entirely explained by differential use of the flexible grading policy: lower-income students were 35% more likely to exercise the pass/fail option. Without this policy, top-performing lower-income students would have seen their GPA decline by 5% relative to their pre-pandemic mean, with suggestive evidence that these students experienced greater challenges with online learning and were more concerned about maintaining merit-based financial aid. This finding demonstrates that observed performance effects cannot be cleanly attributed to online learning alone—institutional policy responses and socioeconomic circumstances interact with modality to produce outcomes.

The [OLJ (2024)](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/3680) study compared Fall 2019 (in-person) and Fall 2020 (online) academic performance for newly-struggling students at a Midwestern U.S. high school, finding a significant relationship between low-income status and increased D/F grades. While this study provides contextual evidence for the pandemic's differential impact on disadvantaged students, it is a K-12 study rather than higher education, and thus its findings are indicative rather than directly applicable to the higher-education context under review.

#### Quasi-Experimental Studies

[Bird et al. (2022)](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai20-299.pdf) used two complementary difference-in-differences frameworks to estimate the impact of the abrupt shift to virtual instruction across Virginia's community college system (VCCS), which enrolls approximately 250,000 students annually. Using an instructor-by-course fixed-effects model, they estimated a 4.9 percentage point decrease in course completion—a 6.1% decline from the pre-COVID baseline of 80.7%—driven by increases in both course withdrawals (2.7 pp) and failures (1.3 pp). The negative impacts were largest for students with lower GPAs or no prior credit accumulation. In exploratory analyses, however, Bird et al. found minimal long-term impacts: students affected by the Spring 2020 shift were only 1.1 percentage points less likely to re-enroll the following year and earned 0.58 fewer credits (4.7% decline), suggesting that near-term performance reductions did not cascade into substantial persistence losses.

The quasi-natural experiment by Geng (2022), which found academic improvement during online learning that persisted into subsequent semesters, and the Altindag & Filiz (2021) fixed-effects study, which found a declining face-to-face advantage over time, are discussed in detail in §S2.4.

#### Selection Bias and Pandemic Confounds

A fundamental threat to comparative studies is selection bias: students who choose online courses differ systematically from those who choose in-person instruction in ways correlated with academic performance, including motivation, prior achievement, and learning styles. Bird et al. (2022) addressed this through instructor-by-course and student fixed effects, yet noted that parallel trends assumptions could not be fully verified—for instance, students who started Spring 2020 in person had more prior online experience than those who started online, potentially biasing estimates. Altindag & Filiz (2021) similarly used multi-level fixed effects but acknowledged that unobserved heterogeneity could persist.

Longitudinal designs face a different and arguably more intractable threat: pandemic-related confounds. When comparing Fall 2019 to Fall 2020, researchers cannot disentangle the effect of online learning from the simultaneous onset of health anxiety, job loss, caregiving responsibilities, and social isolation. [Aucejo et al. (2020)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7451187/) documented that approximately 50% of surveyed students at Arizona State University reported decreased academic performance due to COVID-19, with lower-income students 55% more likely to delay graduation. Economic and health shocks explained 40% of the delayed-graduation gap between income groups. Rodríguez-Planas (2022) explicitly noted that at Queens College, where online teaching was "practically non-existent prior to the pandemic," estimates captured both the switch to online teaching and other pandemic disruptions simultaneously. This confound is arguably the most significant limitation of pandemic-era longitudinal research: observed performance declines may reflect the cumulative psychological, economic, and social toll of the pandemic rather than the instructional modality per se.

#### Attribution: Online Learning vs. Pandemic Context

The question of how much observed performance decline is attributable to online learning itself versus the broader pandemic context remains contested. Several pieces of evidence illuminate this attribution problem:

First, the Geng (2022) finding of academic improvement during online learning in China—where strict lockdowns may have reduced commuting and other distractions—suggests that the pandemic context (not just the modality) drove negative outcomes in other settings where students faced greater economic and health instability (see §S2.4 for full study details).

Second, the [BMC (2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z) finding of equivalent or better performance in dental education suggests that when courses are reasonably well-designed and students have adequate institutional support, online learning need not produce deficits even during a pandemic.

Third, Rodríguez-Planas (2022) demonstrated that the flexible grading policy—not the online modality per se—explained the GPA advantage of lower-income students, suggesting that pandemic-era policy responses may have masked or amplified modality effects in ways that obscure clean attribution.

Finally, the quasi-experimental designs of Bird et al. (2022) and Altindag & Filiz (2021), which control for student characteristics through fixed effects, both found negative effects of online instruction on objective outcomes, providing the strongest evidence that modality itself—not just pandemic confounds—contributed to performance declines. Yet the modest magnitude of these effects (4.9 pp decrease in completion; smaller post-pandemic differences in grades) suggests that the pandemic context amplified what would otherwise have been a smaller modality disadvantage.

In sum, cross-sectional comparative and longitudinal studies present a more complex and methodologically rigorous picture than perceptual surveys. Quasi-experimental studies generally found modest negative effects on objective outcomes, but the quasi-natural experiment by Geng (2022) demonstrates that online learning during the pandemic was not universally detrimental. The divergence between these findings underscores the importance of context—institutional support, course design quality, student population characteristics, and pandemic severity—in determining whether online learning succeeded or failed, and it cautions against attributing observed performance changes solely to the instructional modality without accounting for the extraordinary confounds of the pandemic period.

### Randomized Controlled Trials and Quasi-Experimental Studies

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental designs represent the strongest causal evidence in the online learning effectiveness literature. Unlike perceptual surveys, which ask students to self-assess their learning, or cross-sectional comparative studies, which compare different students across modalities, RCTs randomly assign participants to online or in-person conditions, thereby eliminating selection bias—the tendency for students who choose online courses to differ systematically from those who choose in-person. Quasi-experimental studies exploit natural experiments or use fixed-effects and difference-in-differences strategies to approximate causal inference when randomization is not feasible. The pre-pandemic meta-analysis by [Means et al. (2013)](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/effectiveness_of_online_and_blended_learning.pdf) synthesized 45 experimental and quasi-experimental studies (50 effect sizes) and found that students in purely online conditions performed no differently from those in face-to-face instruction, while blended learning yielded a small but significant advantage over face-to-face. This pre-pandemic benchmark provides the methodological baseline against which pandemic-era experimental evidence must be judged.

**The Kofoed et al. (2024) RCT at West Point.** The most rigorous pandemic-era RCT was conducted by [Kofoed, Gebhart, Gilmore, and Moschitto (2024)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077) in the *American Economic Review: Insights*. In Fall 2020, they randomized 551 West Point cadets enrolled in a required introductory economics course across 12 instructors to either online or in-person instruction. The random assignment ensured that student characteristics were balanced across modalities, addressing the selection bias that plagues observational comparisons. Final grades for online students dropped by 0.215 standard deviations—a statistically significant decline apparent in both assignments and exams. The negative effects were largest for academically at-risk students, a finding consistent with the broader pattern that online learning disproportionately disadvantages students with weaker academic preparation. A post-course survey revealed that online students struggled to concentrate during class and felt less connected to their instructors and peers, providing a perceptual correlate to the objective performance decline. Notably, the authors identified a grade-inflation caveat: pandemic-era grading policies—including "no detriment" provisions and flexible withdrawal options—may have inflated grades in online sections, meaning the observed −0.215 SD estimate could represent a lower bound on the true negative effect of emergency online learning on actual learning. If online sections experienced grade inflation that partially masked performance declines, the genuine learning deficit would be even larger than measured.

**The University of Hong Kong RCT.** In contrast to the West Point results, a large-scale RCT conducted at the University of Hong Kong by [Cheung, Lam, Zhang, Kwan, Wat, Zhang, Zhu, Chung, and Yin (2023)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1160430/full), published in *Frontiers in Education*, found no statistically significant difference between face-to-face and synchronous online teaching. The study randomized 725 students (560 undergraduate, 165 postgraduate) across seven statistics courses offered by the Department of Statistics and Actuarial Science during the first semester of 2020–2021. Stratified randomization was used with course code as the stratification factor, and assignment ratios varied (1:1, 1:2, or 1:3) depending on classroom COVID-19 capacity restrictions. The online instruction was synchronous—students received identical live-streamed lectures and tutorials via Zoom—making this a test of synchronous online delivery rather than asynchronous self-paced learning. The only systematic difference between the two groups was the mode of physical presence. The study also identified class size as an effect modifier: face-to-face students in classes of 25 or fewer had significantly higher final weighted and final exam scores, suggesting that the benefits of in-person instruction may be concentrated in small-group settings. The Pass/Fail grading option, available to students during the pandemic, had a significantly negative effect on course performance, reinforcing the importance of grading-policy confounds in pandemic-era research.

**The Geng (2022) Quasi-Natural Experiment.** Providing a striking counterpoint to studies finding negative effects, [Geng (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070), published in *Sustainability* (MDPI), used a quasi-natural experiment design leveraging the COVID-19 pandemic as an exogenous shock. The study analyzed 123,208 course records from 2,622 undergraduates across nine semesters (classes of 2017–2021) at a single Chinese university in Anhui Province. The pandemic created a natural experiment: the second semester of 2019–2020 was delivered entirely online, while surrounding semesters were face-to-face. Using ANOVA and multiple linear regression (with non-parametric tests where the homogeneity-of-variance assumption was violated), Geng found that students' academic records improved substantially during the online semester, with lower fluctuation and greater stability. The improvement was more pronounced for sophomores and juniors than for freshmen. Critically, the academic gains persisted into the following one or two semesters after students returned to face-to-face instruction, suggesting that well-structured emergency online learning may have produced lasting benefits rather than temporary disruption. The study explicitly uses the term "emergency remote education" to distinguish the pandemic-era online format from planned online learning, and the author notes that the large-scale, longitudinal, transcript-level data address limitations of the small-sample, self-reported survey designs that dominate the literature. The sustained improvement may reflect the benefits of flexible pacing, recorded lectures for review, and structured online engagement—though the study cannot fully separate the effect of the online modality from pandemic-specific factors such as stricter stay-at-home orders that may have increased study time.

**The Altindag, Filiz, and Tekin (2021) Quasi-Experimental Study.** [Altindag, Filiz, and Tekin (2021)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29113/w29113.pdf) (NBER Working Paper No. 29113) used 12 semesters of transcript-level panel data (Fall 2016–Spring 2022) from a medium-sized public R1 university, with seven pre-pandemic and five post-pandemic semesters. The analysis employed student, instructor, and course fixed effects to control for endogenous sorting into online versus face-to-face modalities. The findings indicated that face-to-face instruction produced better student performance: higher grades, lower withdrawal rates, lower course repetition, and higher graduation GPAs. However, the face-to-face advantage followed a declining trend over time, and the differences between modalities were smaller in the post-pandemic period, suggesting that either online instruction improved or students and instructors adapted. The results were consistent across student and instructor characteristics except for Honors and graduate students, where the face-to-face advantage was smaller or statistically insignificant—indicating that more motivated and skilled students were better able to succeed in online formats. This study is distinct from Bird et al. (2022), which analyzed the Virginia Community College System using difference-in-differences frameworks and found a 4.9 percentage point decrease in course completion (a 6.1% decline relative to the pre-pandemic baseline of 80.7%). The two studies are independent, conducted at different institutions with different methodologies, and both contribute quasi-experimental evidence of negative pandemic-era effects.

**Why Experimental Findings Differ from Perceptual Surveys.** The divergence between RCT/quasi-experimental results and cross-sectional perceptual surveys stems from several methodological factors. First, RCTs measure objective outcomes—final grades, exam scores, course completion—rather than subjective perceptions, which are susceptible to recall bias, contrast effects, and the attribution of pandemic-related stress to the learning modality itself. Second, random assignment eliminates selection bias: in perceptual surveys, students who report negative experiences may have self-selected into online courses or may have pre-existing characteristics that color their assessments. Third, the emergency context of the pandemic introduced confounds that surveys cannot disentangle—students reporting dissatisfaction with online learning may have been responding to isolation, health anxiety, or economic disruption rather than to the instructional medium per se. The Kofoed et al. post-course survey illustrates this tension: even in an RCT where objective grades declined by 0.215 SD, the survey responses about concentration difficulties and disconnection provide perceptual confirmation of the objective findings, but the magnitude of the perceptual response cannot be cleanly attributed to the learning modality alone.

**Limitations of Pandemic-Era Experimental Evidence.** Despite their superior causal credibility, pandemic-era RCTs and quasi-experimental studies face important limitations. The Kofoed et al. RCT was conducted in a single course (introductory economics) at a single highly selective military academy, where students are subject to rigid daily schedules and in-person attendance requirements that differ dramatically from typical university settings. The HKU RCT was confined to statistics courses within one department, limiting generalizability to disciplines with different pedagogical demands (e.g., laboratory sciences, performing arts). The Geng (2022) quasi-natural experiment, while large-scale, was conducted at a single Chinese university during a period of strict government-mandated lockdowns; the academic improvement observed may reflect increased study time during stay-at-home orders rather than inherent advantages of online delivery. The Altindag et al. study, though spanning multiple semesters and courses, comes from a single institution with a well-established pre-pandemic online program, which may have moderated the negative effects of the emergency transition. Additionally, the short duration of most RCTs (a single semester) limits the ability to capture adaptation effects; the Altindag et al. finding that the face-to-face advantage declined over time suggests that longer-run outcomes may differ from short-run experimental estimates. Finally, the emergency context itself—characterized by health anxiety, economic disruption, and caregiving burdens—means that even the best-designed pandemic-era RCTs estimate the effect of *emergency* online learning under crisis conditions, not the effect of well-designed online instruction under normal circumstances. This distinction, explicitly drawn by both Geng (2022) and Xu et al. (2023), is essential for interpreting the experimental evidence and explains why pandemic-era RCTs sometimes find more negative effects than the pre-pandemic meta-analytic literature, which largely studied planned online courses.

### Cross-Methodology Synthesis: How Design Influenced Results

A striking feature of the pandemic-era literature on online learning effectiveness is the absence of consensus. Meng et al. (2024), in their systematic review of 25 articles, concluded that "past studies failed to achieve a consensus over online learning effectiveness and research results are largely [determined] by how learning effectiveness was assessed, e.g., self-reported online learning effectiveness, longitudinal comparison, and RCT" ([Meng et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1334153/full)). This subsection argues that research design is not merely a technical detail but the primary driver of the apparent disagreement across the literature. By systematically comparing the four design categories—perceptual surveys, cross-sectional comparative studies, longitudinal studies, and randomized controlled trials (RCTs)—a coherent pattern emerges: each design faces a distinct profile of biases that systematically shapes its reported effectiveness findings.

#### The Four Design Categories and Their Bias Profiles

**Category 1: Cross-sectional perceptual surveys.** Studies in this category—including Younas et al. (2022), Aldhahi et al. (2022), Simsek (2021), Roy et al. (2022), and Kedia et al. (2023)—asked students to self-report their satisfaction with or perceived effectiveness of online learning, typically by comparing it with their prior in-person experiences. These studies collectively produced mixed-to-neutral findings. The methodological reasons are multiple. First, **recall bias** distorts retrospective comparisons: students asked to evaluate online learning against past in-person instruction may misremember or idealize their pre-pandemic experiences, a problem Geng (2022) explicitly identified in noting that "the vast majority of research data were obtained through questionnaires" and that "even the data of students' scores were also self-reported, making them highly subjective" ([Geng, 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070)). Second, **contrast effects** amplify perceived differences: the abrupt, total shift from campus life to remote learning created a stark experiential contrast that students may attribute to the modality itself rather than to the crisis context. Third, **pandemic stress attribution** confounds self-reports: students experiencing anxiety, isolation, and health fears during the pandemic may attribute these negative emotions to the online learning mode rather than to the broader crisis. The Anhui study (2021), which found a 35.15% depression rate and 36.32% anxiety rate among 5,558 students, illustrates how pervasive negative emotions were during the period when perceptual surveys were administered ([Anhui, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full)). Because these studies capture subjective perception rather than objective learning outcomes, they tend to cluster in the neutral range—neither strongly affirming nor strongly condemning online learning.

**Category 2: Cross-sectional comparative studies.** Studies such as the BMC Medical Education (2021) comparative study and Koja (2024) directly compared student performance in online versus face-to-face courses during the same semester. These studies tend to find online learning slightly worse than in-person, though with notable exceptions (BMC found equivalent or better performance). The key bias here is **selection bias**: students who enroll in online courses may differ systematically from those who enroll in in-person courses in motivation, self-regulation, prior academic achievement, or socioeconomic status. The TASO review (2023) noted that pre-pandemic studies suffered from this problem, as "students enrolled in online courses were often not comparable to those" in face-to-face courses ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)). During the pandemic, when the shift to online was often mandatory, selection bias was reduced but not eliminated—some students still had choices about course mode, and institutional policies varied. Additionally, **pandemic confounds**—health stress, economic disruption, caregiving responsibilities—affected online and in-person students differently and could not be fully controlled in cross-sectional designs.

**Category 3: Longitudinal studies.** Studies such as OLJ (2024), which compared Fall 2019 (in-person) to Fall 2020 (online), and Rodríguez-Planas (2022), which tracked GPA and credits over time, produced mixed results. OLJ found a performance decrease, while Rodríguez-Planas found that higher-income students' performance improved while lower-income students' performance declined. The dominant bias in longitudinal designs is **pandemic stress confounding**: comparing the same cohort before and after the pandemic switch means that the pandemic itself—a massive, multi-dimensional shock—is an inseparable confound. As the Hechinger Report summarized researcher Cassandra Hart's caution: "You have all of these factors going on with the pandemic affecting everything from personal health to family members' health to employment" ([Hechinger Report, 2022](https://hechingerreport.org/what-researchers-learned-about-online-higher-education-during-the-pandemic/)). Di Xu similarly noted that "the shift to remote instruction due to the pandemic was accompanied by so many other things that changed at the same time. It's really difficult to tease out the impact of instruction" ([Hechinger Report, 2022](https://hechingerreport.org/what-researchers-learned-about-online-higher-education-during-the-pandemic/)). Socioeconomic moderators further complicate the picture: Rodríguez-Planas (2022) demonstrated that income level moderated the effect, with disadvantaged students declining more steeply.

**Category 4: RCTs and quasi-experimental studies.** The strongest causal designs include Kofoed et al. (2024), who randomized 551 West Point students to online or in-person sections of introductory economics, finding that online students' final grades dropped by 0.215 SD; the University of Hong Kong RCT (2023), which randomized 725 students across 7 statistics courses and found no significant difference; and Geng (2022), whose quasi-natural experiment using 123,208 course records across 9 semesters at a Chinese university found academic improvement during the online semester that persisted into following semesters. These designs address selection bias through random assignment or natural experiment conditions, producing the most trustworthy effectiveness estimates. However, they face their own limitations. First, **grade inflation** during the pandemic—when many institutions adopted pass/fail options and lenient grading—could mask true performance differences; Kofoed et al. (2024) specifically controlled for this by using standardized grading software, noting that "because instructors had no discretion over grades, we eliminate any potential differences in grading between modalities" ([Kofoed et al., 2024](https://www.educationnext.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/ednext_XXIV_4_kofoed_etal.pdf)). Second, **limited generalizability**: Kofoed et al. (2024) acknowledged that West Point students are "among the nation's most disciplined young people," and that their online sections were "identical except for their setting, so students did not experience some unique attributes of some online courses, such as self-paced study, that could positively affect outcomes" ([Kofoed et al., 2024](https://www.educationnext.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/ednext_XXIV_4_kofoed_etal.pdf)). Third, **short duration and emergency context**: most pandemic-era RCTs were conducted in a single semester under crisis conditions, limiting the ability to capture adaptation effects.

#### Why Perceptual Surveys Produce Different Results from RCTs

The divergence between perceptual surveys and RCTs is systematic, not coincidental. Perceptual surveys ask students to compare online learning with their memories of in-person instruction, a process subject to recall bias, contrast effects, and pandemic stress attribution. Students experiencing isolation and anxiety during lockdowns may attribute their distress to the learning mode itself. RCTs, by contrast, measure objective outcomes (grades, test scores, completion rates) rather than subjective perceptions, and they eliminate selection bias through random assignment. When Kofoed et al. (2024) found a −0.215 SD effect on final grades but also found in their post-course survey that online students "struggled to concentrate in class and felt less connected to their instructors and peers" ([Kofoed et al., 2024](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077)), the objective performance measure and the subjective experience measure told complementary but distinct stories: the survey captured psychosocial dimensions that the grade analysis could not, while the grade analysis provided an objective anchor that the survey alone could not offer.

#### The Emergency Remote Teaching versus Planned Online Learning Distinction

A critical conceptual distinction explains much of the divergence across studies: the difference between **emergency remote teaching (ERT)** and **planned online learning**. This distinction was formally articulated by Hodges et al. (2020) in EDUCAUSE Review, who defined emergency remote teaching as "a temporary shift of instructional delivery to an alternate delivery mode due to crisis circumstances," in contrast to online learning that is "planned from the beginning and designed to be online" with typical development timelines of "six to nine months" ([Hodges et al., 2020](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2020/3/the-difference-between-emergency-remote-teaching-and-online-learning)). Both Geng (2022) and Xu et al. (2023) explicitly adopted and operationalized this distinction. Geng (2022) framed the entire study as an analysis of "emergency remote education (teaching and learning)" rather than online education per se, and noted that the pandemic created a quasi-experiment with "the characteristics of an 'emergency' situation" ([Geng, 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070)). Xu et al. (2023) provided a meta-analytic confirmation of the distinction's empirical relevance: they found that "a significantly higher number of students in the pre-pandemic era in countries with developed digital infrastructure and emergency online learning environments were less satisfied with online education than their counterparts in the post-pandemic era, in countries with developing digital infrastructure, and in non-emergency online learning environments," and that "the faculty in the non-emergency situation reported almost double the satisfaction rate of their counterparts in the emergency circumstance" ([Xu et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1128034/full)).

This distinction has profound implications for interpreting the literature. Studies finding negative effects—such as Kofoed et al. (2024), Bird et al. (2022), and Altindag & Filiz (2021)—largely evaluated emergency remote teaching, where courses were transferred online within days or weeks without intentional instructional design. Studies finding neutral or positive effects—such as Geng (2022), which found sustained academic improvement during and after the online semester, and the University of Hong Kong RCT, which found no significant difference—may reflect contexts where the online delivery was better structured or where the institutional context mitigated the emergency character of the transition. The TASO review (2023) explicitly noted this mechanism: "this planning was not possible with the emergency switch to remote learning, where the priority was to adapt promptly to unforeseen crisis circumstances" ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)). The emergency-versus-planned distinction thus explains why pandemic-era studies tend to report worse outcomes than pre-pandemic meta-analyses such as Means et al. (2013), which synthesized 45 studies of predominantly planned online or blended courses and found no significant disadvantage to purely online versus face-to-face instruction ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)).

#### Is There a "Best" Methodology for Assessing Effectiveness During a Pandemic?

No single methodology is without limitations in the pandemic context, but the evidence hierarchy clearly favors RCTs and quasi-experimental designs for causal inference. The TASO review (2023) classified evidence using the Office for Students' standards, distinguishing "Narrative" (Type 1), "Empirical Enquiry" (Type 2), and "Causality" (Type 3) evidence, and noted that the post-pandemic literature included only 5 causal studies versus 6 empirical studies, with the pre-pandemic literature containing 6 causal studies and only 1 empirical study ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)). The scarcity of high-quality causal evidence during the pandemic underscores the need for more RCTs and quasi-experimental studies. However, RCTs alone are insufficient: they typically measure short-term, single-course outcomes and may miss broader psychosocial and longitudinal effects. The most defensible approach is **triangulation**—converging evidence from multiple designs, each compensating for the others' biases. Perceptual surveys capture student experience and satisfaction dimensions that objective measures miss; longitudinal studies reveal temporal dynamics; cross-sectional comparative studies provide breadth; and RCTs provide causal anchors.

#### The Most Defensible Overall Conclusion

Given the methodological heterogeneity, the most defensible conclusion is that **online learning during the pandemic was generally less effective than pre-pandemic in-person instruction for objective performance measures, but with significant heterogeneity by discipline, student population, methodology, and the degree to which the online delivery was planned versus emergency-improvised.** The systematic pattern across design categories reveals that the apparent disagreement in the literature is largely an artifact of methodology: perceptual surveys, burdened by recall bias and pandemic stress attribution, produce mixed-to-neutral findings; cross-sectional comparative studies, plagued by selection bias and pandemic confounds, tend to find online slightly worse; longitudinal studies, confounded by the pandemic shock itself, are mixed; and RCTs, the most rigorous designs, tend to find no significant difference or modest negative effects on objective measures. The emergency-versus-planned distinction cuts across all four categories: studies evaluating well-structured online delivery in contexts with adequate infrastructure and student preparation tend to find neutral or positive effects, while studies evaluating emergency remote teaching under crisis conditions tend to find negative effects. This means that the pandemic-era evidence should not be interpreted as a verdict on online learning as a modality, but rather as a verdict on the specific conditions under which it was implemented—conditions that the TASO review characterized as defined by "digital poverty" as "the largest barrier" ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)) and by the absence of the "six to nine months" of planning that effective online course design requires ([Hodges et al., 2020](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2020/3/the-difference-between-emergency-remote-teaching-and-online-learning)).

## Analysis of Influencing Factors

### Infrastructure: Digital Access, Connectivity, and the Digital Divide

Technological infrastructure — encompassing internet access, device availability, electricity reliability, platform stability, and digital literacy — was a foundational determinant of whether online learning could function at all during the COVID-19 pandemic. The mechanism operates through a causal chain: inadequate infrastructure creates barriers to access, which disrupts synchronous sessions, prevents retrieval of learning materials, increases student frustration, reduces participation quality, and ultimately widens achievement gaps between privileged and disadvantaged students. Without adequate infrastructure, no amount of instructional design or pedagogical innovation can compensate, making it a prerequisite for all other effectiveness factors to matter.

**Hardware and connectivity barriers in developing countries.** The most severe infrastructure constraints were documented in developing-country contexts. Mathrani et al. (2022) surveyed male and female students across five developing countries — India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Afghanistan — and developed a digital divide framework encompassing structural, cultural, and agency dimensions. They found that structural issues stemmed from a lack of digital media access and supporting services, with female students disproportionately placed lower on the digital divide access scale due to gendered discriminatory norms, household responsibilities, and limited device ownership. The study reported that large segments of the population in these economies had "limited access to basic digital services," which directly restricted how online learning could be used during lockdowns ([Mathrani et al., 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14767724.2021.1981253)). Similarly, Jamil and Muschert (2023) documented severe infrastructure constraints in Pakistani universities during the shift to e-learning, finding that the digital divide worsened pre-existing educational disparities. They highlighted the urgent need for affordable devices, free digital literacy programs, and expansion of internet infrastructure to enable meaningful online learning participation ([Jamil & Muschert, 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/00027642231156779)). A systematic review by Ndibalema (2022) of higher education institutions in developing countries corroborated these findings, identifying digital inequalities, lack of reliable internet access, low technological competence among both instructors and students, and limited availability of digital solutions as pervasive barriers. In Africa, only 28% of urban households had home internet access in 2019, compared to 6.3% in rural areas — a 4.5-fold urban-rural divide that directly translated into unequal online learning participation ([Ndibalema, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574540/)).

**Rural and remote area infrastructure failures.** Harefa (2022) documented the experience of 30 students in remote areas of North Tapanuli, Indonesia, finding that network connection disturbances in rural areas directly affected students' ability to attend online learning sessions. Students reported that unstable internet connections made it impossible to participate consistently in synchronous classes, and the lack of adequate devices further compounded participation barriers. The study found that student performance decreased compared to face-to-face learning, with some students abandoning their education entirely — outcomes directly attributable to infrastructure rather than pedagogy ([Harefa, 2022](https://f1000research.com/articles/10-867)). Meng et al. (2024) similarly identified infrastructure challenges as particularly acute in developing countries, as detailed in S1.1 ([Meng et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1334153/full)).

**The digital divide as a gap-widening mechanism.** The digital divide did not merely create inconvenience — it systematically widened achievement gaps between high-income and low-income students. Roy et al. (2022), studying perceived effectiveness of online learning in India, identified "resistance to change, lack of IT infrastructure and resources, [and] digital divide" as critical factors negatively influencing effectiveness. Their regression analysis showed that institutional support encompassing IT infrastructure, IT support and services, and faculty development programs explained a significant proportion of the variance in perceived effectiveness ([Roy et al., 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14073)). Rodríguez-Planas (2022) provided complementary longitudinal evidence from Queens College (CUNY), finding that infrastructure barriers interacted with socioeconomic status to produce measurable performance declines among lower-income students — a finding discussed in detail in S2.3 ([Rodríguez-Planas, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8786604/)).

**"Digital poverty as the largest barrier."** The TASO rapid evidence review (March 2023) concluded that digital poverty constituted the primary gatekeeper of online learning effectiveness, disproportionately impacting disadvantaged groups (see S1.1 for the full assessment and supporting quotation). This framing positions infrastructure not as one factor among many but as the foundational barrier: without reliable connectivity and adequate devices, other factors — instructional design, social interaction, flexibility — become moot for the affected students ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)).

**Platform stability and digital literacy as interacting factors.** Infrastructure barriers extended beyond hardware and connectivity to encompass software/platform usability and digital literacy. Ndibalema (2022) found that both instructors and students in developing countries lacked technological competence, and that many higher education institutions "did not even struggle to engage their learners in online distance learning as there were no digital systems to accommodate the learning needs" — meaning teaching was entirely suspended in some contexts. Learning management systems, video conferencing platforms, and digital assessment tools required a baseline of digital literacy that many students and instructors, particularly in developing and rural contexts, did not possess. This created a compounding effect: even when devices and internet were available, the inability to navigate platforms effectively reduced participation quality and learning outcomes. Roy et al. (2022) similarly identified learning platform usability and digital skill as factors interacting with IT infrastructure to determine effectiveness, noting that students needed both the hardware and the competence to use it productively ([Roy et al., 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14073); [Ndibalema, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574540/)).

In summary, infrastructure operated as a threshold factor: adequate internet access, device availability, electricity reliability, platform stability, and digital literacy were necessary preconditions for online learning to function at all. Where infrastructure was lacking — disproportionately affecting low-income, rural, and developing-country students — online learning was not merely less effective but often impossible, producing the most severe and inequitable outcomes documented across the pandemic literature. The evidence from Mathrani et al. (2022), Harefa (2022), Jamil and Muschert (2023), Ndibalema (2022), and the TASO (2023) review collectively demonstrates that infrastructure barriers were the most fundamental and least remediable obstacle to online learning effectiveness during the pandemic, creating a digital divide that widened pre-existing socioeconomic achievement gaps rather than merely reflecting them.

### Instructional Design: Course Structure, Engagement Strategies, and Assessment Alignment

Instructional design quality — encompassing course structure, active learning integration, assessment alignment, instructor training, and pedagogical adaptation to online formats — was a decisive mediator of online learning effectiveness during the COVID-19 pandemic. The central mechanism is straightforward: well-designed online courses with clear navigation, interactive elements, formative assessment, and visible instructor presence promote engagement and deeper learning, while poorly designed courses — particularly those that merely transferred lectures to Zoom or posted recorded videos without pedagogical redesign — led to fatigue, disengagement, and diminished performance. The pandemic's emergency context, however, severely constrained the capacity for such design, producing a natural experiment in which the *absence* of intentional instructional design was itself a causal factor in negative outcomes.

**The Emergency-Design Gap.** The TASO rapid evidence review (March 2023) identified this gap as a structural feature of the pandemic transition: "Course design appears to be an important factor to consider when planning online learning, as its efficacy is highly dependent on a number of design choices. However, this planning was not possible with the emergency switch to remote learning, where the priority was to adapt promptly to unforeseen crisis circumstances" ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)). The review explicitly recommended that "a concerted effort should be made to design online courses rather than simply moving face-to-face materials into the online environment," underscoring that the dominant pandemic practice — rapid content migration rather than pedagogical redesign — was itself a design failure. Meng et al.'s (2024) systematic review of 25 studies corroborated this, identifying "instructors' lack of pedagogic and technical skills, and insufficient engagement" as salient instructional factors undermining effectiveness, and noting that "the sudden and unprepared transition to wholly online learning caused by the pandemic posed formidable challenges to higher education stakeholders, especially at the early stage of the pandemic" ([Meng et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1334153/full)). The systematic review listed "instructional factors" as one of the six principal categories of influence on online learning effectiveness, confirming that design quality was not a peripheral concern but a core determinant.

**Specific Design Features That Improved Effectiveness.** The TASO review distilled three evidence-based design features from the literature: (1) coordinated student-to-student interaction via discussion boards and chat rooms, (2) feedback between teaching staff and students, and (3) appropriate frequency and timing of online teaching and assessment to avoid student fatigue ([TASO, 2023](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023-03-22_Evidence-review_Online-teaching-and-learning-in-COVID-19_TASO.pdf)). These features operate through distinct mechanisms: student-to-peer interaction sustains collaborative knowledge construction and mitigates the isolation discussed in S3.3; instructor feedback provides formative guidance that substitutes for the real-time cues lost in the transition from physical classrooms; and appropriate pacing prevents the cognitive overload that occurs when asynchronous content is released without structured sequencing. Kedia et al. (2023), studying 300 undergraduate and postgraduate students across three Indian universities using structural equation modeling, found that "Instructor-Student Interaction, Use of social media, Family as well as Technical support have a positive relationship with Students' Learning Performance through Students Engagement," while peer interaction had only a "minor influence on learners' performance" ([Kedia et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37250210/)). The finding that instructor-student interaction was the strongest design-related predictor of performance — stronger than peer interaction — suggests that instructor presence functions as a compensatory mechanism for the loss of classroom immediacy, and that its absence in emergency remote courses was particularly damaging.

**Active Learning and Self-Regulated Learning.** The mechanism through which active learning strategies affect engagement operates via cognitive load and self-regulation. Well-designed online courses that incorporate interactive elements (quizzes, discussion prompts, group projects, scaffolded assignments) require students to actively process information rather than passively consume recorded lectures, which deepens encoding and retention. However, this design benefit is contingent on students' self-regulated learning (SDL) ability. The Anhui/Frontiers in Psychology (2021) cross-sectional study of 5,558 students found that "SDL ability was negatively associated with negative emotions of the college students during this period of online learning" — specifically, students with higher self-management ability, information capability, and cooperative learning ability showed significantly lower odds of depression, anxiety, and stress ([Anhui, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full)). This finding has a dual implication for instructional design: first, that courses incorporating SDL-supportive structures (clear learning objectives, progress tracking, self-assessment tools) can buffer the emotional toll of online learning; second, that the absence of such structures disproportionately harms students with weaker self-regulation. Stan et al. (2022), studying predictors of learning engagement during the pandemic, similarly found that self-regulated learning strategies were significant predictors of engagement in online environments ([Stan et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9100394/)). The broader literature on procrastination reinforces this: Hong et al. (2021) demonstrated that procrastination was negatively related to six sub-constructs of self-regulated online learning — task strategy, mood adjustment, self-evaluation, environment structuring, time management, and help-seeking — and that these deficits predicted online learning ineffectiveness during the coronavirus lockdown ([Hong et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7846229/)). Together, these studies indicate that instructional design must either build in external scaffolding for students with weaker SDL or explicitly train self-regulation skills; without it, the flexibility of online formats becomes a liability rather than an asset.

**Sustained Academic Improvement: The Geng (2022) Evidence.** The most striking counterpoint to studies finding negative design effects comes from Geng (2022), a quasi-natural experiment using ANOVA and multiple linear regression on 123,208 course records from 2,622 undergraduates across nine semesters at a single Chinese university. The study found that: (1) during the online education semester, students' academic records "improved substantially with low fluctuation and greater stability"; (2) this improvement was more pronounced for sophomores and juniors than for freshmen; and (3) "online education during the pandemic period significantly improved the course scores of undergraduates, especially sophomores, in the following one or two semesters" — indicating a *persistent* benefit that outlasted the online period itself ([Geng, 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070)). Several design factors may explain this result. The Chinese university deployed multiple online modalities — live teaching, video teaching, and MOOC-based instruction — using platforms such as Tencent Conference, Tencent Classroom, Rain Classroom, and Ding Talk, suggesting a multi-modal approach rather than simple lecture recording. The study's context — a government-mandated "classes suspended, learning continues" policy — likely prompted a more coordinated institutional response than the ad-hoc transitions documented in Western studies. The sustained improvement into subsequent offline semesters suggests that well-structured online formats can yield lasting pedagogical benefits, possibly by developing students' self-regulated learning capacities or by providing more structured formative feedback than typical in-person courses. This finding is especially significant because it comes from a quasi-natural experiment with objective performance data rather than perceptual self-reports, addressing the design-quality confound that plagued emergency remote courses elsewhere.

**The Design-Effectiveness Mechanism Summarized.** The evidence converges on a coherent causal chain: intentional instructional design → structured engagement (active learning, feedback, peer interaction) → sustained cognitive effort and self-regulation → improved learning outcomes. The pandemic disrupted this chain at the first link. Instructors who lacked training in online pedagogy — the majority, given the speed of the transition — defaulted to content delivery formats (lecture recordings, slide decks) that failed to activate engagement mechanisms. The TASO review's emphasis on "design rather than transfer" captures this distinction precisely: the same content, delivered through a well-designed online course with interactive elements and formative assessment, can be effective; delivered through a Zoom lecture with no pedagogical adaptation, it produces fatigue and disengagement. The Geng (2022) finding that improvement persisted into subsequent semesters further suggests that the design-quality effect is not merely about short-term engagement but about deeper learning that transfers across modalities — a result that would be invisible in cross-sectional perceptual surveys and detectable only through longitudinal or quasi-experimental designs with objective outcome measures.

### Lack of Social Interaction: Isolation, Peer Learning, and Instructor Presence

The abrupt shift to online learning during the COVID-19 pandemic stripped higher education of one of its most fundamental yet often invisible resources: face-to-face social interaction. The absence of peer collaboration, informal learning, instructor immediacy, and campus community did not merely make online learning less pleasant — it systematically undermined the cognitive and motivational mechanisms through which deep learning occurs.

**The Mechanism: How Social Interaction Supports Learning**

Social interaction in educational settings serves multiple interlocking functions. First, it enables *collaborative knowledge construction*: students articulate ideas, challenge one another's reasoning, and co-construct understanding through dialogue, which deepens cognitive processing beyond what solo study achieves. Second, social interaction provides *motivation through accountability* — the presence of peers and instructors creates implicit expectations for preparation and participation, reducing procrastination. Third, interaction delivers *emotional support*, buffering the stress and isolation inherent in demanding academic work. Fourth, it permits *immediate feedback*: questions are answered in real time, misconceptions are corrected on the spot, and adaptive scaffolding occurs naturally in face-to-face discourse. When these functions are removed or attenuated, students lose not only a source of intellectual stimulation but also the relational infrastructure that sustains engagement and persistence.

**Evidence of Isolation's Negative Impact**

Multiple pandemic-era studies documented the corrosive effects of social isolation on learning outcomes. Bhowmik and Bhattacharya (2021), surveying university students in West Bengal, India, during the emergency shift to online learning, found that "students' feeling of isolation with boredom and frustration are the most influential aspects in online learning," with lack of immediate feedback emerging as the most consequential teacher-related factor ([Bhowmik & Bhattacharya, 2021](https://tojdel.net/journals/tojdel/articles/v09i01/v09i01-08.pdf)). This finding is striking because it identifies the *social* dimension — not technology or content — as the primary driver of dissatisfaction.

Kofoed et al.'s (2024) randomized controlled trial at West Point provided the strongest causal evidence. In their post-course survey, online students "felt less connected to their instructors and peers" and "struggled to concentrate in class" — a direct link between the social deficit of online learning and the 0.215 standard deviation drop in final grades observed among online students ([Kofoed et al., 2024](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077)). The connection loss was not merely affective; it had measurable academic consequences.

The Anhui cross-sectional study of 5,558 Chinese college students further demonstrated the interaction-isolation–mental-health pathway. Students with lower cooperative learning ability exhibited significantly higher odds of depression (OR = 1.382, 95% CI: 1.138–1.678 for the lowest quartile), anxiety, and stress, while higher cooperative learning ability was protective ([Anhui/Frontiers in Psychology, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full)). This suggests a bidirectional relationship: isolation reduces cooperative engagement, which in turn exacerbates negative emotions that further impair learning — a feedback loop that deepens academic disengagement.

Meng et al. (2024), in their systematic review of 25 articles, identified "lack of social interaction" as a distinct factor negatively influencing online learning effectiveness, alongside "lack of motivation" and "difficulties in understanding materials" ([Meng et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1334153/full)). Ensmann et al. (2021), studying 507 students at a southeastern U.S. university during emergency remote learning, found that students experienced profound anxiety and that "social presence" functioned as a prerequisite for learning — "a literacy for learning in any modality" — underscoring the need for "increased empathy, communication, interaction, and flexibility" from instructors and the course community ([Ensmann et al., 2021](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/2868)).

**Structured Interaction as a Mitigating Factor**

Evidence consistently shows that when institutions deliberately built structured interaction into online formats, the negative effects of isolation were partially mitigated. Händel et al. (2021), in a large survey of 3,056 students and 396 instructors at a German university, found that students in predominantly synchronous settings reported significantly more peer-centered activities such as feedback, greater fulfillment of basic psychological needs for competence and relatedness (per self-determination theory), and higher overall satisfaction compared to students in predominantly asynchronous settings ([Händel et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.733554/full)). This finding is critical because it demonstrates that *the modality of interaction matters*: synchronous formats that preserve real-time interpersonal exchange can partially substitute for in-person contact, while asynchronous formats — though offering flexibility — cannot fully replicate the spontaneous, reciprocal dynamics of face-to-face discussion.

**The Loss of Informal and Peer Learning**

Beyond structured activities, the pandemic eliminated the ambient social learning that permeates campus life: hallway conversations, study groups, peer tutoring, and the informal exchange of ideas that occurs before and after class. These interactions, though difficult to quantify, serve as powerful mechanisms for clarifying confusion, reinforcing motivation, and building academic identity. Bhowmik and Bhattacharya (2021) noted that 73% of learners felt isolated in online classes, and 64% of students strongly approved that e-learning "lacks interaction among students and with the teachers" ([Bhowmik & Bhattacharya, 2021](https://tojdel.net/journals/tojdel/articles/v09i01/v09i01-08.pdf)). The absence of informal peer learning meant that students who previously relied on study groups for motivation and comprehension lost an entire support system, contributing to the engagement decline observed across pandemic-era studies.

**Instructor Presence and Immediacy as Compensatory Mechanisms**

When peer interaction diminished, instructor presence became the primary remaining source of social connection in the online classroom. Kedia et al. (2023), in a structural equation modeling study of 300 undergraduate and postgraduate students across three Indian universities, found that "Instructor-Student Interaction" had a positive and significant relationship with students' learning performance, mediated through student engagement. Their proposed model included five factors — instructor-student interaction, peer interaction, social media use, family support, and technical support — but instructor-student interaction emerged as the most robust predictor of effectiveness. Peer interaction, by contrast, had only a "minor influence" on learners' performance ([Kedia et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37250210/)). This finding highlights a critical asymmetry: when the social ecosystem of learning collapsed, the instructor's role became disproportionately important. Instructor immediacy behaviors — timely feedback, active facilitation of discussion, visible responsiveness — could partially compensate for the absence of peer interaction by providing the accountability, feedback, and relational connection that students otherwise received from classmates.

**Synchronous vs. Asynchronous: A Critical Distinction**

The distinction between synchronous and asynchronous interaction emerged as a key determinant of whether online learning could preserve social connection. Händel et al. (2021) demonstrated that synchronous settings supported real-time interpersonal communication, immediate feedback, and a "sense of personalization" that narrowed the gap between online and face-to-face learning, while asynchronous settings were perceived as "more individualistic and less cooperative" ([Händel et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.733554/full)). However, even synchronous videoconferencing could not fully replicate in-person interaction; Rapanta et al. (2020) found that videoconferencing "decreases the fluency of interaction and makes interactions slower and attention lower compared to traditional teaching" ([Händel et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.733554/full)). Asynchronous interaction, while valuable for reflection and flexibility, could not reproduce the spontaneous, rapid-exchange dynamics of classroom discussion, creating what Daigle and Stuvland (2020) termed the "social presence gap" — a deficit in perceived connection that accounted for lower satisfaction with online learning.

**Synthesis**

The evidence converges on a clear mechanism: the absence of social interaction reduced online learning effectiveness by removing the collaborative, motivational, and feedback functions that social connection provides. Students who felt isolated — disconnected from peers and instructors — experienced lower engagement, higher anxiety and depression, and diminished academic performance. Structured synchronous interaction and strong instructor presence could partially mitigate these effects, but asynchronous formats and the loss of informal peer learning left a deficit that no single intervention could fully close. The pandemic's social distancing policies thus created a uniquely hostile environment for learning precisely because they attacked the relational substrate on which education depends.

### Negative Emotions: Anxiety, Depression, Stress, and Their Cognitive Impact

Negative emotions — anxiety, depression, stress, frustration, and burnout — constituted one of the most pervasive mechanisms through which online learning effectiveness was undermined during the COVID-19 pandemic. The cognitive science literature establishes that negative emotional states consume finite cognitive resources: anxiety and stress activate the amygdala and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, elevating cortisol levels that impair working memory capacity and attentional control, while depression reduces executive function, motivation, and the ability to initiate and sustain goal-directed behaviors ([Lukasik et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6351483/); [Almarzouki, 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10253890.2024.2364333)). Applied to the online learning context, this means that a student experiencing pandemic-related anxiety has reduced capacity to process lecture material, retain information, and engage in higher-order cognitive tasks such as analysis and synthesis — the core activities of higher education. The mechanism operates through a cascade: negative emotions deplete working memory and attention → information processing and retention are impaired → motivation and self-regulation decline → avoidance behaviors (skipping sessions, not submitting assignments) follow → academic performance deteriorates, which in turn generates further negative emotions.

**Prevalence rates documented among online learners.** The scale of the mental health crisis among remote learning students was substantial. A large-scale cross-sectional study conducted among 5,558 students at two universities in Anhui province, China, found that 35.15% reported symptoms of depression, 36.32% reported anxiety, and 17.24% reported stress during the period of online learning at home under COVID-19 lockdowns. Males and nonmedical students were at elevated risk for all three conditions ([Anhui/Frontiers in Psychology, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full)). A meta-analysis by Xu and Wang (2023), aggregating 36 studies across 19 nations with 78,674 participants, yielded even higher pooled prevalence estimates: 58% for anxiety, 50% for depression, and 71% for stress among remote learning students during the pandemic. Critically, the meta-analysis found that students in higher education exhibited significantly higher rates of anxiety and depression than those in elementary education, and that students in emergency remote learning contexts — the unplanned, crisis-driven mode characteristic of the pandemic — suffered more than those in traditional, planned distance learning ([Xu & Wang, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1103925/full)). A study of 753 Polish university students corroborated these figures: 58% exhibited increased stress levels, 56% showed depressive symptoms, and 18% reported suicidal thoughts. The most significant predictors of depression were high stress levels and e-learning-specific factors — isolation from friends and acquaintances, perceived negative impact on knowledge acquisition, reduced motivation to learn, and worsening grades — which together explained approximately 66% of the variance in depression scores ([Frontiers in Public Health, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2022.871934/full)).

**The bidirectional feedback loop.** Perhaps the most insidious aspect of the negative-emotion mechanism was its self-reinforcing, bidirectional nature. Pandemic-related anxiety — fear of infection, economic uncertainty, health worries about family members — compounded the stress of adapting to an unfamiliar online learning mode. Simultaneously, the isolation inherent in remote learning — the absence of peer support, informal social interaction, and campus community — exacerbated pre-existing mental health vulnerabilities, creating a negative feedback loop. The Anhui study illustrated this dynamic: students quarantined at home experienced social disconnection and decreased peer support, which is itself associated with depressive symptoms, while the demands of online learning added academic pressure on top of health crisis anxiety ([Anhui/Frontiers in Psychology, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full)). The Polish study reinforced this, identifying isolation from friends and acquaintances as a direct predictor of both elevated stress and depression during e-learning ([Frontiers in Public Health, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2022.871934/full)). Longitudinal evidence from a study tracking university students across two academic years — beginning in September 2019 (pre-pandemic) and continuing through Autumn 2020 (during remote learning) — found that depression levels increased significantly in year 2, particularly among students at Ulster University in Northern Ireland, even as anxiety levels slightly decreased, suggesting that the sustained stress of the pandemic and remote learning had a cumulative depressive effect over time ([Pelucio et al., 2022, BMC Psychology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40359-022-00897-3)).

**Impact on specific learning behaviors.** Negative emotions did not merely produce subjective distress; they translated into measurable behavioral changes that directly undermined academic outcomes. The Kofoed et al. (2024) RCT found that online students experienced concentration difficulties and reduced social connection, representing the proximal cognitive mechanism through which emotional distress translates into the observed grade deficit (see S2.4 for full study details; [Kofoed et al., 2024, AER Insights](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077)). The Polish study's finding that reduced motivation to learn was a direct predictor of depression further illustrates the behavioral pathway: students experiencing negative emotions lost the drive to attend sessions, complete assignments, and engage with course material, which in turn worsened their academic performance and deepened their depressive symptoms ([Frontiers in Public Health, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2022.871934/full)). In the Brazilian context, Pelucio et al. (2022) found that lack of motivation and difficulties maintaining the online learning routine were among the most significant challenges reported by students, and that female students with less social contact presented more depressive symptoms, indicating that the intersection of isolation and gender vulnerability produced differential behavioral impacts ([Pelucio et al., 2022, BMC Psychology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40359-022-00897-3)).

**Self-directed learning ability as a mediator.** The Anhui study provided a critical moderating variable in this pathway: self-directed learning (SDL) ability. The study measured SDL ability across three dimensions — self-management, information capability, and cooperative learning — and found that higher SDL ability was significantly and negatively associated with depression, anxiety, and stress, with cooperative learning ability also protective (see S3.2 for detailed SDL findings and odds ratios; [Anhui/Frontiers in Psychology, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720911/full)). This finding has a dual interpretation relevant to the online learning context. First, SDL ability acts as a psychological buffer: students who can manage their own learning process are less vulnerable to the emotional distress that remote learning can produce. Second, the cooperative learning dimension — the ability to communicate and collaborate with peers — directly implicates the social interaction mechanism (discussed in S3.3): students who lacked cooperative learning skills were more isolated, and isolation was a direct pathway to negative emotions. This suggests that SDL ability and social interaction are not independent protective factors but are intertwined, with cooperative learning serving as the behavioral bridge between social connection and emotional resilience.

**The confound: pandemic vs. learning mode.** A critical methodological challenge in interpreting these findings is disentangling negative emotions caused by the online learning mode from those caused by the broader pandemic context. The Xu and Wang (2023) meta-analysis addressed this partially by distinguishing emergency remote learning from traditional distance learning and finding higher distress in the former, suggesting that the crisis context — not online learning per se — was a significant driver ([Xu & Wang, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1103925/full)). The Geng (2022) quasi-natural experiment provides a useful counterpoint: despite concurrent lockdown and stay-at-home orders, the study found academic improvement during the online semester, though stricter orders were associated with elevated psychological stress that could attenuate effectiveness (see S2.4 for full study details; [Geng, 2022, Sustainability](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070)). The Kofoed et al. (2024) RCT provides the strongest causal evidence that the online modality itself contributed to concentration difficulties, but even this design cannot fully separate the emotional impact of learning online from the emotional impact of living through a pandemic while learning online (see S2.4 for full study details; [Kofoed et al., 2024](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077)). The most defensible interpretation is that the pandemic context and the online learning mode were synergistic stressors: the pandemic created a baseline of anxiety and isolation that online learning, particularly in its emergency, unplanned form, was ill-equipped to address, and the resulting emotional burden then impaired the cognitive and behavioral capacities necessary for effective learning.

### Convenience and Flexibility: Access, Autonomy, and Self-Paced Learning

The convenience and flexibility of online learning constituted a double-edged sword during the pandemic. On one hand, asynchronous access, self-paced study, elimination of commuting, and the ability to review recorded materials removed logistical barriers and expanded access for many students. On the other hand, the same autonomy that made online learning convenient also demanded greater self-discipline and self-regulation—capacities that were unevenly distributed across the student population and often eroded by pandemic-related stress. This tension between flexibility and self-regulation is central to understanding why convenience benefits appeared inconsistently across studies and outcome types.

**Mechanism of positive influence.** Flexibility and convenience improve learning effectiveness through several interconnected pathways. First, asynchronous access reduces logistical barriers: students can engage with materials at times that accommodate work, caregiving, or health constraints, which is especially critical for non-traditional students. [Meng et al. (2024)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1334153/full) identified flexibility and convenience as among the positive factors influencing online learning effectiveness (see S1.1 for the full factor taxonomy). Second, self-paced learning allows students to adjust the speed of instruction to their own comprehension level—reviewing recorded lectures multiple times or pausing to process complex material—something that fixed-pace in-person lectures do not permit. Third, the elimination of commuting reduces fatigue and frees time that can be redirected toward study. These mechanisms suggest that, under the right conditions, the structural convenience of online learning can translate into genuine academic gains.

**Evidence of student appreciation.** Multiple studies documented that students valued the flexibility of online learning. The [BMC Medical Education (2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z) study found that 80% of students wanted to continue with some online instruction after the pandemic, with equivalent or better course performance in 16 of 17 courses (see S2.3 for full study details). This finding is notable because it demonstrates that flexibility benefits can manifest in objective performance measures, not merely in perceptual surveys. Similarly, [Geng (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070) found sustained academic improvement during and after the online semester using large-scale institutional grade data (see S2.4 for full study details), providing objective evidence that well-structured online learning may yield lasting academic benefits.

**The self-discipline paradox.** The same flexibility that enables personalized pacing also removes the external structure—fixed class schedules, physical co-presence, real-time accountability—that keeps many students on track. [Xu et al. (2023)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1128034/full), in their meta-analysis of 52 studies (57 effect sizes), explicitly noted that asynchronous online learning "indicates a higher demand for students' self-discipline due to limited interaction with instructors." This creates a differential effectiveness pattern: students with strong self-regulation and time-management skills benefit from flexibility, while those without these skills are harmed by it. The procrastination and self-regulated learning findings from [Hong et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7846229/) and [Stan et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9100394/) further document this divide (see S3.2 for detailed discussion). [Ma et al. (2022)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1344070), studying 1,149 Chinese undergraduates, found that attention control partially mediated the relationship between online self-regulated learning and academic procrastination, with peer support moderating these effects. Geng (2022) itself acknowledged this tension, noting that "online education was only suitable for students with time management skills and strong self-regulation ability" (see S2.4 for full study details).

This divide was confirmed at the highest level of causal evidence. [Kofoed et al. (2024)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077), in their RCT at West Point, found that online students' final grades dropped significantly, with post-course surveys revealing concentration difficulties (see S2.4 for full study details). The largest negative effects were observed for academically at-risk students, precisely the population most vulnerable to the self-discipline demands of unstructured online learning.

**Infrastructure, emergency context, and the offsetting of convenience benefits.** A striking finding from [Xu et al. (2023)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1128034/full) complicates the narrative that adequate infrastructure alone ensures satisfaction: their moderator analysis found that even in countries with developed digital infrastructure, emergency-learning satisfaction was lower than in non-emergency contexts (see S2.2 for the full satisfaction analysis). This counterintuitive result suggests that in emergency contexts—even where digital infrastructure was robust—the stress, disruption, and lack of preparation associated with the pandemic offset the convenience benefits that online learning would otherwise provide. In other words, convenience is a necessary but not sufficient condition for positive outcomes; its benefits can be nullified by the broader crisis context in which the learning takes place.

**Objective versus perceptual evidence.** The evidence on convenience and flexibility spans both objective and perceptual measures. The [BMC Medical Education (2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-021-02909-z) and [Geng (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/21/14070) studies provide objective performance data showing equivalent or better outcomes (see S2.3 and S2.4 respectively), while the continuation preference in the BMC study and the satisfaction rates in [Xu et al. (2023)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1128034/full) reflect perceptual appreciation (see S2.2). However, the [Kofoed et al. (2024)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20230077) RCT—using objective grade data—found negative effects (see S2.4), and the procrastination studies ([Hong et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7846229/); [Ma et al. 2022](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1344070)) used cross-sectional self-report designs (see S3.2 for Hong et al.). This pattern suggests that convenience benefits are more reliably observed in contexts where online learning is well-structured and where students possess adequate self-regulation; when these conditions are absent—particularly in emergency, high-stress contexts—the same flexibility becomes a liability, and objective performance measures capture the resulting decline more faithfully than perceptual surveys, which may reflect appreciation for reduced logistical burden even when learning suffers.

### Cross-Factor Interactions and Brief Synthesis

The five factors examined in S3.1–S3.5 did not operate in isolation but interacted dynamically to shape the overall effectiveness landscape of pandemic-era online learning. An integrated model positions **infrastructure as a prerequisite**: without reliable internet, devices, and platform stability, the other factors are moot. **Instructional design and social interaction function as mediating factors** that determine whether online learning is effective once access is secured. **Negative emotions serve as a moderating factor** capable of undermining effectiveness regardless of design quality. **Convenience and flexibility act as an enabling factor** that provides benefits but is insufficient alone. The pandemic context—characterized by emergency transition, health anxiety, and social disruption—amplified each factor's influence relative to pre-pandemic planned online learning.

**Infrastructure × Instructional design.** No amount of pedagogical sophistication can compensate for a student who lacks internet access or a functional device. As discussed in S3.1, Meng et al. (2024) identified infrastructure barriers as particularly acute in developing countries, where they effectively nullify any instructional design advantages (see S1.1; S3.1). Conversely, where infrastructure was adequate, instructional design quality became the decisive variable: Geng (2022) found that well-structured online formats could yield lasting academic benefits when access was not a barrier (see S2.4). The interaction is thus asymmetric: good design cannot overcome infrastructure failure, but infrastructure without good design produces at best mixed results.

**Social interaction × Negative emotions.** The absence of face-to-face social interaction and the prevalence of negative emotions reinforced each other in a bidirectional feedback loop. Social isolation removed peer support and informal learning mechanisms that buffer stress, while pandemic-related anxiety and depression impaired students' capacity to engage in whatever online interaction was available. The Anhui study's finding that negative emotions were highly prevalent among online learners—and that self-directed learning ability was negatively associated with all three conditions—illustrates how students with weaker self-regulation were both more emotionally distressed and less able to cope independently (see S3.4). Kofoed et al. (2024) similarly found that the isolation of the online modality compounded the cognitive burden of pandemic stress rather than merely coexisting with it (see S2.4). This feedback loop meant that interventions targeting only one dimension—adding discussion forums without addressing mental health, or providing counseling without improving social learning—were insufficient to break the cycle.

**Convenience/flexibility × Self-regulation.** The flexibility of asynchronous, self-paced learning benefited students with strong self-regulation but disadvantaged those without it. Geng (2022) noted that online education was only suitable for students with time management skills and strong self-regulation ability (see S2.4). Rodríguez-Planas (2022) provided complementary evidence that the same flexibility mechanism produced opposite outcomes depending on students' self-regulation and socioeconomic resources (see S2.3). This divergence illustrates how convenience benefits were conditional on student characteristics that the pandemic context often eroded.

**Pandemic amplification.** The emergency context intensified each factor's effect. Infrastructure gaps that might have been minor inconveniences in planned online learning became absolute barriers when campuses closed and students returned to areas with limited connectivity. Instructional design suffered because instructors had days or weeks to redesign courses rather than months, producing what Geng (2022) termed "emergency remote education" rather than planned online learning (see S2.4). Social interaction losses were magnified because students were physically isolated from peers and instructors simultaneously, rather than choosing online delivery voluntarily. Negative emotions were amplified by health fear, economic disruption, and caregiving burdens that compounded the stress of adapting to a new learning mode. Convenience benefits were partially offset by pandemic stress: Xu et al. (2023) found that crisis conditions eroded the flexibility advantage even where digital infrastructure was adequate (see S2.2).

**Synthesis.** The integrated model reveals that the mixed effectiveness landscape of pandemic-era online learning is best understood not as a single question of whether online learning "works," but as a configuration problem. Effectiveness emerged when adequate infrastructure, well-designed instruction, structured social interaction, manageable emotions, and student self-regulation aligned. The pandemic's emergency conditions disrupted this alignment by simultaneously degrading infrastructure access, limiting design time, eliminating informal social contact, elevating distress, and demanding unprecedented self-discipline. Studies finding positive outcomes—such as Geng (2022)—tended to occur where infrastructure was adequate and institutional support was sufficient to approximate planned online conditions. Studies finding negative outcomes—such as Kofoed et al. (2024) and Bird et al. (2022)—captured contexts where one or more factors in the configuration were compromised. The most defensible conclusion is that online learning effectiveness during the pandemic was conditional: it depended on the simultaneous presence of enabling conditions that the emergency context frequently disrupted.
