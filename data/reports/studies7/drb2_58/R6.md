# Online Learning in Higher Education During the COVID-19 Pandemic (January 2020–August 2023): A Systematic Review of Effectiveness and Influencing Factors

**Abstract**

This systematic review synthesizes 18 empirical studies published between 2020 and 2023 examining the effectiveness of online learning in higher education during the COVID-19 pandemic. The overall evidence is mixed: studies employing objective performance measures (e.g., exam scores, course grades) in quasi-experimental designs predominantly found online learning to be effective or equivalent to in-person instruction, while cross-sectional perceptual surveys produced more varied results, and longitudinal studies capturing cumulative effects tended to report negative outcomes. A striking methodological pattern emerged—studies asking students to retrospectively compare online and in-person experiences systematically reported more negative results than randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental designs using objective data, owing to recall bias, the conflation of satisfaction with effectiveness, and the absence of control for confounders. Five key factors—infrastructure, instructional design, lack of social interaction, negative emotions, and convenience/flexibility—jointly determined whether online learning succeeded or failed, with each operating through distinct causal mechanisms that varied across institutional and national contexts.

## Background and Context

The COVID-19 pandemic, declared by the World Health Organization in March 2020, triggered an unprecedented disruption of global higher education. Virtually overnight, universities worldwide were forced to migrate their teaching operations from traditional face-to-face classrooms to online platforms—a transition often described as "emergency remote teaching" to distinguish it from deliberately planned online education. Between January 2020 and August 2023, higher education institutions across more than 40 countries experimented with varying combinations of synchronous video conferencing, asynchronous learning management systems, and hybrid models, generating what amounts to the largest natural experiment in the history of educational technology.

This sudden and massive shift raised two interrelated questions of paramount importance to educators, policymakers, and students alike: First, to what extent was online learning actually effective in producing learning outcomes comparable to those of traditional in-person instruction? Second, what factors determined whether online learning succeeded or failed in particular contexts? Answering these questions requires moving beyond anecdotal impressions and individual institutional reports to synthesize the growing body of empirical evidence accumulated during the pandemic period. The studies reviewed here span multiple continents, research methodologies, and outcome variables—from objective academic performance measures such as exam scores and course grades to subjective indicators such as student satisfaction, perceived effectiveness, and emotional well-being. This diversity is both a strength and a challenge: it provides a rich evidence base, but also means that apparent contradictions between studies often reflect differences in what was measured, how it was measured, and from what temporal vantage point, rather than genuine disagreements about the underlying phenomenon.

## 2. Effectiveness Analysis

### 2.1 Overall Pattern of Findings

The reviewed studies collectively suggest that the effectiveness of online learning during the COVID-19 pandemic was neither universally positive nor universally negative—it was deeply contingent on what was measured, how it was measured, and the contextual conditions under which learning occurred. When effectiveness was assessed through objective academic performance data (exam scores, course grades), the weight of evidence tilted toward effectiveness or equivalence. When effectiveness was assessed through self-reported satisfaction, emotional well-being, or perceived learning quality, results were more negative, particularly in studies from developing countries and in longitudinal designs that captured cumulative effects over time.

This pattern is not merely a statistical curiosity. It reflects a fundamental tension at the heart of online learning evaluation: students can learn effectively in online formats (as evidenced by comparable or superior academic performance) while simultaneously feeling dissatisfied, isolated, and emotionally drained by the experience. Both observations are true; they are simply measuring different constructs. Understanding this distinction is essential for interpreting the evidence base and for designing post-pandemic educational policies.

### 2.2 Cross-Sectional Perceptual Surveys

Seven of the 18 studies employed cross-sectional survey designs in which students were asked—at a single point in time—to report their perceptions of online learning effectiveness, satisfaction, or emotional state. These studies produced the most heterogeneous results:

- **Effective findings**: Wan Mamat et al. (2022) surveyed 349 Malaysian students and found that e-learning was generally perceived as effective, with students reporting positive attitudes toward the technology and its learning support capabilities. Similarly, Sari et al. (2022), in one of the largest surveys reviewed (1,845 students across 62 Indonesian universities), found generally positive satisfaction levels, though with noted areas for improvement in instructor preparation, teaching materials, and internet data allowances.

- **Neutral findings**: Taja-on et al. (2021) surveyed 412 Philippine students and found neutral perceptions—students neither strongly endorsed nor strongly rejected online learning, reporting a mixed experience that defied simple categorization. Thach and Lai (2022), in a large-scale SEM study of 2,338 Vietnamese students, found that while some factors positively predicted satisfaction, others offset those gains, resulting in an overall neutral net effect.

- **Ineffective findings**: Abushammala et al. (2021) found that among 213 Omani students, satisfaction with online learning fell below 50%, with students citing poor internet connectivity, inadequate institutional support, and difficulty engaging with course content. Aguilera-Hermida, Avci, and Poyrazli (2021) surveyed 125 Turkish students and found that positive emotions declined while negative emotions increased during online learning, alongside challenges including internet problems, lack of motivation, and difficulty contacting instructors.

A cross-cutting observation from these survey-based studies is that **satisfaction and perceived effectiveness were highly sensitive to national context and institutional readiness**. Studies from countries with robust digital infrastructure and prior e-learning investments (Malaysia, Indonesia in some institutions) tended to report more positive results, while studies from contexts with weaker infrastructure (Oman, parts of Turkey) reported more negative outcomes. This geographic patterning underscores that "online learning effectiveness" is not a universal property of the medium itself but is mediated by the enabling conditions surrounding its implementation.

### 2.3 Cross-Sectional Comparative Studies

Four studies used cross-sectional comparative designs that contrasted online and in-person cohorts, though without random assignment:

- **Iglesias-Pradas et al. (2021)** compared academic performance across 43 courses at a Spanish university before and during COVID-19, finding that online students achieved higher academic outcomes than their in-person counterparts. This is one of the strongest "effective" findings in the review, though it relies on the assumption that the two cohorts were comparable—a limitation inherent to non-randomized comparisons.

- **Alarifi and Song (2024)** conducted the largest comparative study in this review, analyzing final exam scores of 16,722 first-year students at King Saud University in Saudi Arabia across five mandatory courses. Initial t-test comparisons showed statistically significant score drops for the online (2021) cohort in four of five courses. However, after controlling for gender, class size, and admission scores using multiple linear regression, a more nuanced picture emerged: three courses favored the online cohort, one favored the in-person cohort, and one showed no significant difference. This study exemplifies the critical importance of statistical controls—without them, the simple mean comparison would have led to an "ineffective" conclusion that the multivariate analysis contradicted.

- **Kamal et al. (2023)** studied 369 Iraqi students using logistic regression and found mixed results: virtual classes were perceived positively for addressing learning continuity during the pandemic, but significant infrastructure barriers (internet instability, device limitations) and demographic disparities (gender digital divide, family income, father's education level, number of rooms at home) moderated the experience.

- **Aguilera-Hermida (2020)** used mixed methods with 270 students and found that while online learning was accepted as a necessity, students generally preferred face-to-face instruction, leading to an "ineffective" classification based on acceptance rather than performance metrics.

The comparative studies collectively demonstrate that **the same dataset can yield opposite conclusions depending on whether confounding variables are controlled**. Alarifi and Song's study is the clearest illustration: raw score comparisons suggested online learning was inferior, but regression analysis controlling for student characteristics revealed it was superior in most courses. This finding has profound implications for policy—it suggests that much of the perceived "ineffectiveness" of online learning during the pandemic may have been attributable to differences in student composition between online and in-person cohorts, rather than to the instructional medium itself.

### 2.4 Longitudinal Studies

Two studies employed longitudinal designs that tracked changes over time:

- **Besalti and Satici (2022)** conducted a two-wave panel survey of 249 Turkish students, finding that internet addiction increased over the study period and significantly decreased learning satisfaction. The longitudinal design captured a cumulative negative trajectory that single-time-point surveys would have missed—students may have initially tolerated or even enjoyed online learning, but prolonged exposure eroded satisfaction as the novelty wore off and the social and emotional costs accumulated.

- **Sigurdardottir, Torfason, and Jonsdottir (2022)** compared two undergraduate cohorts at an Icelandic university—one enrolled in 2020 during online instruction and one enrolled in 2021 when some in-person elements resumed. They found that the 2020 cohort formed significantly fewer social ties than comparison cohorts, with consequences for social integration and potentially for long-term retention. This study is notable for examining a downstream consequence of online learning—social network formation—that most other studies did not measure but that may have lasting implications for student success.

Longitudinal studies consistently found negative outcomes, which raises an important question: **does online learning become less effective over time, or do longitudinal designs simply capture different (and more consequential) outcomes?** The evidence suggests both mechanisms are operative. The novelty and initial flexibility of online learning may mask cumulative costs—social isolation, screen fatigue, motivational decline—that only become detectable over extended periods. At the same time, longitudinal designs are more likely to capture outcomes like social integration and sustained motivation that are themselves time-dependent processes. Either way, the practical implication is clear: evaluations based solely on short-term academic performance may systematically overstate the effectiveness of online learning by missing its cumulative social and emotional costs.

### 2.5 Quasi-Experimental Studies

Three studies employed quasi-experimental designs comparing objective academic performance between online and in-person conditions:

- **Gonzalez et al. (2020)** conducted a field experiment with 458 Spanish students, comparing performance before and during COVID-19 confinement. They found that students performed better during the online period, a counterintuitive result that the authors attributed to factors including increased study time (due to eliminated commuting), greater access to recorded materials for review, and the self-paced nature of some online components.

- **Zheng, Bender, and Lyon (2021)** compared course grade distributions between 482 U.S. dental students who took courses online in Summer 2020 and a control group who took the same courses in-person in Summer 2019. In 16 of 17 comparable courses, the online cohort was equally or more likely to receive an A grade than the in-person cohort—a remarkably consistent positive finding across diverse course subjects.

- **Alarifi and Song (2024)**, as discussed in Section 2.3, used multiple regression on a massive dataset and found mixed results after controlling for confounders, with three courses favoring online and one favoring in-person instruction.

Quasi-experimental studies using objective performance data produced the most consistently positive results. This convergence is noteworthy because these studies spanned different countries (Spain, USA, Saudi Arabia), disciplines (general university courses, dental education, freshman foundation courses), and analytical approaches (pre-post comparison, chi-square grade comparison, multiple regression). The consistency of positive findings despite this diversity suggests that **when effectiveness is defined as measurable academic performance, online learning during the pandemic largely succeeded in achieving its primary educational objective**.

### 2.6 Randomized Controlled Trials

Only one study in this review employed a true randomized controlled trial design:

- **Young, Carlbring, and Ng (2023)** randomized 134 participants in Hong Kong to either an 8-week online mindfulness intervention (16 modules plus two half-day workshops) or a control condition. The intervention produced a significant reduction in anxiety (Cohen's d = 0.5), demonstrating that well-designed online interventions can achieve meaningful psychological outcomes.

While this RCT focused on a specific intervention (mindfulness training) rather than a full academic course, its methodological rigor provides important confirmation that online delivery can produce genuine effects when tested under the gold standard of experimental design. The absence of more RCTs in the pandemic literature reflects the practical reality that randomizing students to online versus in-person conditions during an emergency was rarely feasible or ethical. This gap in the evidence base means that causal claims about the relative effectiveness of online versus in-person instruction during the pandemic rest primarily on quasi-experimental and observational evidence, with their attendant limitations.

### 2.7 How Research Methodology Influenced Reported Results

The most striking finding of this review is the systematic relationship between research methodology and reported effectiveness:

| Methodology Type | Studies | Effective | Ineffective | Neutral/Mixed |
|-----------------|---------|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Quasi-experimental (objective measures) | 3 | 2 | 0 | 1 (Mixed) |
| RCT | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| Cross-sectional perceptual survey | 7 | 2 | 2 | 3 |
| Cross-sectional comparative | 4 | 1 | 1 | 2 (Mixed) |
| Longitudinal | 2 | 0 | 2 | 0 |

This pattern demands explanation. **Studies using objective performance data and experimental or quasi-experimental designs consistently found online learning effective or equivalent**, while studies relying on subjective perceptions, retrospective comparisons, or longitudinal tracking of emotional and social outcomes tended to find it ineffective or neutral.

Several mechanisms explain this divergence:

**Recall bias and idealization of the past.** When students in perceptual surveys are asked to compare their current online experience with their remembered in-person experience, they are susceptible to rosy retrospection—the tendency to remember past experiences more favorably than they were at the time. The physical classroom is idealized as a site of engagement and connection, while its inconveniences (commuting, rigid schedules, social anxiety in large lectures) fade from memory. This systematically biases retrospective comparisons against online learning. RCTs and quasi-experiments avoid this bias by measuring concurrent performance rather than recalled impressions.

**Conflation of satisfaction with effectiveness.** Many perceptual surveys equate "students are satisfied" with "learning is effective." These are distinct constructs. Gonzalez et al. (2020) found that students performed better academically online—but it is entirely possible that those same students would have reported lower satisfaction on a survey, because satisfaction is driven by emotional and social factors (isolation, boredom, screen fatigue) that may coexist with perfectly adequate or even superior academic performance. RCTs measuring objective outcomes bypass this conflation entirely.

**Temporal myopia of cross-sectional designs.** A cross-sectional survey administered mid-semester captures a snapshot of student sentiment that may not reflect the full trajectory of learning. Students may report frustration with online learning at a moment when technical glitches are fresh and social isolation is acute, even if their final exam performance ultimately matches or exceeds that of in-person cohorts. Longitudinal designs, by contrast, capture trends—and the two longitudinal studies in this review both found negative trajectories, suggesting that the cumulative costs of online learning (social erosion, motivational decline) manifest gradually and are invisible to single-time-point assessments.

**Lack of control for confounders.** Perceptual surveys typically do not—and cannot—control for the many factors that differ between students who happen to be in online versus in-person conditions during a pandemic: prior academic ability (as measured by admission scores in the Alarifi & Song study), gender composition, class size, socioeconomic background, and digital readiness. The Alarifi and Song (2024) study dramatically illustrated this problem: uncontrolled comparisons showed online learning was inferior, but controlling for just three variables (gender, class size, admission scores) reversed that conclusion for most courses. RCTs eliminate this problem through randomization; quasi-experiments address it through statistical controls; perceptual surveys neither randomize nor control.

**Selection of outcome variable.** Studies measuring academic performance (exam scores, grades) found more positive results than studies measuring satisfaction, emotional well-being, or social connectedness. This is not because one set of outcomes is more "real" than the other—both are legitimate educational outcomes—but because they are influenced by different causal processes. Academic performance in online settings is primarily determined by content access, study time, and self-regulation—factors that online learning can support through recorded lectures, flexible scheduling, and reduced commuting time. Satisfaction and social connectedness, by contrast, are determined by interpersonal interaction, emotional climate, and sense of belonging—factors that online learning inherently constrains. The choice of outcome variable thus pre-disposes findings toward positive or negative conclusions regardless of the underlying quality of instruction.

This methodological analysis leads to a critical practical insight: **the question "Is online learning effective?" is itself underspecified.** Effective for what? If the goal is knowledge acquisition as measured by exam performance, the evidence is largely affirmative. If the goal is student well-being, social integration, and sustained motivation, the evidence is considerably more cautionary. Both conclusions are valid; they are answers to different questions. Post-pandemic educational design must therefore specify which outcomes it prioritizes and design accordingly, rather than treating "effectiveness" as a single monolithic construct.

## 3. Analysis of Influencing Factors

The reviewed studies identify five categories of factors that significantly influenced the effectiveness of online learning during the pandemic. Each factor operated through a distinct causal mechanism, and their interactions often determined whether a particular online learning implementation succeeded or failed.

### 3.1 Infrastructure

**Infrastructure—the availability and quality of digital devices, internet connectivity, physical study spaces, and institutional technology support—functioned as a necessary but not sufficient condition for effective online learning.** Its mechanism of influence was straightforward: without adequate hardware, bandwidth, and a suitable physical environment, students could not reliably access course content, participate in synchronous sessions, or submit assignments, regardless of instructional quality.

The study by **Kamal et al. (2023)** in Iraq provides a vivid illustration. Among 369 Iraqi university students surveyed, logistic regression analysis revealed that the number of rooms in the student's home, family income, father's education level, and access to ICT equipment were all significant positive predictors of virtual classroom satisfaction and effectiveness. Students living in crowded households with limited devices and unstable internet connections faced structural barriers that no amount of pedagogical innovation could overcome. The gender digital divide was particularly pronounced, with female students reporting significantly less access to private study spaces and dedicated devices—reflecting cultural norms around gendered space allocation within Iraqi households.

**Abushammala et al. (2021)** found a similar pattern among 213 Omani students, where satisfaction with online learning fell below 50%. Poor internet connectivity and inadequate institutional technical support were among the most frequently cited barriers. These findings from the Gulf region contrast sharply with the positive findings from better-resourced contexts: Gonzalez et al. (2020) in Spain and Zheng et al. (2021) in the USA both studied institutions with established learning management systems, reliable broadband infrastructure, and students who largely possessed personal computing devices. The infrastructure differential helps explain why studies from developing or resource-constrained contexts (Oman, Iraq, parts of Turkey) more often reported ineffective or mixed results.

The causal mechanism can be conceptualized as an **access threshold**: below a minimum level of infrastructure, online learning cannot function regardless of other factors; above that threshold, infrastructure becomes a diminishing constraint and other factors (instructional design, social interaction, emotional state) become the primary determinants of effectiveness. This threshold effect explains why infrastructure was a dominant predictor in studies from developing countries but was rarely mentioned as a primary concern in studies from well-resourced institutions—once the access threshold is crossed, infrastructure recedes as a salient variable.

### 3.2 Instructional Design

**Instructional design—the deliberate structuring of learning activities, content delivery, assessment, and interaction patterns for the online medium—significantly moderated effectiveness through its impact on student engagement and content comprehension.** The mechanism here is more complex than for infrastructure: good instructional design does not merely enable access (as infrastructure does) but actively shapes how students process information, interact with content and peers, and regulate their own learning.

**Awajan (2023)** demonstrated this principle in Jordan, where a new instructional design model combining the ADDIE framework (Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation) with Universal Design for Learning (UDL) principles was implemented and evaluated through surveys of 47 professors. The study found that courses designed according to this structured model produced better student performance outcomes than courses using ad hoc online adaptations of face-to-face teaching. The ADDIE framework ensured systematic planning of learning objectives, content sequencing, and assessment alignment, while UDL principles ensured multiple means of representation, engagement, and expression—addressing the diversity of learner needs that online environments amplify.

**Zheng, Bender, and Lyon (2021)** provided complementary evidence from their quasi-experimental study of 482 U.S. dental students. Regression analyses revealed that students' perceived engagement with faculty and classmates was a significant predictor of their perceived effectiveness of the online course, with effect sizes ranging from r² = 0.22 to 0.77 across courses. The instructional strategies that students identified as most helpful—including breakout room activities, chat box discussions, polling, gamified learning with apps like Kahoot, and virtual office hours—were all elements of intentional online instructional design, not features that emerged spontaneously from the technology. Conversely, the one course where the online cohort underperformed the in-person cohort (Integrated Case-based Seminars) was characterized by students as having unclear requirements and assessment that was not aligned with lecture materials—classic instructional design failures that would impair learning in any format but were amplified in the online medium.

The causal mechanism connecting instructional design to effectiveness operates through **cognitive load management and engagement scaffolding**. Well-designed online courses reduce extraneous cognitive load by presenting content in manageable chunks, providing clear navigation, and aligning assessment with learning objectives—allowing students' limited attentional resources to be directed toward learning rather than interface navigation. Simultaneously, instructional design elements like interactive activities, formative feedback, and collaborative tasks sustain engagement, which is the proximal driver of learning outcomes. When instructors simply upload lecture recordings or deliver monologues via video conference—reproducing the "sage on the stage" model in a digital format—they fail to exploit the affordances of the online medium and produce predictably inferior results.

### 3.3 Lack of Social Interaction

**The absence of face-to-face social interaction during pandemic online learning constituted one of the most consistently reported negative influences, operating through mechanisms involving social isolation, reduced peer learning, and diminished sense of academic community.**

The longitudinal study by **Sigurdardottir, Torfason, and Jonsdottir (2022)** at an Icelandic university provides the most direct evidence. By comparing two cohorts of undergraduate students—one beginning their studies in 2020 under online conditions and one in 2021 with partial in-person elements—they found that the 2020 cohort formed significantly fewer social ties. The online cohort's social networks were smaller in size and weaker in strength compared to pre-pandemic cohorts, with consequences extending beyond the classroom: early social ties strongly predicted on-time degree completion, meaning that the social network deficit observed in the online cohort could have lasting implications for retention and graduation rates.

**Azmat and Ahmad** provided converging evidence from Pakistan in their mixed-methods study of 1,456 students and 70 teachers. They found that the lack of social interaction during online learning led to psychological problems including anxiety and depression, and significantly decreased learning satisfaction. Students reported that the absence of spontaneous peer discussions, informal study groups, and casual social contact—which occur naturally in physical campus environments—deprived them of collaborative learning opportunities and emotional support networks. The mechanism here is not simply that students "missed" social contact in a hedonic sense; rather, social interaction serves cognitive functions (peer explanation, knowledge co-construction, exposure to diverse perspectives) and emotional functions (belonging, validation, stress buffering) that directly contribute to learning effectiveness.

**Zheng, Bender, and Lyon (2021)** quantified this relationship in their U.S. dental school study, finding that perceived engagement with faculty and classmates predicted perceived course effectiveness with moderate to high correlations (r² = 0.22–0.77) across all but one course. Students explicitly highlighted instructional strategies that fostered social interaction—breakout rooms, chat-based discussions, polling, and virtual office hours—as the most helpful elements of their online experience. When such strategies were absent, students reported difficulty engaging, even in courses where the content itself was well-organized.

The causal mechanism through which social interaction influences effectiveness can be understood through the lens of **social constructivist learning theory**: knowledge is not merely transmitted from instructor to student but is actively constructed through social interaction, negotiation of meaning, and collaborative problem-solving. Online environments that fail to provide structured opportunities for social interaction effectively short-circuit this construction process, reducing learning to passive content consumption—a mode that is less effective for deep learning, less engaging for students, and more vulnerable to motivational decay over time.

### 3.4 Negative Emotions

**Negative emotions—anxiety, depression, stress, boredom, and motivational decline—constituted both a consequence and a cause of reduced online learning effectiveness, creating a self-reinforcing negative cycle.**

**Aguilera-Hermida, Avci, and Poyrazli (2021)** documented this dynamic in their study of 125 Turkish students, finding that positive emotions decreased while negative emotions increased during the shift to online learning. The challenges students reported—internet problems, lack of motivation, difficulty contacting instructors—were both sources of negative emotion and consequences of the online format's limitations. Notably, the study also identified some positive emotional experiences, including more family time and some GPA improvements, suggesting that the emotional impact of online learning was not uniformly negative but was experienced differently by different students.

**Besalti and Satici (2022)** traced a specific emotional pathway in their two-wave longitudinal study of 249 Turkish students: internet addiction increased over the study period, which in turn significantly decreased learning satisfaction. The mechanism here is instructive—increased internet use (mandated by online learning) led to problematic usage patterns (excessive screen time, distraction by social media, disrupted sleep), which eroded both emotional well-being and the capacity for sustained, focused academic work. This finding highlights a paradox of online learning: the same digital environment that enables learning also provides infinite distraction, and without strong self-regulation skills, students can become trapped in a cycle where emotional distress and academic underperformance mutually reinforce each other.

A large-scale study of **5,558 university students in Anhui Province, China** (identified through PubMed database records) further quantified the emotional toll, finding prevalence rates of 35.15% for depression, 36.32% for anxiety, and 17.24% for stress during the pandemic period. The study found that self-directed learning (SDL) capability was significantly negatively correlated with negative emotional states—students with stronger self-regulation skills experienced less psychological distress, suggesting that the emotional burden of online learning was not evenly distributed but fell most heavily on those least equipped to manage the autonomy that online learning demands.

The causal mechanism through which negative emotions impair effectiveness involves **cognitive interference and motivational depletion**. Anxiety and depression consume working memory resources that would otherwise be available for learning, directly reducing cognitive capacity for academic tasks. Stress activates physiological responses (elevated cortisol, disrupted sleep) that impair memory consolidation and attention. Motivational decline reduces the effort students invest in learning activities. These effects compound over time: a student who begins the semester mildly anxious may experience declining academic performance, which increases anxiety, which further impairs performance—a downward spiral that longitudinal designs are uniquely positioned to detect and that cross-sectional academic performance comparisons may entirely miss if the measurement occurs before the spiral has fully developed.

### 3.5 Convenience and Flexibility

**Convenience and flexibility—the elimination of commuting, the ability to review recorded lectures at one's own pace, and the autonomy to schedule study around personal obligations—emerged as the most consistently reported positive influence on online learning effectiveness.**

**Gonzalez et al. (2020)** found that students performed better academically during online learning, and the elimination of commuting time was among the factors the authors identified as potentially contributing to this counterintuitive result. Time previously spent traveling to and from campus was redirected toward study, and the availability of recorded lectures allowed students to review difficult concepts multiple times at their preferred pace.

**Zheng, Bender, and Lyon (2021)** provided the most direct evidence. In their survey of 379 U.S. dental students, respondents explicitly cited "enhanced flexibility" as one of the greatest benefits of online learning in open-ended comments. Students reported that typing questions in the chat box during live online classes was less intimidating than speaking up in a physical classroom—a finding that reveals how flexibility extends beyond scheduling to include modes of participation that reduce social anxiety and increase engagement from less assertive students. Additionally, students noted that not having to commute provided more time for sleep, which they connected to improved self-care and mental health. Over 80% of students in both the DDS and IDS programs wanted to continue with some online instruction post-pandemic, primarily citing these flexibility benefits.

**Alarifi and Song (2024)** observed a related phenomenon in their Saudi Arabian study: the course where online students showed the largest performance advantage (Computer Skills 101, +5.28 points after controlling for confounders) was a basic, conceptual course where self-paced review of instructional videos allowed students to cover material at their preferred speed—concentrating on unfamiliar topics while progressing quickly through already-known content. The authors connected this finding to research on self-paced learning (Tullis & Benjamin, 2011), which has demonstrated that self-paced learners often outperform those studying identical materials at a fixed pace.

The causal mechanism through which convenience and flexibility enhance effectiveness involves **temporal autonomy and cognitive resource optimization**. Traditional in-person education imposes a rigid temporal structure: students must be physically present at specific times, listen to lectures at the instructor's pace, and process information in real-time regardless of their current cognitive state. Online learning—particularly when asynchronous components are included—dissolves this temporal rigidity, allowing students to align learning activities with their own circadian rhythms, attentional cycles, and competing demands. This alignment optimizes the cognitive resources available for learning: a student who reviews a lecture recording when well-rested and focused will encode more information than the same student attempting to absorb the same content in a crowded lecture hall at 8:00 AM after insufficient sleep. Flexibility also enables **mastery-based pacing**, where students spend more time on difficult material and less on already-mastered content—an instructional principle long advocated by educational psychology but rarely achievable in synchronized classroom settings.

However, the benefits of flexibility are not unconditional. They require **sufficient self-regulation capability** to manage the autonomy that flexibility provides—a capability that, as the Chinese Anhui study demonstrated, varies substantially across students and is itself correlated with emotional well-being. Students with weak time management skills, limited metacognitive awareness, or high susceptibility to digital distraction may find that the same flexibility that benefits their self-regulated peers becomes a liability, as unstructured time is consumed by non-academic activities rather than study. This interaction between flexibility and self-regulation helps explain why the same online learning implementation can produce superior outcomes for some students and inferior outcomes for others—a pattern that is invisible in studies reporting only group-level means but emerges clearly in studies employing individual-level analysis.

---

## Conclusion

This systematic review of 18 empirical studies published during 2020–2023 yields two principal conclusions:

**First, the effectiveness of online learning in higher education during the COVID-19 pandemic was contingent on how effectiveness was defined and measured.** When assessed through objective academic performance—exam scores, course grades—online learning was largely effective, with quasi-experimental studies from Spain, the USA, and Saudi Arabia consistently finding that online cohorts performed equivalently or better than in-person comparison groups. When assessed through subjective measures—satisfaction, emotional well-being, social connectedness—results were more negative, particularly in longitudinal designs and in contexts with weak infrastructure. The most important methodological insight is that studies employing retrospective perceptual comparisons (asking students to rate online vs. remembered in-person experiences) systematically underestimated online learning effectiveness relative to studies using objective concurrent measures, owing to recall bias, the conflation of satisfaction with effectiveness, and the failure to control for confounding variables. The Alarifi and Song (2024) study's dramatic reversal of findings after statistical adjustment—from "ineffective" to "mixed/effective"—should serve as a cautionary tale against drawing conclusions from uncontrolled comparisons.

**Second, five factors—infrastructure, instructional design, social interaction, negative emotions, and convenience/flexibility—jointly determined whether online learning succeeded or failed in particular contexts.** Infrastructure functioned as a threshold condition: below a minimum level of digital readiness, no other factor could compensate. Instructional design operated through cognitive load management and engagement scaffolding, with structured frameworks (ADDIE + UDL) outperforming ad hoc adaptations. Lack of social interaction impaired learning through both cognitive (reduced peer learning) and emotional (isolation, diminished belonging) pathways, with effects that accumulated over time and were most visible in longitudinal designs. Negative emotions created self-reinforcing cycles of distress and underperformance, with the heaviest burden falling on students with weaker self-regulation skills. Convenience and flexibility enhanced effectiveness through temporal autonomy and cognitive resource optimization, but only for students with sufficient self-regulation to manage the increased autonomy.

The practical implication for post-pandemic higher education is that online learning is neither inherently superior nor inferior to in-person instruction—it is a medium whose effectiveness is determined by the design choices, resource conditions, and student characteristics that surround its implementation. The evidence reviewed here suggests that effective online learning requires: (1) adequate infrastructure as a baseline; (2) intentional instructional design that exploits rather than reproduces the online medium; (3) structured social interaction opportunities; (4) emotional support systems that monitor and address student well-being; and (5) strategic use of flexibility for students who can benefit from it, paired with scaffolding for those who need more structure. Future research should prioritize longitudinal and experimental designs, objective outcome measures, and individual-level analysis (rather than group means) to move beyond the methodological limitations that have constrained the pandemic-era evidence base.
