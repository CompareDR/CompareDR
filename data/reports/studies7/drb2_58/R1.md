# Systematic Review of the Effectiveness of Online Learning in Higher Education During the COVID-19 Pandemic

## Summary Table of Reviewed Studies

### Scope and coding approach

This review covers empirical studies published from January 1, 2020 through August 31, 2023 that investigated pandemic-era online, distance, or emergency-remote learning in higher education. Eligible studies had to report empirical student data and at least one outcome relevant to effectiveness, such as academic performance, perceived learning, satisfaction, engagement, competency development, or comparative effectiveness relative to face-to-face teaching. Reviews, commentaries, K–12 studies, and publications outside the specified period were excluded. The article and URLs expressly prohibited in the request were excluded from the research process and are not used or cited anywhere in this synthesis.

An important qualification is that much of the literature investigated emergency remote teaching rather than online courses deliberately designed from the outset for online delivery. Supriya et al., for example, explicitly distinguished established online programs from campus courses that abruptly moved online in spring 2020. This distinction matters because pandemic studies often measured the joint consequences of instructional modality, rushed course redesign, altered assessments, lockdown conditions, and psychological stress rather than a pure “online-versus-face-to-face” treatment. [1]

For the final column, Effective means the study reported predominantly positive learning/performance or satisfaction evidence; Ineffective means predominantly inferior effectiveness, learning, or satisfaction relative to face-to-face learning or a clearly negative evaluation; and Neutral means no significant difference or a genuinely mixed pattern in which benefits and disadvantages prevented a defensible positive or negative overall classification. This coding is an interpretive synthesis and should not be confused with a pooled effect size.

The studies are intentionally diverse in discipline and national context. Medical and health-professions education is prominent because the pandemic generated an unusually large empirical literature in those fields, but the evidence base also includes business, statistics, biology and general higher-education populations. The distinction between theoretical/cognitive learning and practical/clinical skill acquisition repeatedly emerges as an important moderator rather than merely a disciplinary difference. [19]

## Effectiveness Analysis

### What the evidence says

The most defensible overall conclusion is that pandemic-era online learning was academically viable but not uniformly educationally equivalent. It was frequently capable of maintaining—and in some cases improving—measurable academic performance in knowledge-oriented courses, while being substantially less successful at reproducing interaction, community, motivation, practical skills and the broader student experience associated with face-to-face education. [20]

A simple unweighted count of the 17 studies above gives four coded Effective, five Neutral and eight Ineffective. That count should not be interpreted as showing that online learning was generally ineffective, however, because the negative studies are disproportionately cross-sectional surveys measuring student perceptions, whereas the studies measuring comparative academic performance with stronger controls are overwhelmingly neutral or positive. A systematic review should therefore weight the type of evidence and outcome being measured, not merely count favorable and unfavorable papers.

Cross-sectional perceptual studies

Eleven of the reviewed studies primarily used cross-sectional student surveys: Adnan and Anwar; Abbasi et al.; Al-Balas et al.; Amir et al.; Dost et al.; Fatani; Aguilera-Hermida; Bączek et al.; Stoehr et al.; Gopal et al.; and Mortagy et al. Seven are coded ineffective, two neutral, and two effective. [21]

The recurring finding in this group is that students often preferred face-to-face instruction or perceived it to be more effective, especially for communication, engagement and practical learning. In the UK medical survey, students did not find online teaching as effective as face-to-face teaching and overwhelmingly doubted that it could replace clinical and practical training. Jordanian clinical students reported only 26.8% overall satisfaction. Pakistani health-sciences students also expressed predominantly negative perceptions. [22]

Yet the survey literature is not uniformly negative. Fatani's deliberately interactive, synchronous, case-based pediatric sessions achieved 82% high satisfaction, illustrating that “online” is not a single pedagogical treatment: an instructor-led case discussion designed around active participation is fundamentally different from a long one-way videoconference lecture or an uploaded slide deck. [8] Gopal et al. similarly found that instructor quality, course design and prompt feedback were strongly associated with satisfaction and perceived performance. [15]

Stoehr et al.'s multinational evidence exemplifies why a binary effective/ineffective judgment can be misleading. Students valued accessibility and flexibility and generally accepted online delivery of lectures, but reported insufficient peer interaction and social isolation, and considered digital delivery unsuitable for most bedside and practical training. Thus online instruction can simultaneously be effective for content transmission and ineffective for experiential components of the same degree program. [14]

Retrospective perception studies

Ives provides a particularly useful pre/post perceptual comparison: 1,731 students retrospectively evaluated their educational experience before and following the abrupt online transition, reporting deterioration in most quality dimensions, especially engagement. [11] This approach improves on simply asking whether students “like online learning,” because it establishes an explicit comparison, but it remains vulnerable to retrospective judgment: the pre-pandemic condition is remembered while the emergency condition is being experienced amid lockdown disruption.

Aguilera-Hermida's mixed-method survey reached a related result from a psychological perspective. Students' motivation, self-efficacy and cognitive engagement declined during emergency online learning, while attitudes, affect, perceived behavioral control and prior comfort with technology helped explain acceptance and engagement. [9]

Longitudinal and repeated-cohort comparative studies

Strict individual-level longitudinal panels were uncommon in the selected evidence. More common were repeated-cohort or historical-control quasi-experiments, in which 2020 online students were compared with students taking the same or similar courses before COVID-19.

These studies tell a markedly more favorable story than most perceptual surveys. Gonzalez et al. compared 458 Spanish students during lockdown with earlier academic-year cohorts and found significantly improved academic performance alongside evidence of more continuous study behavior. [2] El Said compared the same business course in a face-to-face 2019 cohort and fully online 2020 cohort and found no significant overall difference in course performance. [12] Zheng et al. compared U.S. dental courses delivered online in 2020 with their face-to-face predecessors and found that students in 16 of 17 courses were equally or more likely to receive an A. [13]

The major methodological warning comes from Supriya et al. Their enormous multi-year grade dataset showed a positive spring-2020 grade shift, yet almost 70% of students surveyed about an affected course perceived the remote transition as having harmed their learning. Instructors reported increasing flexibility and changing assessment practices, and the authors cautioned that the improvement in grades should not automatically be interpreted as an improvement in understanding. [23]

That finding exposes a major limitation of pandemic-era grade comparisons: grades are outcomes of both student learning and institutional assessment policy. During an emergency, instructors may extend deadlines, simplify assessments, allow different grading options, reduce workload or change examinations. Supriya et al. documented several such accommodations, so a stable or rising grade distribution can coexist with diminished understanding, interaction or preparation for subsequent courses. [1]

Accordingly, the four repeated-cohort/objective studies in this review divide into two Effective and two Neutral, with none clearly Ineffective on academic performance: Gonzalez and Zheng found favorable objective outcomes, while El Said found equivalence and Supriya found higher grades accompanied by sufficiently contradictory perception and assessment evidence to warrant a neutral/mixed classification. [24]

Randomized controlled evidence

Cheung et al. provides the strongest causal design among the included studies. 725 students across seven statistics courses were randomly assigned to online or face-to-face instruction, while instructional materials and other course conditions were kept comparable. There was no significant overall modality effect on the final weighted mark, examination, coursework, tests or assignments. [18]

That result is particularly important because randomization prevents many differences between ordinary online and in-person students from masquerading as modality effects. In a voluntary online program, for example, students who choose online study may differ systematically in employment, age, self-regulation or technological confidence; in historical comparisons, cohorts and assessment policies can change from year to year. Random assignment makes such explanations much less plausible. Cheung et al. therefore provides relatively strong evidence that, for the largely knowledge-based courses studied, online delivery itself did not inherently reduce academic learning. [18]

The RCT also contains an important qualification: face-to-face instruction performed better in classes with 25 or fewer students, suggesting that the value of physical co-presence may become more visible when class size is small enough for intensive instructor–student interaction. The overall null result should therefore not be generalized to every discipline, class size, or practical learning task. [18]

### Why methodology changes the answer

The apparent contradiction between “students say online learning is worse” and “controlled academic comparisons often show no loss” is not really a contradiction. The studies are answering different questions with different counterfactuals.

First, perceptual surveys commonly ask students to compare an ongoing emergency experience with their previous face-to-face experience. Dost et al. explicitly observed that students' low ratings might partly reflect comparison with prior in-person instruction and the sudden, inconsistent implementation of online teaching during the crisis. [7] Students were evaluating not only a delivery technology but also poor internet, reduced social contact, home distraction, diminished campus life, uncertainty about examinations and the loss of clinical or laboratory activity. Those factors naturally enter a judgment of whether “online learning was effective,” even when examination performance is maintained. [25]

Second, the dependent variable differs. Student surveys commonly measure satisfaction, engagement, interaction and perceived learning. Controlled studies more often measure test or course scores. These outcomes need not move together. Supriya et al. provides direct empirical evidence: grades rose while roughly 70% of students perceived that their learning had declined. [26] Bączek et al. similarly found no perceived disadvantage for knowledge acquisition but disadvantages for skills and social competence, showing that conclusions depend on which dimension is labeled “learning effectiveness.” [27]

Third, emergency implementation is confounded with modality in early surveys. Many universities had days or weeks to move courses online. By contrast, a randomized experiment can provide the same content, instructor expectations and assessments in both conditions, allowing the mode of delivery to be isolated more cleanly. The neutral result in Cheung et al. therefore addresses the causal question “What happens when comparable instruction is delivered online rather than face-to-face?” more directly than a survey asking “How satisfied were you with your university's abrupt COVID transition?” [18]

Fourth, objective historical comparisons have an opposite vulnerability: assessment adaptation can make performance look better than underlying learning actually was. Supriya et al. documented greater instructor flexibility and pandemic-era assessment changes; thus grade stability or increases should be treated more cautiously than performance on a standardized, unchanged assessment. [1]

Taken together, the evidence supports a two-part answer to the first research question:

For cognitive academic outcomes, online learning during COVID-19 was generally capable of producing outcomes comparable with face-to-face instruction, and some quasi-experimental studies found higher performance. The strongest randomized study found overall equivalence rather than inferiority. [28]

For the broader educational experience—including interaction, engagement, practical/clinical competence, social learning and students' subjective sense of learning—the pandemic implementation was frequently inferior. This distinction explains why a literature dominated numerically by negative perception surveys can coexist with neutral-to-positive controlled academic-performance evidence. [29]

The appropriate overall characterization is therefore conditional effectiveness rather than either universal success or failure. Online learning worked particularly well for theoretical content when infrastructure was reliable and teaching was intentionally designed for online participation; it worked substantially less well when courses depended on hands-on practice, intensive interpersonal exchange, or when online delivery amounted to hurriedly transferring conventional lectures to a digital platform. [30]

## Analysis of Influencing Factors

### Technology and instructional design

Infrastructure

Infrastructure was a foundational condition of effectiveness rather than merely a matter of convenience. Effective synchronous learning assumes that students can remain connected long enough to hear explanations, see demonstrations, download materials, submit assessments and interact in real time. When bandwidth is unreliable or devices are inadequate, the student does not simply experience lower satisfaction: portions of the instructional process become inaccessible.

Al-Balas et al. offers one of the clearest examples. Among 538 Jordanian clinical medical students who actually participated in pandemic distance learning, 69.1% identified internet streaming quality and coverage as the principal challenge, while overall satisfaction was only 26.8%. Smartphones were also commonly used to access instruction. [5] Unstable streaming can interrupt explanations and demonstrations, make synchronous discussion difficult, and transform an interactive session into fragmented passive exposure. In a clinical field, those deficiencies compound the inherent difficulty of replacing hands-on activity digitally. The same study consequently found much greater acceptance of online delivery for theoretical knowledge than for clinical skill development. [5]

Dost et al.'s much larger UK sample illustrates that infrastructure was not solely a lower-income-country problem. Poor internet connection was reported as a barrier by 21.53% of 2,721 students, alongside distractions and limitations on engagement. [31]

Amir et al. likewise identified unstable internet connections and financial cost among the challenges facing Indonesian dental students, while Adnan and Anwar concluded that technical and monetary barriers to internet access were central reasons compulsory online education struggled in Pakistan. [32]

The mechanism can therefore be expressed as:

poor connectivity/device access → interrupted or reduced participation → fewer opportunities for explanation, questions and feedback → greater cognitive and logistical burden → lower engagement and learning effectiveness.

Infrastructure also creates an equity mechanism. A student with a laptop, broadband and a private room receives a materially different educational treatment from one attempting the same synchronous course through intermittent mobile data in a shared household. The effectiveness of “online education” is therefore partly a property of students' technological environment, not only the instructional platform. The pronounced connectivity findings in Jordan, Pakistan, Indonesia and the UK demonstrate why average effects can conceal substantial variation between students. [33]

Instructional design

The evidence strongly indicates that instructional design moderated the effects of modality. Online learning was most successful when instructors redesigned learning activities around the affordances and limitations of online environments rather than merely transmitting a face-to-face lecture through a screen.

Gopal, Singh and Aggarwal's model is especially informative. Among 544 Indian university students, instructor quality, course design, prompt feedback and student expectations all positively influenced satisfaction, which in turn positively influenced perceived performance. [15] The mechanism is plausible within the study's own empirical model: clear organization reduces uncertainty, appropriately structured activities direct attention toward learning objectives, and timely feedback allows learners to identify and repair misunderstandings before they accumulate.

Fatani provides a concrete instructional example. Rather than relying on passive lectures, the study evaluated synchronous case-based discussions in pediatrics. Eighty-two percent of participants were highly satisfied, and the analysis emphasized teaching presence, cognitive presence and social presence as important to the positive experience. [8] Case discussion requires students to interpret information, respond and interact, thereby replacing some of the participation cues that disappear when a conventional classroom simply moves to videoconferencing.

Al-Balas et al. reinforces the same point from a less successful national implementation: satisfaction was significantly higher where instructors actively participated, used multimedia and devoted adequate time to the sessions. [5] Thus infrastructure establishes whether students can participate, but design determines what they are asked to do once connected.

Assessment design also matters. Supriya et al. found that many instructors increased flexibility and altered assessment practices after the emergency transition. Those accommodations probably helped prevent academic failure and may have improved equity, but they also weakened the interpretation of higher grades as evidence of greater learning. [1] Effective online course design therefore requires not merely accessible content but alignment between learning objectives, teaching activities and assessments; otherwise a course can generate good grades without establishing whether equivalent mastery has been achieved.

The design evidence helps reconcile the highly satisfied students in Fatani with the dissatisfied students in many national surveys. What mattered was not simply “Zoom versus classroom.” It was whether online teaching preserved active participation, coherent sequencing, feedback, instructor presence and opportunities to apply knowledge. [34]

### Social, emotional, and flexibility mechanisms

Lack of social interaction

Loss of social interaction was one of the most consistent negative influences on online-learning effectiveness, operating through several related mechanisms: weaker instructor feedback, fewer peer explanations, reduced accountability, diminished sense of belonging, and—particularly in professional education—the inability to practice interpersonal and physical skills in authentic settings.

Zheng, Bender and Lyon provide unusually direct evidence connecting interaction with effectiveness. In their 482-student U.S. dental-school study, engagement with faculty members and classmates predicted students' perceived effectiveness of online learning. At the same time, objective grades were largely maintained. [13] This combination suggests that interaction is particularly important to how students experience and make sense of learning even when formal course achievement remains stable.

Stoehr et al.'s 3,286-student, 12-country medical survey shows the scale of the social problem: 67% reported insufficient interaction with peers and 64% reported social isolation. Students nevertheless valued online lectures and flexibility, while only small minorities viewed digital delivery as suitable for bedside or practical teaching. [14] The findings demonstrate that social presence is not simply an optional satisfaction feature. In medicine, collaborative reasoning, discussion of cases, observation, patient interaction and supervised skills are integral parts of the learning task.

Dost et al. reported a similar boundary. Although online platforms allowed medical education to continue, 75.99% felt online teaching had not successfully replaced direct clinical teaching and 82.17% believed practical clinical skills could not be learned online. Students also mentioned lack of contact with colleagues and difficulty asking questions among the limitations. [7]

Supriya et al. provides another useful example outside medicine. Students reported negative effects not only on perceived learning but also on interactions with peers and instructors and their sense of belonging to the biology community. The study also noted that some lower-income students experienced particularly large reductions in time spent interacting with classmates. [26]

The mechanism is therefore broader than loneliness:

reduced synchronous and informal contact → fewer questions, explanations and corrective cues + reduced belonging/accountability → weaker engagement and collaborative knowledge construction → lower perceived learning and, for interaction-dependent tasks, lower actual instructional effectiveness.

This mechanism also explains Cheung et al.'s finding that face-to-face teaching gained an advantage in small classes, where physical co-presence can be translated into a greater amount of meaningful instructor–student interaction than in a mass lecture. [18]

Negative emotions

The pandemic combined educational disruption with anxiety, uncertainty, isolation and reduced motivation, meaning that emotional state often became part of the causal environment in which online learning operated.

Aguilera-Hermida found that motivation, self-efficacy and cognitive engagement declined after the transition to emergency online learning. The study's model incorporated attitude, affect, motivation and perceived behavioral control, showing that psychological readiness and confidence with technology were closely related to engagement and acceptance. [9]

Dost et al. likewise identified anxiety among reported barriers, alongside students' open-ended reports of diminished motivation and difficulty concentrating. [7] Ives' large U.S. survey found engagement to be among the dimensions most negatively affected following the pandemic transition. [35]

The likely learning pathway is that negative affect and low self-efficacy weaken motivation and self-regulation, which are especially important when the learner is physically separated from the instructor. A student in a conventional classroom receives external structure automatically through attendance, social cues and immediate instructor monitoring; remote learners must independently initiate tasks, sustain attention and regulate study schedules. Aguilera-Hermida's observed simultaneous declines in motivation, self-efficacy and cognitive engagement are consistent with this pathway. [9]

Care is necessary in causal interpretation. These studies generally cannot separate emotional consequences caused by online instruction from those caused by the pandemic itself. A student's anxiety in spring 2020 might have reflected health fears, economic problems or isolation as well as course design. This is another reason early pandemic perception surveys should not be read as pure estimates of the causal effect of online modality.

Convenience and flexibility

Convenience and flexibility were the most consistent advantages of pandemic online learning, and they help explain why students could simultaneously report lower social satisfaction and a desire to retain some online instruction.

In the UK survey, the major advantages included saving travel time, flexibility and the ability to learn at one's own pace. [7] In Jordan, Al-Balas et al. reported time saving and flexible classes among the dominant perceived benefits, despite the cohort's low overall satisfaction. [5] Across 12 countries, Stoehr et al. found that 84% valued flexibility, one of the strongest positive results in their survey. [14]

Flexibility improves effectiveness through several routes. Recorded or asynchronous materials can be paused, replayed and revisited; removing commuting reduces non-learning time; and time-shifting allows students to coordinate education with employment, caregiving, illness or different household circumstances. Supriya et al. found that some students specifically regarded recorded lectures and the ability to learn at their own pace as positive consequences of the remote transition. [36]

Supriya et al. also illustrates an important equity benefit. Students receiving need-based Pell grants were more likely to be working, and recorded materials could make attendance feasible when work or family responsibilities conflict with synchronous class schedules. [1] Flexibility can therefore increase access to instructional time, particularly for students whose lives do not fit a fixed campus timetable.

But flexibility has a trade-off. The same asynchronous arrangements that permit students to watch lectures later can reduce synchronous peer contact. Supriya et al. explicitly identified the need to balance asynchronous accessibility against opportunities for interaction. [36] Hence:

flexibility/self-paced access → lower logistical barriers + opportunities to replay and schedule learning → potentially greater access and persistence;

but, at high levels of asynchrony,

flexibility → less scheduled contact and external structure → possible declines in peer interaction, accountability and engagement. [37]

The apparent tension between flexibility and social interaction is one of the central findings of the pandemic evidence. The most promising model is therefore not simply “more online” or “more face-to-face,” but a design that uses online delivery for what it does especially well—accessible theoretical content, replayable resources and temporal flexibility—while deliberately protecting synchronous discussion, feedback, collaborative work and in-person practical experiences where these are pedagogically necessary. This interpretation is consistent with students' preference for blended approaches in the Jordanian study and with the simultaneous benefits and deficits observed across the multinational medical evidence. [38]

Taken across all five required factors, the pandemic literature supports a coherent causal framework: infrastructure determines access; instructional design determines the quality of learning activity; social interaction sustains feedback, belonging and experiential learning; emotional state influences motivation and self-regulation; and flexibility determines how easily students can fit learning into real-world constraints. The effectiveness of online higher education during COVID-19 was consequently not a fixed property of the delivery medium. It was the result of how these technological, pedagogical, social and psychological conditions interacted. [39]

The strongest answer to the review's two research questions is therefore: online learning was reasonably effective at preserving cognitive academic achievement, especially in well-designed theoretical courses, but much less consistently effective as a complete substitute for the social, practical and experiential functions of campus education. Its success depended heavily on reliable infrastructure, purposeful course design and feedback, preservation of meaningful interaction, students' emotional and motivational capacity, and the intelligent use of the flexibility that digital delivery makes possible. The randomized evidence that academic scores can remain equivalent, coupled with the extensive survey evidence of impaired interaction and practical learning, suggests that the pandemic did not demonstrate either the failure or the superiority of online higher education; rather, it demonstrated where online learning works, where it does not, and which design conditions determine the difference. [40]
