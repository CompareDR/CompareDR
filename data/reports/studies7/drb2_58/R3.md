# A Systematic Review of the Effectiveness of Online Learning in Higher Education During the COVID-19 Pandemic (January 2020 – August 2023)

**TL;DR**

- Online/emergency remote learning during COVID-19 was, on balance, **"neutral-to-mixed but not catastrophic"** for higher education: the strongest causal evidence (randomized trials) shows small negative effects on genuine learning, while most perceptual surveys and grade-based comparisons show equal-or-better results — a divergence driven mostly by methodology and assessment changes, not by learning itself.
- The single most important lesson is that **how effectiveness was measured determined the answer**: RCTs and objective assessments tend to find modest losses; self-report surveys and pandemic-era grade comparisons (often inflated by open-book/unproctored exams) tend to find gains.
- Effectiveness was strongly moderated by five factors — **infrastructure, instructional design, social interaction, negative emotions, and convenience/flexibility** — such that well-designed, well-resourced online learning approached parity with in-person teaching, while under-resourced "emergency remote teaching" did not.

## SECTION 2: Effectiveness Analysis

### Overall conclusion

Synthesizing across more than 20 primary empirical studies plus several meta-analyses (the *Interactive Learning Environments* (2024) meta-analysis alone pooled 35 studies from 17 countries/regions covering 8,079 students — 4,160 online and 3,919 face-to-face), the evidence does **not** support a simple verdict that online learning "worked" or "failed." Instead, the most defensible conclusion is that **online learning during the pandemic was roughly comparable to face-to-face instruction on measured outcomes, but with a small genuine learning penalty that was masked or reversed in most non-experimental studies by changes in assessment and by self-selection.** When learning is measured with the cleanest tools (randomized assignment plus consistent assessment), online instruction produced small but real deficits; when measured with grades or student self-reports during a period of relaxed assessment security, it looked equal or better.

### Why the answer depends on methodology

**1. Randomized controlled trials / experiments (the causal gold standard).** These consistently find small negative effects of fully online instruction. The Kofoed, Gebhart, Gilmore & Moschitto (2024, *AER: Insights* 6(3):324–40) West Point RCT randomized 551 students in a required introductory economics course across 12 instructors to online or in-person sections; final grades for online students "dropped by 0.215 standard deviations, a result apparent in both assignments and exams and largest for academically at-risk students," and the post-course survey found online students "struggled to concentrate in class and felt less connected to their instructors and peers." The pre-pandemic Alpert, Couch & Harmon (2016) RCT found purely online students scored 5–10 points lower on a cumulative final, while *blended* students were statistically indistinguishable from face-to-face. [American Economic Association][ref-01] The Hong Kong statistics RCT (2023, 725 students) found no significant difference overall, with class size as a moderator. Because randomization removes self-selection, these results isolate the effect of the modality itself — modestly negative for pure online, roughly neutral for blended.

**2. Cross-sectional comparative studies on objective grades.** These frequently find equal or better results — but largely for artifactual reasons. The Gonzalez et al. (2020, *PLOS ONE*) Spanish natural experiment and Iglesias-Pradas et al. (2021) Madrid study both found significantly *higher* performance during confinement; [PLOS][ref-02] the large Italian archival study (30,731 students, 246,416 exams) found online exam pass probability *rose* even as face-to-face pass probability fell. The critical confounds are **grade inflation and assessment-format changes**: many institutions moved to open-book, unproctored, or extended-window online exams. The anatomy ERT study saw exam grades rise ~20% even as students reported *lower* learning and confidence. The magnitude of exam-format inflation is now well documented: Newton (2023, *PNAS*) reports grade inflation on the order of g = 0.4 overall (rising to ≈0.7 for multiple-choice-heavy exams), and Hwang & Lee (2022, *Journal of Emerging Investigators*) found online test scores inflated by roughly 20–50%, versus differences of only 6–8% between comparable in-person exams. Thus higher pandemic grades often reflect easier assessment conditions rather than more learning.

**3. Cross-sectional perceptual/self-report surveys.** These are the most abundant and the most variable, and they are especially vulnerable to **recall/comparison bias** and **confounding from pandemic stressors**. When students compare emergency online learning against their remembered pre-pandemic classroom experience, they typically rate it worse (Bangladesh; Chinese national survey; Jordan) — but this conflates the *modality* with the *trauma of the pandemic*: isolation, bereavement, financial precarity, and technology stress. Conversely, surveys emphasizing logistics (flexibility, recorded lectures, commute savings) skew positive. Faculty surveys (Almahasees et al. 2021; multinational faculty surveys) similarly show ambivalence: convenience acknowledged, effectiveness doubted.

**4. Longitudinal / pre-post studies.** These give a middle-ground, trajectory-sensitive picture. The very large Lubner, Farrell, Perry, Stimers & Berlino (2025, *Higher Education Studies* 15(2):365–380) study of 209,073 U.S. business students across 215 colleges found that on standardized outbound exam scores, "college students' performance statistically increased through the disruption, adaptation, and new normal periods," with significant improvements in five of six degree-modality combinations — a striking contrast to K-12 "learning loss." Yet Whitelaw et al. (2024, *South African Journal of Economics*) found that "performance gains made in 2020 are reversed in 2021, with performance dropping relative to pre-pandemic trends," and concluded that "the improvements observed in 2020 did not reflect true learning gains" — a warning that short-term grade bumps did not durably translate into learning.

### Reconciling the divergence

The apparent contradiction dissolves once four mechanisms are recognized:

- **Recall/comparison bias:** self-reports benchmark against an idealized in-person past and against pandemic misery, biasing perceptions negative.
- **Self-selection:** non-randomized comparisons let motivated/well-resourced students cluster in modalities that suit them, inflating apparent online success.
- **Assessment change:** open-book/unproctored/extended online exams mechanically inflate scores independent of learning (Newton 2023; Hwang & Lee 2022); this is why grade-based studies look rosier than RCTs.
- **"Emergency remote teaching" ≠ well-designed online learning:** the pandemic delivered hastily improvised remote instruction, not the deliberately designed asynchronous+synchronous courses that pre-pandemic literature and meta-analyses (Interactive Learning Environments 2024, g = 0.336; Ulum 2022, 27 studies/1,772 students across USA, Taiwan, Turkey, China, Philippines, Ireland, Georgia) associate with parity or gains. Studies that layered genuine instructional design onto the online shift (Thai interactive case-based course; Japanese combined course) recovered or exceeded baseline performance.

The bottom line: **online learning can be as effective as in-person learning when well-designed and well-resourced, but the emergency version deployed in 2020–2021 carried a small genuine learning penalty that objective, randomized evidence detects and that grade-based studies obscure.**

## SECTION 3: Analysis of Influencing Factors

### (a) Infrastructure — internet connectivity, devices, and the digital divide

**Mechanism:** Reliable connectivity and adequate devices are the *precondition* for any learning transaction online; when absent, they truncate synchronous participation, block access to materials, disrupt assessments, and impose cognitive/emotional load (fear of disconnection) that competes with learning. Infrastructure gaps also map onto socioeconomic status, converting a technical problem into an equity problem.

**Named empirical examples:**

- **Chisadza, Clance, Mthembu, Nicholls & Yitbarek (2021, *African Development Review* 33(S1):S114–S125, University of Pretoria, South Africa):** using pre/post assessment grades plus survey data, the authors state, "We find that students' performance was positively associated with good wifi access, relative to using mobile internet data … the main contributor to lower academic performance in the online setting was poor internet access." This directly demonstrates the mechanism: bandwidth *quality*, not just access, drives outcomes.
- **Indonesia digital-divide case study (Hidayah, *Humaniora*, 2022):** qualitative fieldwork across Jakarta, Bali, Central Java, Riau, and Yogyakarta documented layered divides — unequal device access/control and varying digital skills — showing that "access" is multidimensional and that connectivity instability in a large archipelago was a primary barrier. [ResearchGate][ref-03]

Developing-country surveys (Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nigeria/Ghana) reinforce the pattern: costly mobile data, unreliable electricity, and shared devices systematically depressed engagement and satisfaction.

### (b) Instructional design — course design, interactivity, synchrony, assessment, teacher digital competence

**Mechanism:** Design determines whether the medium supports the social, cognitive, and teaching presence that learning requires. Interactive, active-learning designs create engagement and feedback loops; passive lecture-dumping does not. Synchronous elements enable real-time feedback and community; asynchronous elements enable pacing and review — the strongest results come from blending them. Assessment design determines whether measured "effectiveness" reflects learning or format artifacts. Teacher digital competence determines execution quality.

**Evidence:** The KSOM/RVUCOM case series (2023, *Cureus*) is a natural design experiment: the school relying on small-group interactive PBL saw exam performance drop (55% vs. 77% above the national average), while the school using structured pre-recorded large-group didactics maintained performance — one pedagogy transferred to the online medium more cleanly than the other. The Thai interactive case-based study (2024) and the Japanese combined-course study (2023) show that adding deliberate interactivity/blending recovers or improves outcomes. Alpert et al. (2016) found *blended* design matched face-to-face while pure online lagged. [American Economic Association][ref-01] Controlled instruction studies show synchronous instruction with active-learning strategies outperforming passive lecture. Assessment design is pivotal: open-book/unproctored formats inflated scores substantially (Newton 2023; Hwang & Lee 2022), so design choices literally changed the "effectiveness" signal.

### (c) Lack of social interaction — peer and instructor interaction, community, isolation

**Mechanism:** Interaction drives engagement, motivation, belonging, and persistence; its absence produces disconnection that depresses both well-being and learning. Student–student and student–teacher interaction correlate strongly with achievement and satisfaction, and belonging predicts engagement and retention. Online settings — especially asynchronous or camera-off ones — attenuate the social cues and spontaneous exchange that sustain these processes.

**Named empirical examples:**

- **Kofoed et al. (2024, West Point RCT):** the post-course survey found online students "felt less connected to their instructors and peers" and struggled to concentrate — accompanying the 0.215-SD grade decline. Because assignment was randomized, this links reduced interaction causally to worse outcomes.
- **Chinese serial-mediation study (Yin et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2021; 1,504 students across five provinces):** found that **interaction → academic self-efficacy → student engagement → online-learning satisfaction**, a significant serial mediation explaining 34.6% of satisfaction variance — showing the precise psychological pathway by which interaction sustains effective online learning.

Qualitative work (Indonesia; Asian international students) corroborates that lost social cues increased anxiety and impeded participation.

### (d) Negative emotions — anxiety, stress, depression, boredom, burnout, motivation loss

**Mechanism:** Negative affect consumes working-memory and attentional resources, undermines self-regulation, reduces motivation and persistence, and lowers satisfaction — all of which degrade learning. Pandemic stressors (health fear, isolation, financial strain) compounded modality-specific stressors (technostress, online anxiety).

**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of remote-learning students during COVID-19 (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2022) found high prevalence of anxiety, depression, and stress; cross-sectional studies in Saudi Arabia and Brazil linked depression/anxiety to worse educational behavior and self-rated performance. On boredom specifically, **Ranđelović & Opsenica Kostić (2022, Serbia, n=198)** found academic boredom significantly predicted attention discontinuity (β = .463), lingering off-task thoughts (β = .409), and social-media distraction (β = .416), while *negatively* predicting adaptive behavioral self-regulation (β = –.246) — a direct mechanistic chain from boredom to disengagement, summarized in their words: "students who get bored during online classes are less motivated and achieve poorer academic results." Motivation loss operates similarly: engagement and motivation predict achievement, and amotivation depresses it.

### (e) Convenience / flexibility — time savings, self-pacing, recorded lectures, accessibility

**Mechanism:** Flexibility lowers logistical barriers (commute, scheduling), enables self-paced review (pause/rewind recorded lectures), widens accessibility for students juggling work/family, and can improve autonomous-learning habits — plausibly the mechanism behind Gonzalez et al. (2020) finding that confinement pushed students toward more continuous study and higher scores. [Semantic Scholar][ref-04]

**Evidence:** Across surveys (Jordan; UK; India medical students; a Times Higher Education panel of 2,217 respondents from 120+ countries), flexibility and recorded-lecture access were the most-cited advantages — 45% named viewing lectures at any time and 34% self-pacing, and 85% wanted recorded lectures retained. However, flexibility is double-edged: the same self-pacing invites procrastination (Indian medical students watched <50% of recorded lectures), and asynchronous convenience can erode the interaction and structure that sustain learning — which is why flexibility boosts *satisfaction* more reliably than it boosts *measured achievement*.

**Stage 1 — Immediate (institution/instructor level):**

- Prioritize **blended over pure-online** designs; the evidence (Alpert et al.; Japanese combined course; meta-analyses) shows blended matches face-to-face while pure online lags.
- Combine synchronous interaction (for community, feedback, and concentration) with asynchronous recorded content (for flexible review). Do not simply stream lectures.
- Redesign assessment for validity, not just security: use frequent low-stakes, application-oriented, or project-based tasks rather than relying on high-stakes unproctored exams that inflate scores (g ≈ 0.4–0.7; Newton 2023) without reflecting learning.

**Stage 2 — Structural (system/policy level):**

- Close infrastructure gaps first: subsidize devices/bandwidth and provide campus connectivity hubs, because connectivity quality (Chisadza et al.) causally gates outcomes and equity.
- Invest in **faculty digital-pedagogy training**; the KSOM/RVUCOM contrast shows execution quality, not the medium, drives divergent results.
- Build deliberate community/belonging structures (cohorts, peer groups, proactive outreach) to counter the interaction deficit that RCT evidence ties to lower achievement.
- Integrate mental-health support into online provision, given the measured links from anxiety/depression/boredom to disengagement.

**Benchmarks that would change these recommendations:**

- If future RCTs of *well-designed* (not emergency) online courses show effect sizes near zero, the case for pure-online expands.
- If assessment can be made cheat-resistant and format-neutral (validated remote proctoring or authentic assessment), grade-based comparisons become trustworthy and could be weighted more heavily.
- If infrastructure parity is achieved in a given setting, the digital-divide caveat can be relaxed and attention should shift to design and interaction.

## Caveats

- **Assessment-format confound is pervasive:** most positive grade-based findings are contaminated by open-book/unproctored/extended-window exams (inflation of g ≈ 0.4, up to ~20–50% on some tests); treat "higher grades during COVID" as weak evidence of "more learning."
- **Emergency remote teaching ≠ online learning:** 2020–2021 conditions were improvised; findings may understate the potential of purpose-built online courses and overstate the medium's inherent weaknesses.
- **Publication and self-selection biases** affect non-randomized studies; perceptual surveys additionally suffer recall/comparison bias and pandemic-stressor confounding.
- **Discipline matters:** practical/clinical/lab-based fields (anatomy, nursing skills) suffered more than theory-based fields; results are not uniformly generalizable.
- **Some cited items are conference proceedings or preprints** (the Serbian boredom study is an INPACT proceedings paper; the KSOM/RVUCOM case series was preprinted before publication) and should be weighted accordingly.
- A few source details (exact author lists for the Italian archival study, the Chinese national survey, and the *Interactive Learning Environments* meta-analysis) were available only via secondary indexing at the time of writing and should be verified against the primary articles.
