# Auto-Scaling in Cloud Computing: Technology Classification, Challenges, and Solutions

Auto-scaling technologies divide cleanly into **reactive methods** (threshold/rule-based, control-theoretic, queuing-theory, and reinforcement-learning approaches that act after a monitored condition changes) and **proactive/predictive methods** (time-series, machine-/deep-learning forecasting, and hybrids that scale ahead of predicted demand) — and the strongest real-world and research systems combine both. Below, each technical method is explained by its working principle, advantages, and limitations, followed by five key challenge areas each grounded in a specific, verified published study.

**Scope note:** This report is built entirely from independent primary sources (IEEE, ACM, Springer, Elsevier, arXiv, and official AWS/Azure/Kubernetes/KEDA documentation). It deliberately excludes one specific 2024 survey and all of its mirrors, drawing instead on the underlying primary studies.

## TL;DR

- **Two families, one loop:** Reactive auto-scalers (thresholds, control theory, queuing theory, RL) react after metrics move and dominate commercial products for their simplicity and accuracy; proactive auto-scalers (ARIMA, LSTM/GRU, hybrids) forecast demand to scale ahead of time. Both run inside a Monitor–Analyze– Plan–Execute (MAPE) loop and can scale horizontally (instances/replicas) or vertically (CPU/memory).
- **Best practice is hybrid:** No single method wins everywhere — pure reactive lags behind bursts and startup delays, pure predictive fails on unseen/irregular workloads, so production systems (AWS Predictive + dynamic scaling; Kubernetes HPA/VPA/Cluster Autoscaler + KEDA; research systems like ARIMA-PID and LSTM+RL) blend responsiveness with foresight.
- **Challenges are context-specific:** Energy efficiency (SLA-vs-power trade-off in VM consolidation), the dynamic bursty nature of telecom/NFV service chains, the stateful fragility of big-data frameworks, the pay-per-resource cost of iterative graph processing, and the accuracy ceiling of workload prediction each require tailored solutions — demonstrated by published systems that report large, concrete gains (e.g., ElasticSFC cutting SLA violations from 72.97% to 0.03%; a heterogeneity-aware graph autoscaler cutting cost >50%).

# Auto-Scaling Technology Classification

Auto-scaling (elasticity) is the automatic, on-demand provisioning and de-provisioning of cloud resources to match fluctuating workload while honoring Service Level Agreements (SLAs) and minimizing cost. By anticipation capability, auto-scalers fall into two broad classes: **reactive** methods, which allocate/deallocate resources only after a monitored condition crosses a threshold, and **proactive (predictive)** methods, which forecast future demand and scale ahead of time. Reactive scaling is simple, accurate, and dominant in commercial products; proactive scaling is more advanced but depends on prediction quality. Most production systems blend the two.

The auto-scaling decision loop is typically framed as MAPE (Monitor–Analyze–Plan–Execute). Scaling can be **horizontal** (add/remove instances/replicas — "scale out/in") or **vertical** (resize CPU/memory of an instance — "scale up/down").

## Reactive Methods

### 1. Threshold / rule-based scaling

**Working principle.** The operator defines rules on performance metrics (e.g., CPU utilization, request rate, queue length). When a metric exceeds an upper threshold for a defined period, a scale-out action fires; below a lower threshold, scale-in fires. Thresholds may be **static** (fixed values) or **dynamic** (adjusted at runtime). This is the mechanism behind AWS EC2 Auto Scaling target-tracking/step/simple policies, Azure autoscale rules, and Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA). In HPA, the controller computes desiredReplicas = ceil\[currentReplicas × (currentMetricValue / desiredMetricValue)\]. Per the Kubernetes documentation, scaling is skipped within "the default cluster-wide tolerance of 10%" (set via the kube-controller-manager --horizontal-pod-autoscaler-tolerance flag, default 0.1); Kubernetes v1.33 made this tolerance configurable per-object via the HPAConfigurableTolerance feature gate. RightScale historically combined per-VM rules with a voting scheme: if a majority of VMs vote to scale, the action is taken.

**Advantages.** Simple and intuitive; easy to understand and configure; low computational overhead; accurate and high-performing for well-understood workloads; broadly supported by every major cloud/orchestrator.

**Limitations.** Purely reactive — resources are provisioned only after load has already risen, and VM start-up (≈5–10 minutes historically) introduces latency and performance degradation. Setting good thresholds requires expert knowledge and must be re-tuned per application/workload. Poor at handling sudden bursts; can oscillate; each metric usually handled independently.

### 2. Control-theory-based approaches

**Working principle.** The system is modeled as a dynamical process; a controller drives a controlled output (e.g., response time, CPU load) toward a reference (the SLA target) by adjusting an input (number of VMs/containers). **Open-loop** controllers choose actions from inputs alone; **closed-loop (feedback)** controllers use the error (difference between actual and desired output) to correct the next action. **PID** controllers combine Proportional, Integral, and Derivative terms; controller "gains" set the magnitude of scaling change. Gains can be **fixed** or **adaptive** (self-tuned online). Example: Baarzi & Kesidis (2021) used PID control with eBPF-collected metrics for combined vertical/horizontal microservice scaling.

**Advantages.** Strong mathematical foundation; low decision latency; feedback naturally corrects errors and disturbances; adaptive/self-tuning gains handle changing conditions; well suited to keeping SLA metrics near a set-point.

**Limitations.** Requires a reliable performance model, which is hard to obtain and often low in scalability/fidelity; non-adaptive (fixed-gain) controllers perform poorly on slowly varying workloads and cannot find good gains without a model; many actuations may be needed on the physical system to stabilize; often application/architecture-specific, limiting extendability; struggles with stochastic behavior, noisy measurements, and actuation delay.

### 3. Queuing-theory-based models

**Working principle.** The application/system is modeled as a queue (or network of queues) in A/S/C (Kendall) notation — e.g., M/M/C: Poisson arrivals, exponential service, C servers. From arrival rate, service rate, and desired response time or queue length, the model computes the number of servers/instances needed to meet the SLA. For multi-tier apps, queuing networks model each component. It is frequently used as the resource-estimation engine feeding both reactive and proactive controllers.

**Advantages.** Analytically grounded, interpretable estimates of required capacity; directly links latency/queue-length SLAs to resource counts; improves resource utilization and QoS; can identify energy-efficient operating points.

**Limitations.** Classical queuing theory assumes stationary systems, so it poorly captures the non-stationary dynamics of real clouds; solving complex queuing networks is computationally heavy; accuracy depends on correct distribution assumptions. Often combined with control theory, thresholds, or Markov Decision Processes to compensate.

### 4. Reinforcement learning (RL) / Q-learning

**Working principle.** The auto-scaler is an agent that observes a state (e.g., CPU utilization band, workload, current replicas), takes an action (scale out/in/no-op), and receives a reward reflecting SLA compliance and cost. Over time it learns an optimal scaling policy. **Q-learning** is off-policy, updating Q-values toward the best next action; **SARSA** is on-policy, using the action actually taken. Extensions include Deep Q-Networks (DQN), Dyna-Q+, and fuzzy RL (Fuzzy Q-Learning / Fuzzy SARSA). RL requires no a-priori system model and learns online. (Although RL learns from history and can be used proactively, it is classically grouped under model-free/experience-driven methods.)

**Advantages.** No need for an explicit performance model; learns and adapts to the specific application/environment online; handles decision-making under uncertainty; can outperform threshold policies (which are often set too tightly); can auto-tune thresholds.

**Limitations.** Slow convergence and long learning/exploration phase (poor early performance); large state– action spaces cause scalability problems (table explosion), motivating tile coding/function approximation; deep RL adds sample inefficiency and computational cost; reward design and system integration are non-trivial.

## Proactive (Predictive) Methods

### 5. Time-series analysis and statistical forecasting (ARIMA, ARMA/SARIMA, exponential smoothing, moving average)

**Working principle.** Historical metric values (CPU, request rate) are modeled as a time series to forecast H steps ahead; the forecast drives advance provisioning. **ARIMA(p,d,q)** combines autoregression, differencing (for stationarity), and moving-average terms; **SARIMA** adds seasonality; exponential smoothing and moving averages weight recent observations. The predicted load is then translated (often via queuing theory or a ratio rule) into a resource count.

**Advantages.** Well-established, relatively easy to build and computationally efficient; captures trend and seasonality; enables scaling before booting delays hit; effective for recurring/periodic patterns.

**Limitations.** Assumes largely linear/stationary structure, so accuracy degrades on non-stationary, bursty, non-linear traffic; ARIMA prediction accuracy worsens as the number of steps ahead increases (reported moderate accuracy on cloud workloads); requires periodic re-fitting.

### 6. Machine-learning and deep-learning prediction (regression, SVM/SVR, neural networks, LSTM/GRU, ensembles)

**Working principle.** Supervised models learn a mapping from historical/multivariate features to future workload or resource demand. Linear/support-vector regression capture simpler relations; feed-forward neural networks and evolutionary-trained networks capture non-linearities; **LSTM/GRU** recurrent networks learn long-range temporal dependencies; CNN-LSTM/Bi-LSTM and ensembles capture multi-scale patterns. The forecast feeds the scaling controller. AWS EC2/ECS **Predictive Scaling** is the productized industry version: per the AWS EC2 Auto Scaling User Guide, "a minimum of 24 hours of data is required to create the initial forecasts. However, having a full 14 days of historical data results in more accurate forecasts," producing "an hourly forecast for the next 48 hours." AWS also increased forecast frequency — its Jan 6, 2023 announcement states predictive scaling now "produces predictive scaling policy forecasts 4x per day, up from 1x per day... Reducing our forecast interval from 24 hours to 6 hours produces more accurate predictive scaling policies." Predictive scaling is combined with dynamic scaling for unpredicted spikes.

**Advantages.** Higher accuracy on complex, non-linear, bursty workloads than statistical models (LSTM reported to raise accuracy meaningfully over ARIMA); learns multivariate interactions; captures long-term dependencies; supports multi-step forecasting; productized and mature in industry.

**Limitations.** Requires large training data and offline/continuous retraining; deep models are computationally heavy with long training times (an issue on resource-constrained edge nodes); risk of concept drift, cold-start, and overfitting; no model guarantees perfect accuracy, so forecast error propagates to scaling errors; deployment in real-time control loops can be costly.

### 7. Hybrid approaches

**Working principle.** Combine reactive and proactive components so the proactive part plans for predictable demand while the reactive part catches unpredicted spikes; or combine statistical + ML models (e.g., ARIMA for linear trend + LSTM on residuals). Examples: FLAS (predictive time-series + reactive high-level-metric estimation); RHAS (robust hybrid for web apps); SVM-based proactive + reactive rules for SLA-constrained clouds; ARIMA-PID (ARIMA forecast + PID control); ARIMA-OLSTM with a Pelican Optimization Algorithm for scaling decisions; LSTM+RL (LSTM predicts requests, RL picks the scaling action).

**Advantages.** Combines responsiveness (reactive) with foresight (proactive); reduces forecast error impact on unseen workloads; empirically outperforms pure-reactive or pure-proactive in most cases (e.g., LSTM+RL reduced SLA violations by 10–30% vs other approaches).

**Limitations.** Greater architectural and tuning complexity; inherits ML data/retraining demands; more components to integrate and observe.

### Industry practice (real-world grounding)

- **Kubernetes** offers HPA (replica count, reactive), VPA (right-sizing requests/limits), and Cluster Autoscaler (adds/removes nodes reactively when pods can't schedule). Kubernetes v1.33 made HPA tolerance configurable per-object.
- **KEDA** — per its official site (keda.sh), offers a "catalog of 70+ built-in scalers for various cloud platforms, databases, messaging systems, telemetry systems, CI/CD, and more" (Kafka lag, queue length, Prometheus queries, Azure/AWS/GCP queues), supports **scale-to-zero**, and even offers AI-based predictive scaling via PredictKube. KEDA graduated in the CNCF on August 22, 2023.
- **AWS Auto Scaling** offers target-tracking/step/simple (reactive) plus ML-based **Predictive Scaling** (EC2 since 2018; ECS since Nov 2024). AWS's own documentation states predictive scaling can "potentially save you money on your EC2 bill by helping you avoid the need to over provision capacity" and provides a "ForecastOnly" evaluation mode; a widely cited secondary/marketing figure puts the savings at "up to 15% on EC2 costs" (awsforengineers.com), which should be validated per-workload rather than assumed.

### Comparison table — methods at a glance

| Method | Class | Core principle | Advantages | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Threshold/rule-based | Reactive | Fire scaling when metric crosses threshold | Simple, low overhead, universal | After-the-fact; tuning-heavy; startup lag |
| Control theory (PID) | Reactive (adaptive can be hybrid) | Feedback drives output to SLA set-point | Rigorous; low latency; self-correcting | Needs model; gain tuning; noise/delay sensitive |
| Queuing theory | Reactive/estimation | Model as queue; compute needed servers | Interpretable capacity math; QoS-linked | Assumes stationarity; heavy for networks |
| RL / Q-learning | Reactive/learning | Learn scaling policy via reward | No model; adapts online | Slow convergence; state explosion |
| Time-series (ARIMA) | Proactive | Forecast future load statistically | Efficient; captures seasonality | Poor on non-linear/bursty; multi-step decay |
| ML/DL (LSTM etc.) | Proactive | Learn non-linear demand forecast | High accuracy; long dependencies | Data/retraining cost; drift; heavy |
| Hybrid | Both | Combine predictive plan + reactive catch | Best empirical results | Complexity; integration cost |

# Main Challenges and Solutions

## Energy efficiency

**Problem manifestation.** Data centers consume a large and growing share of global electricity, and idle servers waste power. As Meisner, Gold & Wenisch document in "PowerNap: eliminating server idle power" (ACM SIGARCH Computer Architecture News, Vol. 37, No. 1, ASPLOS 2009), in typical deployments server utilization is below 30%, yet idle servers still consume 60% of their peak power draw. Naïve auto-scaling that leaves under-utilized VMs running, or that scales aggressively, either wastes energy or causes SLA violations. The central difficulty is the **energy–performance trade-off**: consolidating workloads onto fewer active hosts (turning idle ones off) saves energy but aggressive VM migration/consolidation causes performance degradation and SLA violations.

**Specific study & solution.** **"Autonomous DRL-based energy efficient VM consolidation for cloud data centers"** (Sustainable Computing: Informatics and Systems, Elsevier, 2022) applies deep reinforcement learning to dynamic VM consolidation — detecting under/over-loaded hosts, selecting VMs to migrate, and placing them to minimize the number of active servers while respecting SLA. The RL agent learns migration/placement policies that jointly optimize energy and SLA rather than using fixed heuristics, reporting improved energy efficiency and reduced SLA violations versus heuristic baselines. A complementary primary study, **E2SVM (PLoS ONE, 2024)**, adds SLA-aware adaptive-threshold host detection plus energy-aware VM selection/placement to stabilize the energy-vs-SLA-violation trade-off.

## Dynamic nature of telecom services

**Problem manifestation.** In NFV-based telecom clouds, services are built as **Service Function Chains (SFCs)** — ordered sequences of Virtual Network Functions (firewall, load balancer, IDS, etc.) that all matching traffic must traverse in order. Telecom traffic is highly dynamic and bursty; when any VNF in the chain becomes overloaded, the whole chain's end-to-end latency SLA is breached. Prior approaches typically scaled only compute OR only network, and focused on VNF placement rather than run-time auto-scaling under changing load.

**Specific study & solution.** **ElasticSFC** — Adel Nadjaran Toosi, Jungmin Son, Qinghua Chi & Rajkumar Buyya, *"ElasticSFC: Auto-scaling techniques for elastic service function chaining in network functions virtualization-based clouds,"* Journal of Systems and Software, Vol. 152, June 2019, pp. 108–119 (DOI 10.1016/j.jss.2019.02.052). ElasticSFC is a heuristic auto-scaler that unifies **horizontal + vertical VNF scaling, VNF migration, dynamic bandwidth allocation, and dynamic flow scheduling** in one framework, integrated with an ETSI-NFV MANO stack and an SDN controller. It runs every 60 s, comparing measured end-to-end latency against the SLA (minus a slack margin); over-utilized links trigger bandwidth scaling or flow re-routing/migration, and over-utilized VNFs trigger vertical scale-up then horizontal scale-out. Evaluated in an extended CloudSimSDN simulator using real 24-hour German Wikipedia traces (~29 million requests) across a 3-tier app with 4 SFC policies, **ElasticSFC reduced SLA violations to 0.03% versus 72.97% with no scaling**, while keeping average response time at 0.96 s and dynamically releasing resources (bandwidth dropped from 2 MB/s back to 0.5 MB/s once demand fell) to cut cost versus permanent over-provisioning.

## Big data processing

**Problem manifestation.** Big-data frameworks (Hadoop/MapReduce, Spark) are **distributed and stateful**, which makes safe run-time scaling hard: scaling down can lose in-progress state or HDFS data, and node failures/resource contention are common in the cloud, severely impacting job completion and SLA. Default rule-based autoscalers (e.g., Spark dynamic allocation) are reactive and configuration-heavy, often over-provisioning on shuffle-heavy/skewed jobs. Predicting job runtime and required resources accurately is the core difficulty.

**Specific study & solution.** **Gandhi, Thota, Dube, Kochut & Zhang, "Autoscaling for Hadoop Clusters," IEEE IC2E 2016** (building on their ICAC 2015 work). They develop novel **gray-box performance models** that relate Hadoop job execution time to resource allocation and workload parameters, [IEEE Xplore][ref-01] separately modeling Map-stage and Reduce-stage times [Stony Brook University][ref-02] and tuning the generic models via regression on workload profiles (WordCount, TeraSort, K-means). The model then computes the resources needed to meet a user-specified response-time SLA, [IEEE Xplore][ref-01] and the autoscaler issues commands to the Hadoop and OpenStack APIs [Stony Brook University][ref-03] — even handling node failures and multi-job contention. Validation on an OpenStack Hadoop cluster showed accurate, model-driven scaling that met SLAs and delivered significant resource savings without compromising performance. [IEEE Xplore][ref-04]

## Cost-effective graph processing

**Problem manifestation.** Large-scale iterative graph processing (social networks, IoT) is inefficient on MapReduce, so Pregel-like frameworks (Giraph) are used — but on pay-as-you-go clouds, **scalability is directly tied to monetary cost**. Most graph frameworks were built for commodity clusters that aren't charged per-resource, and frameworks like iGiraph only used **homogeneous** VMs, [unimelb][ref-05] missing the opportunity to exploit cloud resource heterogeneity to cut cost as a convergent graph algorithm's active workload shrinks over iterations.

**Specific study & solution.** **Heidari & Buyya, "A Cost-Efficient Auto-Scaling Algorithm for Large-Scale Graph Processing in Cloud Environments with Heterogeneous Resources," IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, Vol. 47, No. 8, Aug. 2021, pp. 1729–1741** (published online Aug. 2019; DOI 10.1109/TSE.2019.2934849). They equip the iGiraph framework with a **resource-based auto-scaling algorithm** plus a **characteristic-based dynamic repartitioning** method and smart process monitoring. [unimelb][ref-05] For convergent algorithms (single-source shortest path, connected components), as vertices deactivate over supersteps, the system automatically adjusts the **number and types** of heterogeneous VMs, merging/moving partitions to smaller/fewer machines. The approach outperforms Giraph and **reduces dollar cost by more than 50%** while ensuring faster execution.

## Workload prediction

**Problem manifestation.** Proactive auto-scaling is only as good as its forecast. Cloud/web workloads are volatile, non-stationary, and bursty, with long-term dependencies and non-linear patterns; a single model rarely fits all applications and timeframes. Under-prediction causes SLA violations and under-provisioning; over-prediction wastes resources/energy. Even deep models struggle with highly irregular bursts, concept drift, and multi-step-ahead accuracy decay.

**Specific study & solution.** **EsDNN — "esDNN: Deep Neural Network based Multivariate Workload Prediction Approach in Cloud Environment,"** (Xu, Song, Buyya et al.; ACM Transactions / arXiv, 2022). It uses a supervised, GRU-based deep neural network adapted for **multivariate, multi-step** workload prediction. Evaluated on real cloud traces, esDNN **reduced mean square error significantly (≈15% lower than a GRU-only baseline)** and, when applied to machine auto-scaling, **reduced the number of active hosts**, cutting provider cost. A complementary primary example is the **Facebook Prophet + LSTM hybrid for Kubernetes** (Frontiers in Computer Science, 2025), which captures seasonality (Prophet) plus non-linear dependencies (LSTM) to predict pod requirements on NASA and FIFA World Cup traces, outperforming single-model solutions and default Kubernetes HPA.

# Recommendations

1. **Default to reactive threshold-based scaling (HPA / target tracking) as a baseline** for any new workload — it is simple, robust, and universally supported. Configure resource requests correctly and set a sensible tolerance band (default 10% in Kubernetes) to avoid oscillation.
2. **Add event-driven scaling (KEDA) for bursty, queue- or event-based workloads**, using scale-to-zero to cut idle cost. Benchmark: if you see cold-start SLA misses on message-driven spikes, adopt KEDA scalers on the relevant event source.
3. **Layer proactive/predictive scaling on top of reactive when workloads show recurring patterns** (daily/weekly peaks). Start with AWS/Azure/GCP native predictive scaling in "forecast only" mode; graduate to active mode only after validating forecast accuracy. Threshold to escalate to custom ML (LSTM/GRU/hybrid): when native predictors miss non-linear or multivariate patterns and SLA-violation or cost targets aren't met.
4. **For stateful big-data/graph workloads, use model-driven or cost-aware autoscalers** (gray-box performance models for Hadoop/Spark; heterogeneity-aware scaling for graph jobs) rather than generic CPU rules, because state and per-resource billing dominate.
5. **Optimize energy explicitly** via SLA-aware consolidation (adaptive thresholds + energy-aware VM selection/placement), and consider DRL-based consolidation where you have the data and can tolerate a training phase.
6. **Always pair pod-level and node-level scaling** (HPA/VPA + Cluster Autoscaler/Karpenter), since pod scaling fails once cluster capacity is exhausted.
7. **Continuously retrain and monitor predictive models** to counter concept drift; keep a reactive safety net for unpredicted spikes (i.e., run hybrid).

# Caveats

- Reported quantitative results (e.g., ElasticSFC's 0.03% vs 72.97% SLA violations, >50% graph cost reduction, ~15% esDNN MSE improvement, ~10–30% SLA-violation reductions for LSTM+RL) come from the authors' own experiments under specific workloads and simulators (often CloudSim/CloudSimSDN or single-application testbeds) and may not generalize to all production environments.
- Vendor claims are calibrated carefully here: AWS's own docs describe predictive scaling as helping "avoid the need to over provision capacity" without a fixed percentage, while the frequently quoted "up to 15% EC2 savings" is a secondary/marketing figure — validate with "forecast only" mode before trusting it.
- Method categories overlap: adaptive thresholds, control theory with prediction, and RL can straddle reactive/proactive boundaries; many real systems are hybrids.
- Some sources are preprints (arXiv) or secondary surveys; where possible, primary publications were used, but a few results are corroborated via survey summaries.
- This report intentionally excludes one 2024 survey and its mirrors per the task constraint; all challenge-example studies were located and verified independently from their primary publishers.
