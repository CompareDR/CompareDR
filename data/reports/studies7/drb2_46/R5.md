# Auto-Scaling in Cloud Computing: Technology Classification, Challenges, and Research Solutions

**Executive Summary.** Auto-scaling — the automatic adjustment of allocated computing resources in response to changing workloads — is the mechanism that operationalizes cloud elasticity, allowing applications to meet Service Level Agreements (SLAs) while paying only for the resources they actually need ([Lorido-Botrán et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10723-014-9314-7)). Mainstream techniques fall into two broad families: **reactive methods**, which trigger scaling actions after a change in demand has been observed (threshold-based rules, control theory, queueing theory, reinforcement learning, and fuzzy logic), and **proactive methods**, which forecast future demand and provision capacity in advance (statistical time-series models, classical machine learning, deep learning, and hybrid/ensemble designs) ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149), [Lorido-Botrán et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10723-014-9314-7)). Despite two decades of research, applying auto-scaling in practice remains difficult. This report classifies the technical landscape and then examines five persistent challenge areas — **energy efficiency, the dynamic nature of telecom services, big data processing, cost-effective graph processing, and workload prediction** — illustrating each with a specific peer-reviewed study: a deep-reinforcement-learning autoscaler that incorporates datacenter cooling power ([Jawaddi & Ismail, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213138823005015)), machine-learning traffic forecasting for 5G core scaling ([Alawe et al., 2018](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8553653/)), the DS2 controller for streaming dataflows ([Kalavri et al., OSDI 2018](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/kalavri)), the cost-efficient heterogeneity-aware iGiraph autoscaler ([Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)), and an attention-based LSTM encoder-decoder predictor with scroll forecasting ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13638-019-1605-z)).

# Auto-Scaling Technology Classification

Auto-scaling is the process of automatically increasing or decreasing the computational resources assigned to an application — typically virtual machines, containers, or pods — so that performance targets are met at minimum monetary cost. Scaling can be **horizontal** (changing the *number* of instances) or **vertical** (changing the *size* of CPU/memory allocated to an existing instance), and the decision logic behind it is what distinguishes one auto-scaling technique from another ([Al-Dhuraibi et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSC.2017.2711009)). In one of the most cited reviews of the field, Lorido-Botrán, Miguel-Alonso and Lozano classify auto-scaling proposals into five main technical categories: static threshold-based rules, control theory, reinforcement learning, queueing theory, and time-series analysis ([Lorido-Botrán et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10723-014-9314-7)). Qu, Calheiros and Buyya, surveying web-application auto-scaling specifically, organize the design space along the decision dimension — reactive versus proactive — together with the resource-estimation technique used underneath ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149)).

The reactive/proactive dichotomy is fundamentally about **timing**. Reactive systems observe the system as it is — CPU utilization, request rate, queue depth, response time — and act only after a monitored signal crosses a defined boundary; proactive systems use historical traces to *predict* what the workload will look like minutes or hours ahead and pre-provision capacity so that resources are already warm when demand arrives ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149), [Chen et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3190507)). The practical difference is illustrated below: because VMs and containers take time to boot, configure, and warm caches, a purely reactive controller suffers an under-provisioning window at every sudden load increase, whereas a proactive controller can absorb that lead time by acting early.

Historically, the field has evolved from simple to increasingly intelligent decision mechanisms. A 2025 survey of cloud-native auto-scaling notes that "initially, threshold methods, heuristic methods, control theory methods, and queuing theory methods were used. Over time, these evolved into machine learning, deep learning, and reinforcement learning methods," with graph neural networks and attention mechanisms introduced more recently to handle complex microservice chains ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)). Container-based deployments have driven a parallel taxonomy covering cloud, edge and fog settings ([Dogani et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comcom.2023.06.010)). ## Reactive Methods

Reactive methods make scaling decisions based on the **current or recently observed state** of the system. They are the default in every major commercial platform — AWS Auto Scaling Groups, Azure Virtual Machine Scale Sets, Google Cloud Autoscaler, and the Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) all implement rule-based reactive scaling on metrics such as CPU or memory consumption ([Kubernetes Documentation](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/run-application/horizontal-pod-autoscale/), [Frontiers in Computer Science, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2025.1509165/full)). Their common strength is simplicity and safety: no model of the future is required, and behaviour is easy to audit. Their common weakness is latency — they always act *after* the workload has already changed, so they cannot hide the provisioning lead time of new resources, and their reactive nature "can lead to overprovision or performance degradations due to the cold start time of the applications" ([Frontiers in Computer Science, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2025.1509165/full)).

### Threshold-Based (Rule-Based) Auto-Scaling

**Core principle.** Threshold-based auto-scaling is the oldest and most widely deployed technique. An administrator defines lower and upper bounds on one or more monitored metrics — for example, "if average CPU utilization exceeds 70% for five consecutive minutes, add two instances; if it drops below 30%, remove one" — together with a scaling group bounded by minimum and maximum capacity ([AWS Documentation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/autoscaling/ec2/userguide/as-scale-based-on-demand.html)). Commercial platforms have layered refinements on top of the raw idea: *simple scaling* performs one fixed action per alarm breach; *step scaling* graduates the response by breach magnitude; and *target tracking* behaves like a thermostat, continuously adjusting capacity to hold a chosen metric near a target value ([MindMesh Academy](https://www.mindmeshacademy.com/certifications/aws/aws-certified-cloudops-engineer-associate/study-guide/3-1-1-ec2-auto-scaling-policies-and-lifecycle-hooks)). Because newly launched instances take minutes to boot and register with the load balancer, all industrial implementations add **cooldown periods** and **instance warm-up** timers that suppress further actions until the previous action's effect is visible in the metrics; the default AWS cooldown is 300 seconds ([Huawei Cloud Documentation](https://support.huaweicloud.com/intl/en-us/as_faq/as_faq_0035.html)).

**Advantages.** The approach is conceptually transparent, trivial to configure, computationally free at runtime, and universally supported by cloud providers, which is why it remains the industrial baseline ([Lorido-Botrán et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10723-014-9314-7)). Behaviour is deterministic and auditable — an operator can always explain exactly why the autoscaler acted — and no training data or system model is needed, making it robust for stable, well-understood workloads.

**Limitations.** Choosing good thresholds is notoriously difficult: values that are too tight trigger scaling on noise, while values that are too loose react too late, and the correct setting shifts with workload mix, instance type, and application version ([Lorido-Botrán et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10723-014-9314-7)). Threshold systems are prone to **oscillation (thrashing)** — a feedback loop in which scale-out lowers the metric, triggering scale-in, which raises it again — which is precisely what cooldown timers exist to suppress ([Huawei Cloud Documentation](https://support.huaweicloud.com/intl/en-us/as_faq/as_faq_0035.html)). Most fundamentally, thresholds are blind to the future: a flash crowd can saturate capacity long before the alarm evaluation window, metric aggregation delay, and VM start-up time have elapsed, causing SLA violations exactly when the service is under most stress ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149)).

### Control-Theory-Based Auto-Scaling

**Core principle.** Control-theoretic autoscalers treat the cloud application as a dynamical system to be regulated. Following the MAPE-K (Monitor–Analyze–Plan–Execute over a shared Knowledge base) loop of autonomic computing, a controller periodically measures an output such as average response time or utilization, compares it with a set-point, and computes a corrective scaling action using feedback laws — proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controllers, model-predictive control, or state estimators such as the Kalman filter ([Chen et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3190507)). Gandhi et al., for example, combined a Kalman filter with a queueing model to perform adaptive, model-driven autoscaling that estimates the parameters of the application's performance model online and provisions capacity with explicit performance guarantees ([Gandhi et al., ICAC 2014](https://www.usenix.org/conference/icac14/technical-sessions/presentation/gandhi)). Control theory has also been applied in industrial cloud settings, where feedback loops drive adaptation of multi-tier enterprise applications ([Barna et al., 2016](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7527853)).

**Advantages.** Control theory brings **mathematical guarantees** that rule-based systems lack: stability, settling time, and steady-state error can be analyzed formally, so a well-designed controller provably converges to its target without oscillating ([Chen et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3190507)). Adaptive variants re-estimate model parameters online, allowing the controller to track slowly drifting application behaviour without manual re-tuning, and the framework composes cleanly with the self-adaptive MAPE-K architecture used across autonomic systems ([Gandhi et al., ICAC 2014](https://www.usenix.org/conference/icac14/technical-sessions/presentation/gandhi)).

**Limitations.** Classical controllers assume the plant is approximately linear and time-invariant, but cloud applications exhibit strongly non-linear, time-varying behaviour (think of garbage-collection pauses, cache effects, and noisy neighbours), so controller gains tuned for one operating region can misbehave in another ([Lorido-Botrán et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10723-014-9314-7)). Designing the controller requires a reasonably accurate performance model of the application, and system identification under changing workloads is itself a hard problem; the approach also reacts to measured error, so like all reactive methods it cannot anticipate an arriving spike unless combined with a predictor ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149)).

### Queueing-Theory-Based Auto-Scaling

**Core principle.** Queueing-theoretic methods model the application (or each tier of a multi-tier application) as a network of queues — M/M/1, M/G/1, or more complex stations — in which requests arrive, wait, and are served. Analytical results then map a given arrival rate to expected response time, utilization, and dropping probability, allowing the autoscaler to compute the minimum number of servers that keeps the predicted response time below the SLA bound ([Lorido-Botrán et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10723-014-9314-7)). The technique is frequently embedded inside other systems: Gandhi et al.'s model-driven autoscaler uses a queueing model as the plant whose parameters the Kalman filter tracks ([Gandhi et al., ICAC 2014](https://www.usenix.org/conference/icac14/technical-sessions/presentation/gandhi)), and the DASA algorithm for 5G virtualized network functions models the VNF service as a queueing system in which instances are powered on and off according to the number of pending job requests, quantifying the cost–latency trade-off analytically ([Ren et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05803)).

**Advantages.** Queueing models are **interpretable and fast**: once calibrated, evaluating a candidate configuration is a closed-form computation rather than a simulation or a learning process, which suits online decision-making ([Lorido-Botrán et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10723-014-9314-7)). They connect infrastructure metrics directly to user-facing quantities — response time, queueing delay, rejection probability — making them a natural fit for SLA-driven scaling, and they provide a principled way to size capacity for a target utilization level.

**Limitations.** Standard queueing results rest on assumptions — Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, stationarity — that real cloud workloads routinely violate, particularly under bursty, heavy-tailed, or strongly periodic traffic, so model predictions can diverge from measured behaviour ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149)). Accurate parameter estimation (arrival rates, service-time distributions per tier) requires continuous measurement effort, and multi-tier or microservice topologies quickly push the models toward intractability, which is one reason the field has shifted toward learning-based methods ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)).

### Reinforcement-Learning-Based Auto-Scaling

**Core principle.** Reinforcement learning (RL) frames auto-scaling as a Markov Decision Process: an agent observes the system state (workload, current allocation, response time), chooses an action (add/remove/resize instances), receives a reward encoding the cost–performance trade-off (e.g., negative SLA violations minus resource cost), and over many interactions learns a policy that maximizes long-run reward. Tabular algorithms such as Q-learning and SARSA, and deep variants such as DQN and DDQN, are the most common choices ([García-Galán et al., RL autoscaling survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.09957)). Arabnejad et al. combined a self-adaptive fuzzy logic controller with Fuzzy SARSA and Fuzzy Q-learning on an OpenStack testbed, showing that both learners could handle sudden and periodic traffic while reducing operating cost and preventing SLA violations ([Arabnejad et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07114)). More recent cloud-native work pairs RL with workload predictors — for example, an enhanced Deep Q-Network driven by a spatio-temporal graph neural network workload forecast, deployed at Ant Group to raise CPU utilization under SLA constraints ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)).

**Advantages.** RL requires **no hand-crafted rules and no explicit performance model**: the policy is learned directly from interaction, which makes it attractive for complex, poorly modelled systems, and it naturally optimizes long-term objectives (total cost plus SLA compliance) rather than instantaneous thresholds ([García-Galán et al., RL autoscaling survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.09957)). Because the policy is a function of state, a trained agent generalizes across workload regimes and can coordinate horizontal and vertical scaling jointly.

**Limitations.** The weaknesses are equally well documented. Convergence time grows with the dimension of the state and action spaces, and "since these algorithms do not have an adequate initial policy they have a poor initial performance," meaning that exploratory actions taken early in learning can directly hurt makespan, cost, and SLA compliance in production ([García-Galán et al., RL autoscaling survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.09957)). A survey of ML-based autoscaling likewise highlights RL's "poor initial performance as the agent begins to explore" and "long convergence time," noting that one representative system demonstrated acceptable convergence only for up to 16 VMs per application with a small state space ([Survey on ML-based Autoscaling](https://www.net.in.tum.de/fileadmin/TUM/NET/NET-2022-07-1/NET-2022-07-1_11.pdf)). Reward shaping is delicate, deep RL adds training instability and compute overhead, and deploying an exploring agent against live traffic remains the approach's biggest practical barrier.

### Fuzzy-Logic-Based Auto-Scaling

**Core principle.** Fuzzy autoscalers encode human operational expertise as linguistic rules over fuzzified metrics — for example, "IF cpu_usage is HIGH AND response_time is RISING THEN add TWO instances" — where membership functions map crisp measurements to graded categories and an inference engine aggregates rule activations into a concrete scaling action. Because inputs are graded rather than binary, fuzzy controllers interpolate smoothly between rules instead of switching abruptly at a hard threshold ([Arabnejad et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07114)). Self-adaptive variants learn or modify the fuzzy rule base at runtime; Arabnejad et al.'s system, for instance, lets reinforcement learning adjust the fuzzy rules online, blending the two reactive paradigms ([Arabnejad et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07114)).

**Advantages.** Fuzzy logic is well suited to the **imprecision inherent in cloud monitoring**: metrics like "high CPU" or "degraded latency" are naturally fuzzy, and the controller remains stable where a crisp threshold would chatter ([Lorido-Botrán et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10723-014-9314-7)). No mathematical model of the application is needed, rule bases are human-readable (operators can inspect and edit them), and inference is computationally cheap.

**Limitations.** The quality of the controller is bounded by the quality of the expert knowledge it encodes: designing membership functions and a complete, non-contradictory rule base requires domain expertise, and a static rule base cannot track an evolving workload without an adaptation mechanism on top ([Arabnejad et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07114)). Like all reactive techniques, fuzzy controllers act only after the fact and provide no anticipation of predictable demand surges.

## Proactive Methods

Proactive methods attack the fundamental weakness of reactive control — its inability to act before demand changes — by **forecasting future workload or resource requirements** and scheduling scaling actions ahead of time ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149)). This matters because provisioning is never instantaneous: VMs and containers must boot, initialize, register with load balancers, and warm caches before they serve traffic at full efficiency, so a predictor that looks even a few minutes ahead can eliminate the under-provisioning window that reactive systems inevitably suffer ([MDPI Electronics, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/16/3333)). Workload forecasting itself is a mature research area with its own taxonomy of statistical and machine-learning methods ([Masdari & Khoshnevis, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10586-019-03010-3)). The accuracy of the predictor is the Achilles' heel of the whole approach: over-forecasting wastes money, under-forecasting violates SLAs, which is why production-grade systems usually combine prediction with a reactive safety net.

### Statistical Time-Series Forecasting

**Core principle.** The classical proactive approach treats the workload as a time series and extrapolates it with statistical models: moving averages, exponential smoothing, Holt-Winters triple exponential smoothing for seasonal data, and ARIMA/SARIMA for autocorrelated series. Nikravesh, Ajila and Lung built an autonomic prediction system for IaaS resource provisioning whose central hypothesis was that prediction accuracy — and therefore scaling quality — improves when complementary forecasting models are combined and their parameters periodically re-tuned, rather than relying on any single fixed model ([Nikravesh et al., 2015](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/7194655/)). In a production-scale example, a CDN autoscaling framework built on 18 months of real traffic traces used Seasonal ARIMA for low-cost, competitive-accuracy forecasting at hourly and 10-minute horizons, feeding predictions into a queue-based model that converts forecast load into required cache-server capacity ([MDPI Electronics, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/16/3333)).

**Advantages.** Statistical models are **cheap to train, fast to execute, and interpretable**: coefficients have physical meaning (trend, seasonality), uncertainty intervals come with the model, and the modest compute cost makes them suitable for embedding directly inside a control loop that runs every minute ([MDPI Electronics, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/16/3333)). For workloads with strong, stable periodicity — business-hours traffic, daily video-streaming peaks — simple seasonal models already capture most of the predictable structure.

**Limitations.** Classical techniques are "effective for short- to medium-term forecasting of stationary data" but "often struggle with complex, nonlinear, and high-dimensional time series data" ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)). ARIMA-family models assume (transformed) stationarity and linear dependence, so they degrade sharply under flash crowds, campaign launches, or concept drift — precisely the regimes where proactive scaling would be most valuable — and they must be re-identified whenever the workload's statistical character changes ([Masdari & Khoshnevis, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10586-019-03010-3)).

### Classical Machine-Learning Methods

**Core principle.** Machine-learning predictors replace the fixed statistical form with a learned regression: models such as linear/ridge regression, support vector regression, random forests, gradient-boosted trees, and shallow artificial neural networks map engineered features — recent lagged values, time-of-day, calendar indicators, request-type mix — to future workload or future resource demand ([Masdari & Khoshnevis, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10586-019-03010-3)). In the edge/5G context, an AI-driven VNF auto-scaling framework trained and compared a battery of regressors (linear regression, ridge regression, support vector regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and multi-layer perceptrons) to relate VNF workload to CPU/memory consumption, and combined them with forecasting models including random forest and XGBoost to drive predictive horizontal and vertical scaling ([MDPI Electronics, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/9/1808)).

**Advantages.** Classical ML models capture **non-linear relationships** that defeat ARIMA-style methods, train in seconds to minutes on commodity hardware, and handle multivariate inputs naturally — a random forest can consume dozens of correlated metrics without manual model structure design ([Masdari & Khoshnevis, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10586-019-03010-3)). Tree ensembles additionally expose feature importances, giving operators some insight into what drives the forecast.

**Limitations.** These models are only as good as their **feature engineering**: they have no inherent notion of temporal order, so lag windows and seasonal indicators must be hand-crafted, and long-range dependencies are difficult to represent ([Masdari & Khoshnevis, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10586-019-03010-3)). They require representative historical data for training, generalize poorly to workload patterns not seen in the training set, and must be retrained as applications evolve — an operational burden that grows with the number of scaled services.

### Deep-Learning-Based Prediction

**Core principle.** Deep learning models learn temporal structure directly from raw sequences. Recurrent architectures (LSTM, GRU) propagate hidden state through time to capture long-range dependencies; convolutional and hybrid CNN–LSTM stacks extract local patterns; attention mechanisms and encoder–decoder designs weight the most relevant history; and Transformer-family models capture long-term dependencies with parallelizable training ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)). Representative systems include an attention-based LSTM encoder-decoder for cloud workload prediction, which adds a "scroll prediction" scheme that decomposes long-horizon forecasts into short segments to suppress error accumulation ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13638-019-1605-z)); a bidirectional-LSTM autoscaler integrated with Kubernetes, which showed deep models driving pod-scaling decisions directly ([MDPI Applied Sciences, 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/11/9/3835)); and the E2LG architecture, which decomposes workload series into frequency bands via empirical mode decomposition and forecasts each band with an ensemble GAN/LSTM, improving prediction accuracy by at least 5–12% on average over contemporary CNN/LSTM/SVR hybrids ([Yazdanian & Sharifian, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11227-021-03723-6)). The most recent wave applies spatio-temporal graph neural networks to model dependencies between microservices, and Informer-style Transformers for long sequences ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)).

**Advantages.** Deep models currently deliver the **highest forecast accuracy on complex, non-linear, high-volume cloud traces**: they learn high-dimensional features automatically, handle multivariate input, and — with attention — can model both periodic structure and irregular bursts ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)). An LSTM-based workload forecaster for cloud datacenters demonstrated mean squared error as low as 3.17×10⁻³ on benchmark web-server traces ([Kumar et al., 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877050917328557)), illustrating the accuracy headroom over statistical baselines.

**Limitations.** Deep models are **data-hungry and computationally expensive** to train, behave as black boxes that are hard to audit, and can fail abruptly on sudden regime changes or short-lifecycle workloads with little history ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)). Multi-step-ahead forecasts accumulate error — the very problem Zhu et al.'s scroll prediction was designed to mitigate — and model training/inference cost must itself be budgeted inside the control loop, which motivates adaptive model selection between cheap statistical models and expensive deep ones ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13638-019-1605-z), [MDPI Electronics, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/16/3333)).

### Hybrid and Ensemble Approaches

**Core principle.** Hybrid designs acknowledge that no single method wins under all workloads and combine techniques along two axes: *reactive + proactive* (a forecaster handles the predictable component while a reactive controller catches residual surprises) and *model ensembles* (several predictors run in parallel and their outputs are selected or weighted by recent accuracy). FLAS implements a reference reactive-plus-proactive architecture for distributed services, fusing prediction-driven scaling with threshold-driven corrections ([Qu et al., 2018 taxonomy context](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149)). RobustScaler, presented at ICDE 2022, combines non-homogeneous Poisson process workload modelling with stochastically constrained optimization to make QoS-aware scaling decisions that remain robust under complex, real-world workload scenarios ([Qian et al., ICDE 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICDE53745.2022.00252)). In industry, Microsoft Azure SQL Database serverless uses Moneyball, a proactive auto-scaling system that forecasts per-database resource needs to resize serverless databases ahead of demand ([Poppe et al., PVLDB 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.14778/3514061.3514073)). Ensemble forecasters weight model outputs inversely to recent error: the CDN framework described earlier linearly combines S-ARIMA and LSTM predictions with coefficients continuously adjusted by measured MAE, and switches between models as their relative accuracy drifts ([MDPI Electronics, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/16/3333)).

**Advantages.** Hybrids deliver **robustness**: the proactive component absorbs provisioning lead time for patterned demand, while the reactive component bounds the damage when forecasts are wrong — the failure mode that pure proactive systems fear most ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149)). Ensembles smooth out individual model weaknesses and adapt to concept drift without manual model re-selection.

**Limitations.** Complexity is the price: hybrid systems have more moving parts to tune, monitor, and debug, and coordinating two controllers requires careful arbitration logic to avoid contradictory actions ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149)). Ensemble decision-making can itself become a bottleneck in high-traffic, highly distributed settings ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)).

# Main Challenges and Solutions

Classifying techniques is only half the picture. Academia and industry repeatedly hit the same walls when deploying auto-scaling against real workloads and real infrastructure: energy budgets, telecom-grade traffic dynamics, the scale-out semantics of big-data engines, the unusual cost structure of graph processing, and the fundamental difficulty of predicting cloud workloads. Each subsection below first describes how the challenge manifests in practice, then presents one specific published research study and the solution it proposes.

## Energy Efficiency

**The challenge.** Cloud datacenters are among the fastest-growing electricity consumers on the planet. The International Energy Agency estimates that datacentres consumed around **415 TWh of electricity in 2024 — roughly 1.5% of global consumption** — after growing at about 12% per year over the preceding five years, and projects consumption to more than double to about **945 TWh by 2030** in its base case ([IEA, Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-demand-from-ai)). Auto-scaling sits directly on this energy lever: every over-provisioned VM burns power while idle, every unnecessary scale-out multiplies that waste, and aggressive scale-in can force expensive re-boots later. The problem is compounded because energy is not only a function of servers' CPU utilization — cooling systems consume a large share of facility power and respond to how load is placed on physical hosts — yet most autoscalers optimize only for performance and instance count, treating energy as an externality ([Jawaddi & Ismail, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213138823005015)).

Global data-centre electricity consumption, 2024 estimate and 2030 projection (IEA)

**A research solution.** Jawaddi and Ismail (Universiti Teknologi MARA) address exactly this blind spot in *"Enhancing energy efficiency in cloud scaling: A DRL-based approach incorporating cooling power"* (Sustainable Computing: Informatics and Systems, 2023). They observe that existing energy-driven autoscalers drive scaling on metrics such as host energy consumption, throughput, or execution time, but that "limited attention [has been] paid to incorporating cooling power as a metric for driving energy-efficient cloud scaling" ([Jawaddi & Ismail, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213138823005015)). Their solution is a deep reinforcement learning autoscaler — implemented with both DQN and DDQN and benchmarked against tabular Q-learning — that vertically scales CPU, RAM, and bandwidth allocations while taking **host energy consumption and datacenter cooling power together** as the primary decision metrics. DRL replaces slow, memory-intensive lookup tables with differentiable function approximators, enabling the agent to learn in the high-dimensional state space that energy-aware decisions require ([Jawaddi & Ismail, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213138823005015)).

The work is instructive beyond its specific algorithm. It demonstrates why the energy challenge pushes designers toward the RL corner of the taxonomy — the cooling/power dynamics of a datacenter are too non-linear for threshold rules or classical controllers — and it illustrates the vertical-scaling argument that adjusting a running host's resources avoids the provisioning delay and replication overhead of horizontal scaling, which matters for stateful components such as database servers ([Jawaddi & Ismail, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213138823005015)). At the same time, the paper's own discussion of convergence-speed sensitivity to resource allocation echoes the general RL limitations catalogued in Part One, reminding us that energy-aware autoscaling inherits the training-cost and exploration-risk problems of its underlying method.

## Dynamic Nature of Telecom Services

**The challenge.** Telecom workloads are among the most hostile environments for auto-scaling. With Network Function Virtualization (NFV), mobile operators run core-network functions — the virtualized Evolved Packet Core (vEPC), the 5G Access and Mobility Management Function (AMF), firewalls, routers — as software VNFs on cloud infrastructure, and these must absorb traffic that swings with time-of-day cycles, mass events, and user mobility, while meeting strict latency and availability SLAs ([Ren et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05803)). Scaling a VNF is not like scaling a stateless web tier: instances carry signaling state, power-on/power-off decisions interact with legacy equipment capacity, and a slow or wrong scaling decision translates directly into dropped sessions and violated QoS for subscribers. Designing "an auto-scaling algorithm with low operation cost and low latency while considering the capacity of legacy network equipment" is explicitly identified as a hard problem in the 5G literature ([Ren et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05803)).

**A research solution.** Alawe, Ksentini, Hadjadj-Aoul and Bertin tackle the telecom dynamics problem with proactive machine learning in *"Improving Traffic Forecasting for 5G Core Network Scalability: A Machine Learning Approach"* (IEEE Network, vol. 32, no. 6, 2018) ([Alawe et al., 2018](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8553653/)). Rather than waiting for load metrics to breach thresholds, they forecast the traffic that 5G core network functions will face and scale VNFs ahead of demand — for instance predicting the signaling load on the AMF — so that instances are already in place when the surge arrives. The paper, which has been cited over 300 times ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8553653/)), helped establish ML-based traffic forecasting as the standard route to scalability in softwarized 5G cores.

The approach has since been extended along two lines that show how the field matured. On the analytical side, Ren et al.'s DASA algorithm models the VNF service as a queueing system in which instances are powered on and off according to pending job requests, using the model to quantify the operation-cost-versus-latency trade-off and showing significant cost reduction given a latency upper bound ([Ren et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05803)). On the deep-learning side, recent work trains regression models (linear, ridge, SVR, random forest, gradient boosting, MLP) to map VNF workload to resource consumption and forecasting models (XGBoost, TCN, LSTM/GRU, Transformers) to anticipate load, then executes predictive horizontal *and* vertical scaling, validated on real Internet traffic traces for firewall, router, and virtual-switch VNFs in edge/5G IoT environments ([MDPI Electronics, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/9/1808)). Together these studies trace the arc from reactive threshold scaling of VNFs to proactive, ML-driven, QoS-aware telecom elasticity.

## Big Data Processing

**The challenge.** Streaming and batch big-data workloads stress auto-scalers in ways that web applications do not. Streaming computations are "by nature long-running, and their workloads can change in unpredictable ways," so maintaining throughput and latency requires dynamically scaling the resources of a running dataflow — yet most stream processors historically left "the difficult task of deciding *how much* to scale to the user" ([Kalavri et al., OSDI 2018](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/kalavri)). Where automatic scaling existed, policies relied on coarse-grained metrics such as observed throughput, backpressure, and CPU utilization, and as a result "tend to show incorrect provisioning, oscillations, and long convergence times" ([Kalavri et al., OSDI 2018](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/kalavri)). The difficulty is structural: a dataflow is a pipeline of operators with heterogeneous per-operator capacities, so the *global* bottleneck may be invisible to local metrics, and scaling one operator without scaling its neighbours merely moves the bottleneck downstream. Reconfiguration is also expensive, since scaling a stateful streaming operator requires redistributing state without losing in-flight correctness.

**A research solution.** Kalavri et al.'s **DS2** ("Three Steps is All You Need: Fast, Accurate, Automatic Scaling Decisions for Distributed Streaming Dataflows," OSDI 2018) is the canonical answer ([Kalavri et al., OSDI 2018](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/kalavri)). DS2 combines a general performance model of streaming dataflows with lightweight system-level instrumentation to estimate the *true* processing and output rates of each individual operator — the rate it could sustain if never back-pressured — rather than the distorted rates observable at the metrics level. Given a target input rate, the model then computes, in a single pass, the required parallelism of *every* operator simultaneously, instead of iterating operator-by-operator corrections.

The results were striking: applied to Apache Flink and Timely Dataflow, DS2 "converges to the optimal, backpressure-free configuration in a single step instead of six" when compared against Dhalion, the then state-of-the-art autoscaler in Twitter's Heron ([Kalavri et al., OSDI 2018](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/kalavri)). DS2 remains reactive in the classification of Part One — it acts on observed performance traces — but it shows how a principled performance model can give a reactive controller near-proactive decisiveness, eliminating the oscillation and slow convergence that plague metric-driven scaling of dataflows. Its design has since influenced commercial stream-processing autoscaling, where controllers traverse the dataflow graph to set per-operator parallelism from estimated data rates ([Confluent](https://www.confluent.io/blog/serverless-stream-processing-flink/)).

## Cost-Effective Graph Processing

**The challenge.** Large-scale graph analytics — social networks, IoT topologies, scientific computing — are dominated by iterative algorithms on frameworks descended from Pregel and Apache Giraph. These workloads have an unusual cost signature on pay-as-you-go clouds: general-purpose engines such as MapReduce are inefficient for iterative graph computation, while purpose-built graph frameworks "are implemented for commodity clusters where they will not be charged for the resources that they consume," leaving their behaviour on metered public clouds under-studied ([Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)). Users face virtualization overhead, imperfect VM placement affecting communication bandwidth and latency, and a fundamental mismatch: for *convergent* algorithms such as single-source shortest path (SSSP) and connected components (CC), the number of active vertices and inter-machine messages **shrinks as iterations proceed**, so a cluster sized for the first superstep is drastically over-provisioned — and over-billed — by the last one ([Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)).

**A research solution.** Heidari and Buyya (University of Melbourne CLOUDS Lab) propose *"A Cost-Efficient Auto-Scaling Algorithm for Large-Scale Graph Processing in Cloud Environments with Heterogeneous Resources"* (IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, vol. 47, no. 8, 2021) ([Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)). Their system, **iGiraph-heterogeneity-aware**, embeds a resource-based auto-scaling algorithm inside the iGiraph framework with three cooperating modules on the master node: a monitoring module tracking messages, bandwidth, CPU, and memory per VM; a decision-making module that replaces or removes VMs whenever the cost of the next superstep can be made lower than the current one (formally requiring *C(Sᵢ₊₁) ≤ C(Sᵢ)*); and a partition distributor that executes a novel **characteristic-based dynamic repartitioning**, migrating high-degree vertices toward their neighbours and mapping partitions onto heterogeneous VM types so that smaller, cheaper VMs absorb shrinking partitions as the computation converges ([Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)).

Evaluated on Australia's NECTAR research cloud with real YouTube, Amazon, Pokec, and Twitter graphs under SSSP and CC workloads, the heterogeneity-aware autoscaler **cut the dollar cost of processing by more than 50% compared with standard Giraph** and by more than 20% compared with the original homogeneous iGiraph, while remaining competitive or better on execution time ([Heidari & Buyya, IEEE TSE 2021](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/papers/Graph-AutoScaleTSE.pdf)). The study is a model answer to the cost-efficiency challenge: rather than treating the cloud as a fixed cluster, it exploits exactly the features that make public clouds economically distinctive — heterogeneous VM types and per-second billing — and aligns resource shape with the algorithm's convergence profile.

## Workload Prediction

**The challenge.** Every proactive method in Part One ultimately stands or falls on the quality of its workload forecast, and prediction is hard because cloud workload time series "at subminute scale [are] highly chaotic and volatile," mixing periodic cycles with sudden bursts and long-range dependencies that defeat traditional statistical approaches ([Yazdanian & Sharifian, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11227-021-03723-6)). Two failure modes dominate: single-step predictors cannot see far enough ahead to cover VM provisioning lead time, while naive multi-step predictors accumulate error so quickly that long-horizon forecasts become unusable — each predicted value feeding the next prediction amplifies the deviation ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13638-019-1605-z)). Prediction error translates directly into money and SLA risk: over-forecasts cause idle spend, under-forecasts cause the very violations that proactive scaling was meant to prevent ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149)).

**A research solution.** Zhu, Zhang, Chen and Gao propose *"A novel approach to workload prediction using attention-based LSTM encoder-decoder network in cloud environment"* (EURASIP Journal on Wireless Communications and Networking, 2019) ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13638-019-1605-z)). Their model uses one LSTM as an encoder that compresses the historical workload sequence into a context vector, and a second LSTM as decoder that generates the forecast, with an **attention mechanism** letting the decoder weigh which historical time steps matter most for each predicted step — particularly effective for batch-style workloads. To fight error accumulation over long horizons, they introduce a **scroll prediction** method that splits a long forecast into several short segments instead of extending one recursive chain ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13638-019-1605-z)).

On the Alibaba cluster traces and the Dinda benchmark, the attention encoder-decoder outperformed basic LSTM and GRU encoder-decoder baselines as well as non-RNN methods (ARIMA, PSR+EA-GMDH), and — critically for multi-step forecasting — showed the slowest error growth: across a 12-step-ahead horizon its RMSE grew by only about 0.52×10⁻², versus 0.66×10⁻² for the GRU encoder-decoder and 0.87×10⁻² for plain LSTM ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13638-019-1605-z)). The study exemplifies how the workload-prediction challenge is being met not by abandoning forecasting but by architectural refinement — attention for relevance weighting, scroll decomposition for horizon control — with hybrid successors such as E2LG (empirical-mode decomposition plus GAN/LSTM ensembles) pushing accuracy further for noisy cloud traces ([Yazdanian & Sharifian, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11227-021-03723-6)).

## Concluding Observations

Reading the two parts together reveals a coherent trajectory. The reactive family remains the operational bedrock — every hyperscaler ships threshold-based autoscaling, hardened with cooldowns and warm-up timers — because it is safe, cheap, and explainable ([AWS Documentation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/autoscaling/ec2/userguide/as-scale-based-on-demand.html)). Control-theoretic and queueing-theoretic methods supply the analytical rigor that thresholds lack, while reinforcement learning and fuzzy logic trade that rigor for adaptability to systems that resist modelling. The proactive family answers the structural weakness of reaction — provisioning lead time — at the price of forecast dependence, and the clear trend in both the literature and production systems (RobustScaler, Moneyball, DS2's descendants) is toward **hybrids that pair prediction with a reactive safety net and ensembles that hedge across models** ([Qu et al., 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3148149), [Poppe et al., PVLDB 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.14778/3514061.3514073)).

The five challenges show that "auto-scaling" is no longer a single problem but a family of domain-specific ones. Energy constraints push toward energy-aware reward functions in RL controllers; telecom dynamics push toward forecast-driven VNF orchestration under hard latency bounds; big-data engines need model-based per-operator scaling decisions; graph analytics need cost-shaped heterogeneous elasticity; and all proactive ambitions rest on workload prediction accurate enough to trust with production traffic. As cloud-native architectures add microservice dependency graphs, edge heterogeneity, and serverless cold starts to the mix, the research frontier is moving toward graph-aware predictors, transformer-class forecasters, and safe, fast-converging reinforcement learners — techniques designed to keep pace with infrastructure that grows more dynamic every year ([Auto-scaling Approaches for Cloud-native Applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17128v1)).
