## Detailed Comparison of Conventional Micro-hydroturbines and PATs

### Operational Efficiency: Best Efficiency Point and Partial Load Performance

**Best Efficiency Point (BEP) Efficiency Comparison**

The most fundamental efficiency gap between conventional micro-hydroturbines and PATs lies at the best efficiency point. Conventional turbines are purpose-built for energy extraction and achieve high BEP efficiencies: Pelton turbines typically reach [85–92%](https://manufacturingcostanalytics.com/hydro-turbine-types-comparison) at BEP, with some sources reporting up to 90–95% on micro-scale systems; Francis turbines achieve [90–95%](https://manufacturingcostanalytics.com/hydro-turbine-types-comparison); Turgo turbines reach approximately [80–90%](https://manufacturingcostanalytics.com/hydro-turbine-types-comparison), with pico-hydro studies observing over 80% under optimal conditions; and Crossflow (Banki) turbines achieve [70–85%](https://manufacturingcostanalytics.com/hydro-turbine-types-comparison). Kaplan turbines, the most efficient reaction type, reach [88–94%](https://manufacturingcostanalytics.com/hydro-turbine-types-comparable).

PATs, by contrast, operate standard pumps in reverse mode and achieve significantly lower BEP efficiencies. As established by Carravetta et al. (2022), PAT efficiency at BEP varies considerably with pump type, reaching maximum values of approximately [65–75%](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526), which is notably lower than the maximum efficiency achieved by traditional turbines. In small-scale laboratory prototypes using small centrifugal pumps, overall energy conversion ratios can be as low as 16%, though larger plants employing more efficient semi-axial submersible pumps can achieve conversion ratios up to [40%](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526). This efficiency gap of 15–25 percentage points at BEP represents the primary performance penalty of choosing PATs over conventional turbines.

**Pump-Mode vs. Turbine-Mode BEP Relationship**

A critical question for PAT selection is how turbine-mode BEP relates to pump-mode BEP. KSB's technical documentation states that "in general, turbine efficiency corresponds to pump efficiency," meaning turbine-mode BEP efficiency is approximately equal to pump-mode BEP efficiency for a given machine. Yang et al. (2022) confirm this, noting that the pump "reversal can run at the optimum efficiency point" and that peak efficiency in turbine mode is the same as in pump mode. However, Renzi et al. (2020) caution that this assumption "might be a limitation because the mechanical efficiency of a PaT at BEP can vary depending on the internal fluid dynamic behaviour of the flow and on the external operating conditions."

Importantly, while the BEP efficiency values are approximately equal, the operating parameters at BEP differ significantly between modes. KSB explicitly states that "the flow rate and turbine head are always greater in turbine mode at BEP than the flow rate and head are in the pump mode at the same rotational speed." Yang et al. (2022) corroborate this, noting that "the head and flow at the best efficiency point are higher than the pump mode." This means that for a given pump selected from a manufacturer's catalog, the turbine-mode BEP will occur at a higher flow rate and higher head than the pump-mode BEP, a relationship governed by the affinity laws and the different velocity triangle configurations in the two modes.

**PAT Partial-Load Performance and Operating Flow Range**

PATs exhibit a sharp efficiency decline when operating away from BEP, which is their most significant operational limitation. The flow operating range of a PAT is limited to approximately [70–130% of the BEP flow rate](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148122007790), and "efficiency decreases sharply under partial load conditions" outside this band. This narrow operating range is a direct consequence of the PAT's lack of internal mechanical regulation devices (see S1.3 for a detailed comparison of regulation capabilities). The efficiency penalty is substantial: operating a pump as a turbine generates a loss in efficiency of [3% to 10% for non-designed conditions](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321), with lower efficiencies particularly pronounced at reduced flow rates.

The Variable Operating Strategy (VOS) developed by Carravetta et al. (2013) addresses this limitation by optimizing PAT geometry selection for a given flow-head distribution pattern, achieving overall plant efficiencies of up to [50%](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526) under variable conditions; VOS and its role in PAT regulation are discussed in detail in S1.3.

**Conventional Turbine Partial-Load Performance**

Conventional turbines maintain efficiency across wider flow ranges due to purpose-built regulation mechanisms. Francis turbines employ [wicket gates](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines) (guide vanes) that adjust the flow area entering the runner, allowing efficient operation across a broad range of flow rates. Kaplan turbines combine [adjustable blades with wicket gates](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines), providing dual regulation that extends the efficient operating range even further — Kaplan turbines can maintain high efficiency from approximately 50% to 100% of design flow. Pelton turbines achieve partial-load regulation through [needle nozzles and deflectors](http://waterturbines.wikidot.com/main:types-of-water-turbines), with multi-jet configurations capable of operating efficiently at flows as low as [10% of design flow](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/). Crossflow turbines use [guide vanes](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines) to direct flow onto a limited portion of the runner, enabling reasonable efficiency across variable flow conditions. These design features give conventional turbines a decisive advantage in urban water distribution networks, where diurnal and seasonal flow variations are inherent.

**PAT BEP Prediction Methods**

Since most pump manufacturers do not provide turbine-mode performance data, predicting PAT BEP from pump-mode characteristics is essential. Four main approaches exist:

1. **Theoretical (Stepanoff and related correlations):** Early methods by Stepanoff, Childs, Sharma, Wong, Williams, and Alatorre-Frenk use pump efficiency or specific speed to predict turbine-mode BEP. However, Lin et al. (2021) found that these correlations can produce deviations of more than 20% between experimental and predicted values for standard pumps, though the Stepanoff method showed relatively higher accuracy. The accuracy of these methods depends heavily on the statistical database size and PAT type coverage.

2. **CFD numerical simulation:** Computational Fluid Dynamics has become a vital methodology for PAT performance prediction. Carravetta et al. (2017) developed a CFD-based method validated against experimental data for three pumps with different specific speeds, achieving predictions within 5–10% of experimental values at certain load regions. Lin et al. (2021) found that CFD predictions agreed well with test results, with relative errors under 5% at large-load conditions and under 10% at part-load conditions, though accuracy declined under partial-load conditions due to more complex turbulence characteristics. CFD is effective for identifying losses in turbo-machinery components but requires careful mesh quality, boundary condition specification, and turbulence model selection.

3. **Experimental testing:** Direct testing on dedicated test benches per ISO 9906 standards provides the most reliable BEP data. However, experimental testing is costly and time-consuming, and many published studies rely on numerical methods validated against limited experimental data.

4. **Non-dimensional analytical methodology:** Renzi et al. (2020) developed a model based on non-dimensional parameters using an experimental dataset of 59 PaTs. The model establishes linear correlations between specific speeds in pump and turbine modes, and between specific diameters in both modes, covering a specific speed range of 0.09–2.24. A second-order polynomial equation forecasts mechanical efficiency in turbine mode using pump-mode specific speed and mechanical efficiency as independent variables. Validation against six additional PaTs demonstrated good forecast capability and better agreement with experimental and numerical data than four other models available in literature. Lin et al. (2021) proposed an impeller-volute matching (IVM) theoretical method validated on three pumps with specific speeds from 58.7 to 129.6, achieving prediction errors of less than 5%.

The specific speed (n_s) of the selected pump significantly influences PAT performance and BEP prediction accuracy. Low specific speed pumps (radial impellers) tend to have different loss distributions in turbine mode compared to high specific speed pumps (axial/mixed-flow), affecting both BEP efficiency and the shape of the efficiency curve. The Renzi et al. (2020) model's broad specific speed coverage (0.09–2.24) represents an improvement over earlier methods that were often limited to narrow specific speed ranges.

### Cost Comparison: Procurement, Installation, and O&M

**Procurement Cost.** The most salient economic advantage of PATs over conventional micro-hydroturbines lies in procurement cost. PATs repurpose standardized centrifugal pumps — mass-produced by a global supplier base — in reverse mode, eliminating the bespoke engineering and specialist manufacturing that conventional turbines require. The NCAT micro-hydro guide estimates that the cost of a PAT is 50% or less than that of a comparable conventional turbine, citing Natural Resources Canada (2004) ([NCAT, 2011](https://www.ncat.org/publication/micro-hydro-power-a-beginners-guide-to-design-and-installation/)). This figure is corroborated by Novara et al. (2019), whose cost model for PATs in run-of-river and in-pipe microhydropower applications found that PAT technology is less expensive per unit of installed power than conventional hydraulic turbines by a factor of 5 for installations exceeding 20 kW ([Novara & McNabola, 2019, ASCE J. Water Resour. Plan. Manag.](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/abs/10.1061/(ASCE)WR.1943-5452.0001063)). The same study developed a set of equations enabling designers to predict PAT and generator cost from nominal flow rate and available hydraulic head, providing a standardized cost-estimation framework that conventional turbine selection — which typically requires custom runner geometry optimization — lacks.

For conventional micro-hydroturbines, installed costs span a wide range. IRENA reports small hydropower project costs of USD 1,300–8,000/kW, with significant site-specific variation ([IRENA, 2012](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2012/RE_Technologies_Cost_Analysis-HYDROPOWER.pdf)). The NCAT guide places typical micro-hydro installation costs at approximately USD 1,500–4,000/kW for conventional equipment ([NCAT, 2011](https://www.ncat.org/publication/micro-hydro-power-a-beginners-guide-to-design-and-installation/)). PAT systems, by leveraging off-the-shelf pump products from manufacturers such as KSB, Grundfos, and Flowserve, typically fall at the lower end or below this range, though precise per-kW figures depend heavily on pump size, specific speed, and the selected generator pairing.

**Installation Costs.** Civil works represent a major barrier to the diffusion of small hydropower in water distribution networks (WDNs), as emphasized by Carravetta et al. (2022) ([Carravetta et al., 2022, Water 14(10), 1526](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). In conventional micro-hydroturbine installations, civil works typically include a powerhouse structure, penstock piping, forebay, trash rack, tailrace, and foundation — costs that scale steeply for the small power outputs typical of urban WDN sites (recoverable power per node is generally limited; see S4.1). For PAT installations, the civil works requirement can be similarly burdensome: existing PRVs are generally installed in small manholes under street pavement, and enlarging these manholes or constructing new waterproof enclosures to host PAT equipment and electrical components represents a significant and often prohibitive expense ([Carravetta et al., 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).

To address this, Carravetta et al. proposed the Energy Booster (EB) concept, which encapsulates two submersible PATs inside a water booster unit that can be placed under an existing manhole by merely opening a hole beneath it. This approach drastically reduces civil works costs and resolves concerns about installing electrical and electronic components in the unfavorable underground environment ([Carravetta et al., 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). The EB design thus directly targets the largest cost barrier to PAT deployment in urban settings.

Additionally, PAT systems require regulation equipment that conventional turbines possess internally. Hydraulic regulation (HR) demands series-parallel control valves and associated piping, while electrical regulation (ER) requires a variable speed drive (VSD). Both add to installation cost and complexity, though Carravetta et al. note that the cost of these regulation systems is generally modest relative to the PAT itself ([Carravetta et al., 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). A simplified HR system using multiple PATs with ON/OFF valves has been proposed as a lower-cost alternative.

**Operation and Maintenance Costs.** IRENA's cost analysis reports that annual O&M costs for small hydropower projects typically range from 1% to 4% of installed costs, with very small (pico) systems reaching up to 6% ([IRENA, 2012](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2012/RE_Technologies_Cost_Analysis-HYDROPOWER.pdf)). Large hydropower projects average 2–2.5%. PATs, having no internal regulating mechanism and a simpler structure with fewer moving parts, may offer lower O&M costs than conventional turbines, though published quantitative comparisons are limited. The absence of wicket gates, adjustable blades, or nozzle deflectors eliminates associated maintenance items, while the use of standardized pump components means that spare parts are readily available and competitively priced. However, operating a pump in reverse mode may impose accelerated wear on bearings and seals designed for pump-mode rotation, partially offsetting these savings. Conventional turbines, though more complex, benefit from mature design standards (IEC 60193, ASME B31.4) and well-established maintenance protocols that can extend service life to 25–50+ years with proper care.

**Payback Period.** The payback period is where the PAT cost advantage becomes most pronounced. Yang et al. (2022) report that the investment payback period for conventional hydraulic turbines can be as long as 15 years, whereas for PATs it can be shortened to 2 years for the same capacity ([Yang et al., 2022, Frontiers in Energy Research](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.1003449/full)). A comparative case study found a cross-flow turbine payback period of 6.04 years — significantly longer than the 3.42 years for a PAT of comparable output ([Rotilio et al., 2023, Int. J. Sustainable Energy](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606)). Another municipal water supply case study reported an estimated payback period of 27 months, with a net present value (NPV) of USD 64,476 and an internal rate of return (IRR) of 63% ([ResearchGate, 2021](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/355299721_Pump-as-turbine_for_energy_recovery_in_municipal_water_supply_networks_A_review)). At the urban network scale, the NetZeroCities project documented a 35 kW PAT plus battery storage prototype designed to recover energy dissipated by a PRV at a drinking water treatment plant entrance ([NetZeroCities](https://netzerocities.app/resource-778)). The LIFE NEXUS Project has mapped energy recovery locations across more than 70 European sites, identifying approximately 3,000 MW of potential hydropower capacity in urban water networks.

The variation in reported payback periods (2–9 years for PATs vs. up to 15 years for conventional turbines) reflects differences in site-specific conditions, energy prices, regulatory costs, and whether civil works are required. The case study citing a 9-year payback (total system cost €373,000, annual savings €36,600) likely reflects a configuration with substantial civil works or regulation system costs. In all cases, PAT systems show shorter payback periods than conventional turbines, with the gap widening for smaller installations where the per-kW cost difference is greatest.

**Techno-Economic Assessment Frameworks.** Fecarotta et al. (2020) provided a comprehensive techno-economic feasibility study of PATs in water distribution networks, evaluating PAT replacement of PRVs across multiple scenarios with NPV analysis ([Fecarotta et al., 2020, Renewable Energy, 162, 1292–1306](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/renene/v162y2020icp1292-1306.html)). Their analysis demonstrated that PAT replacement of PRVs can be economically feasible, achieving both effective pressure control and energy recovery. Subsequent reviews have confirmed that the NPV of PAT operation ranges from 6% to 29% higher than that of the PRV-only scenario across simulated conditions ([Bideris-Davos & Vovos, 2024, Water 16(15), 2129](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/16/15/2129)). Novara et al. (2019) developed predictive cost equations allowing designers to estimate PAT and generator cost from nominal flow rate and available head, providing a standardized preliminary costing tool that conventional turbine selection — dependent on bespoke quotations from specialist manufacturers — typically lacks ([Novara & McNabola, 2019](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/abs/10.1061/(ASCE)WR.1943-5452.0001063)).

In summary, PATs hold a decisive cost advantage across all three lifecycle stages. Procurement costs are roughly half those of conventional turbines (and up to five times lower for installations above 20 kW), installation costs are reduced by the Energy Booster concept though still burdened by civil works and regulation system requirements, and O&M costs benefit from simpler architecture and standardized components. Payback periods for PATs range from 2 to 9 years compared with up to 15 years for conventional turbines, with the most favorable PAT economics occurring at small power outputs typical of urban WDN dissipation nodes. Systemic institutional barriers — including the federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC) and incentive misalignment — are discussed in S4.1.

### Pressure Regulation Capability

In urban water distribution networks (WDNs), pressure regulation is a primary operational requirement: excess pressure must be dissipated to prevent pipe bursts, reduce leakage, and ensure reliable service delivery. Conventional micro-hydroturbines and PATs differ fundamentally in how they meet this requirement, and these differences significantly influence technology selection, system design, and project economics.

#### Conventional Turbines: Mature Internal Regulation Mechanisms

Conventional micro-hydroturbines benefit from purpose-built, internally actuated flow regulation mechanisms that have been refined over decades of hydraulic engineering practice.

**Francis turbines** employ **wicket gates** (also called guide vanes) — a ring of adjustable vanes positioned upstream of the runner — to regulate flow. By varying the opening angle of these gates, the cross-sectional area available to the incoming water is controlled, which in turn adjusts the flow rate and the angular momentum imparted to the runner. This allows the turbine to match power output to demand while maintaining the required head drop across the machine. The wicket gates also function as pre-swirl generators, optimizing the velocity triangle at the runner inlet for efficient energy conversion across a range of operating conditions ([US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines); [GridPro Blog, 2020](https://blog.gridpro.com/understanding-the-flow-through-francis-turbines/)).

**Kaplan turbines** provide **dual regulation** through two independently adjustable mechanisms: wicket gates (as in Francis turbines) plus **adjustable runner blades**. The blade pitch can be optimized for the prevailing head and flow conditions, while the wicket gates provide coarse flow control. This combination — often termed "double regulation" — enables Kaplan turbines to maintain high efficiency across a very wide range of operating conditions, making them particularly suitable for low-head sites with variable flow ([ScienceDirect, Kaplan Turbines overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/kaplan-turbines); [Chesterton blog](https://blog.chesterton.com/sealing/kaplan-turbine-blade-runner-trunnion-sealing-checklist/)). The double-regulation system allows the runner speed to be adapted to any head-flow combination within the turbine's operating envelope ([UnitBV, kinematic modelling](https://webbut.unitbv.ro/index.php/Series_I/article/download/3749/2943/7285)).

#### PATs: Absence of Internal Regulation and External Alternatives

PATs, by contrast, are standard centrifugal pumps operated in reverse mode. A pump is designed to add energy to a fluid, not to regulate it, and as a consequence **PATs possess no internal mechanical regulation devices**. As Yang et al. (2022) state directly: "As a turbine, the pump has no regulating mechanism, and its flow regulation is mainly achieved by setting regulating valves in the inlet and outlet pipelines" ([Yang et al. 2022, Frontiers in Energy Research](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.1003449/full)). Carravetta et al. (2022) confirm this, noting that "the main difficulty is represented by the need for ensuring a required head drop under variable operating conditions, i.e., head and discharge variations" when a PAT is installed in a hydropower plant ([Carravetta et al. 2022, Water 14(10), 1526](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).

To compensate for this inherent limitation, two principal external regulation strategies have been developed:

**Hydraulic Regulation (HR):** A series-parallel hydraulic circuit places a control valve upstream of the PAT and a bypass line in parallel. When the available head exceeds what the PAT can absorb at the current flow, the series valve dissipates the excess pressure. When the flow is too high for the PAT to handle efficiently, the bypass valve diverts a portion of the flow around the PAT. This approach is well investigated in the literature and was found by Carravetta et al. (2013) to be **more flexible and more efficient** than electrical regulation, with a shorter payback period for the electromechanical equipment ([Carravetta et al. 2013, Energies 6(1), 411–424](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/6/1/411); [Cambridge Prisms: Water, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-prisms-water/article/hydraulic-and-electrical-regulations-of-pumps-as-turbines-for-energy-recovery-in-water-distribution-networks-energy-and-economic-analysis/11A8E6728AE92434D1A085B84378FC64)).

**Electrical Regulation (ER):** An inverter (variable speed drive) is placed between the PAT generator and the electrical grid, allowing the rotational speed to be adjusted so that the PAT operates as close to its Best Efficiency Point (BEP) as possible under varying flow and head conditions. ER has been less extensively investigated than HR, and fewer studies are available. The Cambridge study notes that Marini et al. found energy recovery and net present value may increase by more than 30% and 15%, respectively, when the optimal PAT regulation strategy is employed, underscoring the importance of regulation selection ([Cambridge Prisms: Water, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-prisms-water/article/hydraulic-and-electrical-regulations-of-pumps-as-turbines-for-energy-recovery-in-water-distribution-networks-energy-and-economic-analysis/11A8E6728AE92434D1A085B84378FC64)).

**Simplified ON/OFF Systems:** Carravetta et al. (2022) also propose a simplified HR approach using multiple PATs with common ON/OFF valves, trading some regulation precision for lower cost. This option is particularly relevant for small peripheral network branches where recoverable power is very low ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).

Both HR and ER add to the total plant cost: HR requires additional control valves and piping, while ER requires a variable speed drive. These costs must be weighed against the energy gains they enable, and the choice between them depends on site-specific flow variability, available head range, and economic constraints.

#### Can a Standalone PAT Replace a PRV?

A critical question is whether a PAT without any external regulation system can adequately replace a Pressure Reducing Valve (PRV). The evidence indicates that this is generally **not feasible under the variable conditions typical of WDNs**. Lydon et al. (2017) emphasize that PRV sites require maintaining pressure within a prescribed downstream range, and that flow variability at these sites regularly exceeds ±50% of the mean flow rate — a range far wider than the PAT's usable operating window of approximately 70–130% of BEP flow ([Lydon et al. 2017, Renewable Energy 114, 1038–1050](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148117307498)). Sinagra et al. (2023) confirm that PATs have an "absence of any hydraulic system to regulate the downstream service pressure, thus rendering necessary the installation of a downstream control valve" ([Sinagra et al. 2023, Water Resources Management](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-023-03575-0)). Viana et al. (2023) demonstrated this risk empirically: in a real WDN where two PRVs were replaced with PATs, one PAT (PAT2) exhibited negative outlet pressure, adversely affecting the pressure in the downstream district metered area ([Viana et al. 2023, RBCIAMB](https://www.rbciamb.com.br/Publicacoes_RBCIAMB/article/view/1551)).

Carravetta et al. (2022) do propose that "a containment of the plant cost could result from relaxing the constraint on the required head drop, using a stand-alone PAT without regulation," but this approach explicitly sacrifices guaranteed pressure control for cost reduction and is only viable in situations where the network can tolerate the resulting pressure fluctuations ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). The PRV replacement concept — substituting a dissipative valve with an energy recovery device that also regulates pressure — therefore requires either external regulation (HR or ER) or acceptance of degraded pressure control.

#### Variable Operating Strategy (VOS)

The **Variable Operating Strategy (VOS)**, developed by Carravetta et al. (2013), is a design procedure for PAT selection in WDNs that explicitly addresses the challenge of variable operating conditions. The procedure is based on overall plant efficiency criteria and selects the optimal PAT geometry (impeller diameter, rotational speed) for a given flow-head distribution pattern and network back-pressure. When applied under the VOS framework, PAT-based hydropower plants can achieve overall plant efficiency values of up to 50% despite the large variability of flow and head in WDNs ([Carravetta et al. 2013, Energies 6(1), 411–424](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/6/1/411); [Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). The VOS procedure was originally developed for HR mode and subsequently extended to ER mode, enabling a direct comparison between the two regulation strategies. Carravetta et al. (2013) found that HR was more flexible than ER and achieved higher overall efficiency, with a shorter payback period for the electromechanical equipment ([Carravetta et al. 2013](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/6/1/411)).

#### Energy Booster (EB) Concept

The **Energy Booster (EB)**, proposed by Carravetta et al. (2022), is a novel installation concept designed to overcome the dual barriers of civil works cost and regulation complexity that have historically limited PAT deployment in WDNs. The EB consists of two submersible pumps used in inverse (turbine) mode, encapsulated within a water booster housing. This assembly can be installed under an existing manhole by merely opening a hole beneath it, eliminating the need for new civil works or enlarged manholes. The EB approach drastically reduces installation costs and addresses technical concerns about operating electrical and electronic components in the wet, confined environment of underground water infrastructure. The concept is specifically targeted at peripheral network branches where flow rates are very low and recoverable energy is limited, making cost containment critical for economic viability ([Carravetta et al. 2022, Water 14(10), 1526](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). While the laboratory prototype exhibited a modest efficiency of 16% (due to the use of small centrifugal pumps), the authors project that larger plants using more efficient semi-axial submersed pumps could achieve energy conversion ratios of up to 40%.

#### PRS Turbine and Data-Driven Digital Twin Approach

The **PRS turbine** represents a newer approach that bridges the gap between conventional turbines and PATs. It is a crossflow-type turbine equipped with an **internal flap** concentric with the impeller, which allows modification of the turbine's characteristic curve by partially closing the impeller inlet surface. This provides the PRS turbine with an inherent hydraulic regulation capability — a feature that PATs lack entirely. The flap rotation angle (λ) varies from fully closed (λ = 0) to fully open (λ = 100), enabling the turbine to adapt its performance to varying flow and head conditions while maintaining positive outlet pressure ([Sinagra et al. 2023, Water Resources Management 37, 3171](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-023-03575-0)).

Sinagra et al. (2023) proposed an **Advanced Real Time Control (ARTC)** logic for the PRS turbine, built around a **data-driven digital twin** of the surrounding water network. The digital twin incorporates a hydraulic model of the network and provides a one-to-one relationship between configuration parameters (valve settings, turbine flap angle) and state variables (flow rates, pressures). Crucially, the digital twin model equations are **continuously updated** based on recorded sensor measurements, enabling real-time calibration and control. This approach was applied to the Montescuro Ovest pipeline at the San Giovannello station in a real water distribution network. The ARTC logic was shown to provide nearly identical performance to the existing traditional Real Time Control (RTC) in the current operational scenario while being more robust — guaranteeing better hydropower generation under modified operational scenarios. This methodology represents a new approach to regulating valves in hydroelectric plants that are currently managed with traditional automation algorithms ([Sinagra et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-023-03575-0)).

#### Summary of Regulation Capability Differences

The fundamental asymmetry is clear: conventional micro-hydroturbines (Francis, Kaplan) possess mature, internally actuated regulation mechanisms — wicket gates and adjustable blades — that enable them to maintain pressure and efficiency across a wide range of operating conditions without external hardware. PATs, by contrast, are inherently unregulated machines that require external hydraulic or electrical regulation systems to function effectively in the variable environment of a WDN. This difference drives additional system complexity, cost, and design effort for PAT-based installations. The VOS method, Energy Booster concept, and emerging approaches like the PRS turbine with digital twin control represent progressively more sophisticated responses to this fundamental limitation, aiming to make PAT-based energy recovery viable under the real-world constraints of urban water supply networks.

### Design and Operational Complexity

**Turbine Selection Process.** The design pathways for conventional micro-hydroturbines and PATs differ fundamentally. Conventional turbines are selected by matching the site's head and flow regime to a purpose-built machine: Pelton turbines for high-head (>50 m), low-flow sites; Francis turbines for medium-head (40–600 m) applications; and Kaplan turbines for low-head (<70 m), high-flow conditions. This matching relies on mature design standards—[IEC 60193](https://www.evs.ee/et/iec-60193-2019) governs model acceptance tests for hydraulic turbines, storage pumps, and pump-turbines, while [ASME B31.4](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/cfr/ibr/002/asme.b31.4.2002.pdf) provides pipeline transportation system requirements relevant to penstock and conduit design. Performance curves are clearly specified by manufacturers, and runner geometry is optimized for the design point.

PAT selection, by contrast, involves choosing a standard centrifugal pump from manufacturer catalogs (KSB, Grundfos, Flowserve, etc.) and operating it in reverse mode. The key complication is that **pump manufacturers typically do not provide turbine-mode characteristic curves**, making performance prediction a specialized task. As confirmed by multiple sources, "[s]ince the characteristic curves under turbine mode are not supplied by manufacturer of pumps, the theoretical model for energy performance of centrifugal [pumps as turbines] becomes essential" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148112003485)). Researchers have developed several prediction approaches—theoretical methods such as the Stepanoff and Suter parameter approaches, CFD numerical simulation, and experimental calibration per ISO 9906—but each carries trade-offs in accuracy and computational cost ([TechScience review](https://www.techscience.com/fdmp/v21n6/62781/html); [Frontiers in Energy Research](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.1003449/full)). The selection of the optimal PAT therefore depends on a careful economic and investment analysis, as demonstrated by several studies ([Cambridge Prisms: Water](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-prisms-water/article/hydraulic-and-electrical-regulations-of-pumps-as-turbines-for-energy-recovery-in-water-distribution-networks-energy-and-economic-analysis/11A8E6728AE92434D1A085B84378FC64)).

**Civil Works Requirements.** Civil works represent a major barrier to the diffusion of small hydropower in water distribution networks. Carravetta et al. (2022) emphasize that in existing water networks, PRVs are "generally installed in small manholes located under the street pavement," and the need to increase manhole size or install waterproof closures for electrical components adds unmanageable cost ([Water 14(10), 1526](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). Conventional turbine installations typically require purpose-built powerhouses, penstocks, and tailrace structures—substantial civil works even at micro scale. The Energy Booster (EB) concept, which addresses this barrier by encapsulating PATs in a water booster unit installable under existing manholes, is detailed in S1.2. Carravetta et al. (2021) also compared alternative PAT installation layouts, evaluating trade-offs between bypass configurations, series-parallel arrangements, and integrated manhole solutions ([ASCE](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0001480)).

**Control and Automation Systems.** Conventional turbines employ mature governor systems—mechanical or electro-hydraulic—that adjust wicket gates (Francis) or adjustable blades plus wicket gates (Kaplan) to regulate flow and maintain pressure. These internal regulation mechanisms are integrated into the turbine design.

PATs, by contrast, require external regulation systems—hydraulic regulation (HR) with series-parallel control valves or electrical regulation (ER) with variable speed drives—to operate effectively under variable WDN conditions. The choice between HR and ER, including their relative performance, cost implications, and the Variable Operating Strategy (VOS) for optimizing PAT geometry and regulation mode selection, is fully addressed in S1.3.

SCADA integration and remote monitoring are essential for both technologies in urban WDN applications, enabling centralized oversight of distributed generation assets, pressure management, and fault detection. However, SCADA integration for PAT systems must accommodate the additional regulation hardware (control valves, VFDs, sensors) that conventional turbines do not require.

**Grid Connection and Power Quality.** Connecting small-scale generation (typically ≤20 kW at individual PRV nodes) to the electrical grid requires compliance with interconnection standards. [IEEE 1547-2018](https://standards.ieee.org/standard/1547-2018.html) establishes the criteria and requirements for interconnecting distributed energy resources (DERs) with electric power systems, including provisions for anti-islanding protection—a critical safety feature that prevents a DER from continuing to generate power during a utility grid outage. The standard also sets power quality benchmarks, including limits on total harmonic distortion (THD), generally required to be below 5% for small generators. For micro-hydro and PAT installations, compliance with IEEE 1547-2018 necessitates power conditioning equipment, protective relays, and grid-synchronization controls, adding to system complexity and cost.

**Water Hammer Protection.** Both technologies require protection against water hammer—pressure surges caused by sudden flow changes (e.g., valve closure, turbine trip). Primary protective measures include **surge tanks** (vertical columns of water that absorb pressure transients), **air valves** (which admit air to prevent vacuum formation during column separation), and **penstock anchoring** to resist dynamic forces. Surge tank design for hydropower applications is well established, with configurations ranging from simple open tanks to restricted-orifice and differential types ([Power Magazine](https://www.powermag.com/hydraulic-stability-of-surge-tanks/)). For PAT installations in existing pipe networks, water hammer risk is compounded by the presence of additional control valves and bypass lines in the HR configuration, requiring careful transient analysis during design.

**Cavitation Management.** Cavitation—formation and collapse of vapor bubbles when local pressure drops below vapor pressure—poses a particular challenge for PATs operating in reverse mode. Research indicates that PAT reverse mode is more prone to cavitation than conventional pump mode, though damage patterns differ between the two modes ([ResearchGate review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362052789_Pump_as_turbine_cavitation_performance_for_both_conventional_and_reverse_operating_modes_A_review)). In conventional turbines, cavitation is managed through design features such as runner geometry optimization, draft tube design, and material selection (stainless steel runners). For PATs, cavitation risk must be assessed using Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH) analysis, comparing available NPSH (NPSHa) against required NPSH (NPSHr) in turbine mode—data that pump manufacturers do not typically supply. This uncertainty complicates PAT selection and may require experimental or CFD-based cavitation assessment for critical installations.

**System Effectiveness Design Approach.** Carravetta et al. (2013) introduced the concept of **system effectiveness (E)** as a multi-criteria objective function for optimizing PAT design in WDNs, incorporating three factors ([Water 5(3), 1211](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211)):

1. **Capability:** The PAT's performance under hydraulic or electrical regulation, quantified by the energy conversion efficiency at the design point.
2. **Flexibility:** The PAT's ability to operate under conditions slightly different from design values, accounting for the variability of flow and head in real networks.
3. **Reliability:** Based on the lifecycle of PAT components, estimated using the Barringer reliability model, which considers hydraulic conditions and manufacturing standards (since no PAT failure database exists due to the rarity of installations).

This system effectiveness approach extends the VOS methodology by incorporating lifecycle considerations into the optimization procedure, enabling selection of a PAT that balances efficiency, adaptability, and durability rather than maximizing efficiency alone. The methodology was applied to a real system, demonstrating that hydraulic regulation mode is preferable to electrical regulation for API-standard pumps ([Water 5(3), 1211](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/5/3/1211)).

**Optimization Models for PRV Energy and Water Reduction.** Fecarotta and McNabola (2017) developed a **mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP)** model for the optimal localization of PATs in water distribution networks, aiming to maximize energy recovery and reduce water leakage simultaneously ([Water Resources Management 31(15)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/waterr/v31y2017i15d10.1007_s11269-017-1795-2.html)). In this formulation, binary variables represent the presence of PATs on network links, and the nonlinear hydraulic equations of the network are coupled with the optimization procedure. The MINLP framework was found to be the most suitable technique for PAT placement, outperforming NLP and genetic algorithm approaches ([Nguyen et al. 2020, Water 12(7), 1979](https://d-nb.info/1241963517/34)). Nguyen et al. (2020) extended this work by introducing additional constraints on minimum power generation and feasible operating ranges (flow and head bounds), ensuring that selected PAT locations yield adequate energy output and that PATs operate within their design envelopes. Morani et al. (2022) further developed a MINLP model for joint PAT and PRV placement, achieving a 21% improvement in leakage reduction compared to previous studies on the same benchmark network ([Environmental Science Proceedings 21(1), 2](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-4931/21/1/2)).

**Summary of Complexity Differences.** Conventional turbines offer simpler operational integration—mature governors, clear performance curves, and established design standards reduce engineering uncertainty—but require bespoke design and substantial civil works. PATs leverage standardized pump products and dramatically lower procurement costs, but introduce complexity in turbine-mode performance prediction, external regulation systems (HR or ER), cavitation assessment under variable conditions, and the need for real-time control to manage off-design operation. The Energy Booster concept and system effectiveness design approach represent significant advances in reducing PAT installation and operational complexity, but the technology still lacks the decades of operational experience and standardized design frameworks that conventional turbines enjoy.

### Expected Service Life

Conventional micro-hydroturbines and Pump as Turbine (PAT) systems differ markedly in expected service life, driven by fundamental differences in their design heritage, operating environment, and degradation mechanisms. Conventional turbines are purpose-built for turbine operation with mature design standards, while PATs operate standard pumps in reverse mode — an operating condition for which the pump was not originally optimized.

#### Conventional Micro-hydroturbine Design Life

Conventional micro-hydroturbines are engineered specifically for hydraulic-to-mechanical energy conversion, with design standards such as ASME B31.4 and IEC 60193 governing their construction. Industry sources indicate a typical **design life of 40–50 years** for well-maintained units, though the **economic life** — the point at which cumulative operation and maintenance (O&M) costs exceed the value of generated power — averages **22–28 years** under standard operating conditions ([Flow Machinery cost analysis](https://flowmachinery.com/rotating-equipment/water-turbine-cost-analysis-purchase-installation-and-lifecy)). Natural Resources Canada's micro-hydropower introduction documents cite a service life of **20–30 years** for micro-hydropower systems with low maintenance costs ([Natural Resources Canada, Introduction to Micro-Hydropower Systems](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/sites/nrcan/files/canmetenergy/files/pubs/Intro_MicroHydro_ENG.pdf)). The NCAT micro-hydro guide characterizes micro-hydro systems as long-term structural investments with "many years of operating life" ([NCAT, Micro-Hydro Power: A Beginner's Guide](https://www.ncat.org/publication/micro-hydro-power-a-beginners-guide-to-design-and-installation/)). For context, hydropower industry literature notes that major hydroelectric equipment — turbines, generators, and large valves — has longer lifecycles than equivalent equipment in other power generation sectors, and that refurbishment (rather than replacement) is often the preferred approach during major overhauls ([National Hydropower Association, 2022](https://hydro.org/powerhouse/article/hydropowers-universal-dilemma-repair-or-replace/)).

#### PAT Service Life in Reverse Mode

PAT service life data in turbine mode remain limited, as the technology has been studied primarily for performance prediction rather than long-term durability. However, several factors suggest that PAT service life may be shorter than that of purpose-built turbines:

1. **Bearing wear under reversed thrust characteristics.** In turbine mode, the PAT rotor rotates in the opposite direction to pump mode, altering radial and axial thrust patterns. The L10 bearing life computation methodology is the same for turbine and pump modes, but the radial thrust vs. flow-rate characteristic is fundamentally different: in pump mode, maximum radial thrust occurs at zero flow, whereas in turbine mode the thrust is lowest at the zero-power-output flow rate and rises continuously from there ([Bradshaw, 2023, Reverse Operating Pumps Part 5](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/reverse-operating-pumps-part-5-simon-bradshaw-h1ure)). The higher differential head in turbine mode can produce elevated radial thrust, requiring verification that rolling-element bearings have adequate life over the expected operating range (typically targeting >25,000 hours L10 life). Additionally, bearing lubrication systems — constant-level oilers, oil rings, and forced-lubrication circuits — may require modification for reverse rotation, as optimized tilting-pad bearings with directed oil feeds (e.g., leading-edge groove designs) may only function correctly in one rotational direction ([Bradshaw, 2023](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/reverse-operating-pumps-part-5-simon-bradshaw-h1ure)).

2. **Seal degradation.** Mechanical seals in standard pumps are often designed for a single direction of rotation. Multi-spring single seals can typically operate in either direction, but gas seals or seals with pumping rings are frequently manufactured for one rotational sense and must be specified for reverse rotation at the time of purchase ([Bradshaw, 2023](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/reverse-operating-pumps-part-5-simon-bradshaw-h1ure)). Reverse-mode operation can accelerate seal wear, particularly under the variable flow and head conditions characteristic of water distribution networks (WDNs).

3. **Cavitation susceptibility.** PATs operating in reverse mode are more prone to cavitation than in conventional pump mode, particularly under off-design flow conditions. Research on PAT cavitation performance indicates that while PAT reverse mode is more susceptible to cavitation onset, the resulting damage may be less severe than in conventional pump mode; however, impeller geometric modifications can mitigate this risk ([Renewable Energy, vol. 168, 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/rensus/v168y2022ics1364032122006700.html)). In WDN applications with highly variable diurnal and seasonal flow patterns, repeated cavitation cycles can reduce impeller and casing integrity over time.

4. **Overspeed risk.** A PAT is susceptible to runaway overspeed under load loss (generator malfunction, grid disconnection, or coupling failure). The runaway speed can be significantly higher than normal operating speed depending on pump specific speed, and the rotor system must be designed to withstand this condition or a separate safety system must be installed ([Bradshaw, 2023](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/reverse-operating-pumps-part-5-simon-bradshaw-h1ure)).

A 2025 review of PAT performance prediction and optimal design explicitly identifies wear and corrosion as ongoing challenges and highlights the need for advanced materials and surface treatment technologies to extend PAT service life ([Sun et al., 2025, Fluid Dynamics & Materials Processing, 21(6), 1261–1298](https://www.techscience.com/fdmp/v21n6/62781/html); note: published June 2025, slightly outside the primary time scope, cited as supplementary).

#### Degradation Mechanisms: Cavitation and Sediment Wear

Both conventional turbines and PATs are subject to three principal degradation mechanisms:

- **Cavitation:** Vapor bubble collapse on runner/impeller surfaces causes pitting and material removal. In conventional turbines, cavitation is managed through proper hydraulic design ( runner geometry optimization per IEC 60193), adequate submergence, and sometimes air injection. In PATs, cavitation risk is heightened in reverse mode due to altered flow patterns at the impeller inlet, particularly under variable WDN conditions. Predictive maintenance using acoustic emission monitoring can detect incipient cavitation before pitting occurs ([Flow Machinery cost analysis](https://flowmachinery.com/rotating-equipment/water-turbine-cost-analysis-purchase-installation-and-lifecy)).

- **Sediment erosion:** Abrasive wear from suspended particles in the water stream is a primary degradation mechanism, particularly in water supply systems where turbidity and particulate matter are present. In Francis turbines, erosion predominantly affects runner blades, guide vanes, stay vanes, facing plates, and labyrinth seals, with four erosion types identified at guide vanes (turbulence, secondary flow, leakage, and acceleration erosion) and three at the runner (turbulence, acceleration, and cross-flow erosion) ([Shrestha et al., 2024, Frontiers in Mechanical Engineering, 12:1526120](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/mechanical-engineering/articles/10.3389/fmech.2024.1526120/full)). Research on sediment erosion in Francis turbines documents efficiency losses of **4–8% under heavy sediment loads** ([Thapa et al., sediment erosion in hydro turbines](https://www.academia.edu/15225037/Sediment_erosion_in_hydro_turbines_and_its_effect_on_the_flow_around_guide_vanes_of_Francis_turbine)), and one study reports a mean efficiency loss of approximately **35% over two years** for Francis turbines operating in sand-laden water ([Kashyap et al., 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11033088/)). Pelton turbines experience erosion primarily on injector needles and bucket cutout regions, with severity dependent on needle opening and sediment concentration ([Shrestha et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/mechanical-engineering/articles/10.3389/fmech.2024.1526120/full)).

- **Material fatigue:** Cyclic loading from pressure fluctuations, start-stop sequences, and grid-induced transients contributes to fatigue cracking in runner blades, guide vanes, and shaft components over multi-decade service lives.

#### Blade Erosion–Efficiency Relationship in Francis Turbines

The ReportSpec references a claim that "15% blade erosion → Francis efficiency drop from ~91% to ~85%." This specific quantitative relationship appears in a practitioner-level industry cost analysis, which states: *"At 15% blade erosion (measurable via ultrasonic thickness mapping), a Francis turbine's peak efficiency drops from 91.3% to 84.7% — and crucially, the efficiency cliff steepens, meaning a 10% flow reduction now causes a 22% power loss instead of 14%"* ([Flow Machinery cost analysis](https://flowmachinery.com/rotating-equipment/water-turbine-cost-analysis-purchase-installation-and-lifecy)). This source is a commercial practitioner blog rather than a peer-reviewed publication. The general principle it describes — that blade erosion progressively reduces Francis turbine efficiency and steepens the efficiency-loss curve — is consistent with peer-reviewed findings that sediment erosion causes efficiency losses of 4–8% under heavy sediment loads and can reach ~35% over two years under severe conditions ([Thapa et al.](https://www.academia.edu/15225037/Sediment_erosion_in_hydro_turbines_and_its_effect_on_the_flow_around_guide_vanes_of_Francis_turbine); [Kashyap et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11033088/)). However, the specific "15% erosion → 91.3% to 84.7%" figure should be treated as a practitioner estimate pending verification from peer-reviewed experimental or field studies.

#### Component Replacement Cycles and Costs

Maintenance costs are structured across three tiers, as documented in industry cost analyses:

| Maintenance Tier | Description | Typical Cost (100 kW unit) |
|---|---|---|
| **Preventive** | Annual oil analysis (ASTM D6595), bearing vibration trending (ISO 10816-3), seal inspection | ~$2,100/year |
| **Predictive** | Thermal imaging of stator windings, acoustic emission for cavitation onset, digital twin-based performance deviation alerts | +$4,800/year (reduces unplanned downtime by ~71%) |
| **Corrective** | Runner refurbishment ($18k–$32k), generator rewind ($12k), or full replacement ($67k+) | Triggered by cumulative efficiency loss >7% or vibration >7.1 mm/s RMS |

*Sources: [Flow Machinery cost analysis](https://flowmachinery.com/rotating-equipment/water-turbine-cost-analysis-purchase-installation-and-lifecy); [NREL, 2023 Hydropower Reliability Report (referenced therein)](https://www.nrel.gov/docs/fy26osti/101207.pdf)*

Component replacement cycles vary significantly by site conditions. In high-silt environments, runner blade refurbishment may be needed every 2–5 years, while low-silt, clean-water sites may extend corrective intervals to 5–10+ years. A Vermont pilot project (Otter Creek, 2023) demonstrated that MEMS-based pressure sensors detecting incipient cavitation before pitting occurs extended corrective maintenance intervals from every 2.1 years to every 5.8 years, effectively extending runner life from 12 to 27 years ([Flow Machinery cost analysis](https://flowmachinery.com/rotating-equipment/water-turbine-cost-analysis-purchase-installation-and-lifecy)).

#### Predictive Maintenance and Digital Twin Technologies

Modern predictive maintenance strategies — incorporating thermal imaging, acoustic emission monitoring, and vibration trending per ISO 10816-3 — can significantly extend the economic life of both conventional turbines and PATs. Digital twin calibration, which uses real-time sensor data to update performance models and predict degradation trajectories, has been shown to reduce Year 1–5 uncertainty bands by approximately 63%, enabling more precise maintenance scheduling and extending economic life from the typical 22–28 years to 35+ years in DOE-funded demonstration projects ([Flow Machinery cost analysis](https://flowmachinery.com/rotating-equipment/water-turbine-cost-analysis-purchase-installation-and-lifecy)). For PATs specifically, a thesis at Trinity College Dublin has developed predictive maintenance solutions focused on cavitation detection in PAT hydropower systems ([TARA thesis, Trinity College Dublin](https://www.tara.tcd.ie/bitstreams/76f4604c-3c20-4495-8e38-d8a3b6c27a51/download)), though this work remains in early stages.

#### Summary Comparison

Conventional micro-hydroturbines offer a well-established service life of **40–50 years design life** (22–28 years economic life) backed by mature design standards and decades of field experience. PATs, leveraging standardized pump products, benefit from the pump industry's manufacturing maturity but face reverse-mode-specific degradation risks — altered bearing thrust patterns, seal directionality, heightened cavitation susceptibility, and overspeed hazards — that can shorten service life relative to purpose-built turbines. The absence of comprehensive long-term field data on PAT durability in WDN applications remains a gap; the limited evidence suggests that with appropriate predictive maintenance and impeller/bearing modifications, PAT service life can approach that of conventional small turbines, but under variable WDN conditions without such measures, accelerated wear on bearings and seals is likely.

### Market Availability and Supply Chain

**Supplier Ecosystem: PATs vs. Conventional Micro-hydroturbines**

PATs draw on the mature, globally standardized centrifugal pump market, which features a large number of suppliers across all major regions. KSB, one of the world's leading pump manufacturers, explicitly documents that standard centrifugal pumps can be run in reverse as turbines without any modification to the casing or impeller geometry, requiring only that flow rate, head, and rotational speed rules be observed ([KSB, "Turbine Mode"](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)). KSB identifies key PAT supply-chain advantages: a closely spaced selection chart of standard pumps offering a broad performance range, wide material and sealing options, favorably priced standard products, short delivery times, and minimum control and monitoring requirements. The principal disadvantage noted is the lack of internal control equipment and slightly inferior efficiencies compared with bespoke turbines ([KSB, "Turbine Mode"](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)). SPP Pumps, another major centrifugal pump manufacturer, has offered PAT solutions since 2009 and reports that pump and spare parts are more readily accessible than those for conventional turbines, with power output up to 500 kW achievable and no requirement for large infrastructure ([SPP Pumps, 2022](https://www.spppumps.com/embracing-hydroelectric-power-the-concept-reality-of-pumps-as-turbines-pats/)).

Conventional micro-hydroturbines, by contrast, rely on a more fragmented supplier base. In the United States, key suppliers include Natel Energy (specializing in FishSafe™ Kaplan, Francis, and propeller turbines for small modular hydropower), Canyon Hydro (over 40 years of experience building hydroelectric systems to utility specifications), and Soar Hydropower (focusing on energy recovery from water systems) ([micro-hydro-power.com, "Hydro Turbine Manufacturers USA"](https://www.micro-hydro-power.com/hydro-turbine-manufacturers-usa/); [Natel Energy](https://www.natelenergy.com/)). These firms typically manufacture bespoke or semi-bespoke turbines matched to site-specific head and flow conditions, resulting in longer lead times and higher engineering costs. Global heavyweights such as Voith Hydro and Andritz Hydro serve the broader hydropower market but generally focus on larger installations rather than the sub-100 kW urban WDN segment ([micro-hydro-power.com](https://www.micro-hydro-power.com/hydro-turbine-manufacturers-usa/)).

**Regional Deployment Patterns and Representative Case Studies**

PAT deployment in urban water distribution networks varies significantly by region:

The EB prototype used Caprari submersible pumps (models E4XED30/5 and E4XED50/6) and demonstrated seven operating conditions through parallel branch configurations, though measured BEP efficiencies were low (0.31–0.32) due to the small pump sizes used.

- **Middle East (Iran)**: A case study in Urmia city examined PAT installation in a PRV bypass configuration for energy recovery from high-pressure zones in the water distribution network. The study confirmed that PATs can replace PRVs to simultaneously regulate pressure and generate electricity, though the analysis highlighted the need for careful PAT selection to match site-specific head and flow conditions ([ResearchGate, "Energy recovery from water distribution networks using micro hydropower: A case study in Iran"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359986841_Energy_recovery_from_water_distribution_networks_using_micro_hydropower_A_case_study_in_Iran)).

**PAT Market Maturity by Region**

PAT market maturity is highest in Europe, where academic research (led by groups at the University of Naples, University of Padua, and others) has been translated into pilot installations and commercialization pathways such as the Energy Booster concept. The European regulatory environment, with stringent energy-efficiency mandates and sustainability targets, provides favorable conditions for PAT adoption. North America is at an emerging stage, with the Grundfos–InPipe Energy partnership representing the first major commercial deployment in the US municipal water sector. The Asia-Pacific region shows growing interest, driven by rapid urbanization and infrastructure development, though PAT-specific deployments in urban WDNs remain limited compared to conventional small hydropower installations. The Middle East, particularly Iran, has seen academic and pilot-scale interest in PAT technology for water network energy recovery, though large-scale commercial deployment has not yet materialized. Overall, PAT technology in urban water supply networks remains in the early-commercialization phase globally, with the supply-chain advantages of standardized pump products not yet fully translated into widespread utility adoption due to institutional and economic barriers discussed in S4.1.

**Dimensions with Greatest Divergence**

The two technologies exhibit the greatest divergence in three dimensions:

1. **Operational Efficiency at BEP**: Conventional micro-hydroturbines hold a decisive advantage, with BEP efficiencies of 80–92% compared to PATs' 65–75% — a gap of approximately 10–20 percentage points. This gap is intrinsic: conventional turbines are purpose-optimized for energy extraction, while PATs operate pumps in reverse mode with suboptimal hydraulic geometry for turbine operation. KSB explicitly acknowledges this, noting "slightly inferior efficiencies compared with turbines designed and manufactured to order" ([KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)). However, PATs partially compensate through the VOS method, achieving plant efficiency up to 50% under the variable flow/head conditions typical of urban water distribution networks ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).

2. **Pressure Regulation Capability**: Conventional turbines possess mature internal regulation mechanisms — wicket gates for Francis, adjustable blades plus wicket gates for Kaplan — enabling efficient flow control across wide operating ranges without external devices. PATs have no internal mechanical regulation devices whatsoever ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526); [KSB](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998); [Yang et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.1003449/full)), requiring external hydraulic regulation (HR with series-parallel control valves) or electrical regulation (ER with variable speed drives). This fundamental difference means that PAT installations in water distribution networks — where flow and head vary continuously with demand — incur additional regulation system costs and complexity that conventional turbine installations do not.

3. **Cost and Payback**: PATs hold a decisive advantage in procurement cost (estimated at 50% or less of conventional turbines per [NCAT](https://www.ncat.org/publication/micro-hydro-power-a-beginners-guide-to-design-and-installation/), and potentially as little as 10% per [Crespo Chacón et al. 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/11/1/149)) and payback period (as little as 2 years vs. up to 15 years for conventional turbines per [Yang et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.1003449/full); cross-flow turbine payback 6.04 years vs. PAT 3.42 years per [Nasir et al. 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606)). This cost advantage is the primary driver of PAT adoption in urban WDNs, where recoverable power at individual dissipation nodes is generally ≤20 kW ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)), making the lower procurement cost critical for project viability.

In the remaining dimensions — installation cost, O&M, design complexity, service life, and market availability — the picture is more nuanced. PATs benefit from standardized supply chains and shorter delivery times, but their installation costs can be inflated by civil works requirements and additional regulation systems. The Energy Booster concept partially addresses this by encapsulating the PAT within existing manholes ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). Conventional turbines offer longer established service lives (25–50+ years) and mature design standards, while PAT service-life data remains limited. For urban WDN energy recovery specifically, the trade-off between higher efficiency and regulation capability (conventional turbines) versus lower cost and standardized availability (PATs) remains the central technology selection dilemma, with the ≤20 kW recoverable power per node heavily favoring PAT economics despite their efficiency and regulation disadvantages.

## Technical Parameters and Applicability of Various Turbine Types

### Impulse Turbines: Pelton, Turgo, Crossflow

Impulse turbines convert water's potential energy into kinetic energy via nozzles, directing high-velocity jets onto runner buckets or blades that operate at atmospheric pressure. They are classified by low specific speed and are best suited for high-head, low-to-medium flow conditions. The three principal impulse turbine types—Pelton, Turgo, and Crossflow—occupy distinct niches within the head-flow parameter space, each with characteristic efficiency profiles and pressure regulation mechanisms.

#### Pelton Turbine

The Pelton wheel, invented by American engineer **Lester Allan Pelton** in the 1870s and patented in October 1880, is the archetypal impulse turbine. Its runner consists of spoon-shaped buckets mounted on the periphery of a circular disc; one to six nozzles direct water jets tangentially onto the buckets, turning the jet through nearly 180° to transfer momentum with minimal exit velocity. Maximum power transfer occurs when the runner rim speed equals half the jet velocity ([Wikipedia – Pelton wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelton_wheel)).

- **Applicable head range:** 15–1,800 m, with practical installations commonly above 50 m and extending to several hundred metres for large utility-scale units ([Wikipedia – Pelton wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelton_wheel); [Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/pelton-and-turgo-turbines/)).
- **Applicable flow rate range:** Low flow by design; as little as ~0.005 m³/s (≈0.2 cfs) on the smallest units, rising to ~1 m³/s on larger multi-jet systems ([Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/); [Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/pelton-and-turgo-turbines/)).
- **Design-point efficiency range:** 80–90% at BEP for small hydro units; peak efficiencies up to 95% achievable on well-designed systems, with 90% routinely attained even at micro scale (≥5 kW) ([Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/pelton-and-turgo-turbines/); [US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines)). Multi-jet Pelton turbines maintain high efficiency down to ~10% of design flow ([Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)).
- **Pressure regulation:** Flow is regulated by a **needle nozzle (spear-jet)**—a cone-shaped spear that moves axially within the nozzle to vary the jet orifice area. In emergency shutdowns, a **deflector** diverts the jet away from the buckets to prevent runaway speed, allowing the spear to be closed slowly and limiting water-hammer overpressure. Multiple jets can be staged to broaden the efficient flow range ([US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines); [Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/); [Canyon Hydro Guide](https://www.canyonhydro.com/guide/HydroGuide4.html)).
- **Specific speed (n_s):** 12–30 for single-jet configurations; multi-jet variants increase the effective specific speed by a factor of √N (where N = number of jets) ([OSTI – Small Hydro Power in China](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/20187230)).
- **Suitability for urban water supply networks:** Limited. Pelton turbines discharge to atmospheric pressure and cannot maintain downstream pipeline pressure, making them poorly suited for in-pipe installations in municipal water distribution systems where residual pressure must be preserved ([Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)).

#### Turgo Turbine

The **Turgo turbine** was designed in 1919 by **Gilbert Gilkes & Gordon** (UK) as a modification of the Pelton wheel. The key geometric difference is that the water jet strikes one side of the runner and exits from the opposite side, rather than being reversed within a single bucket. This allows the runner to be approximately half the diameter of a Pelton runner for the same power, and twice the specific speed, enabling greater water flow capacity ([Wikipedia – Turgo turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turgo_turbine); [Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/pelton-and-turgo-turbines/)).

- **Applicable head range:** 15–300 m, bridging the gap between Pelton and Francis turbines ([Wikipedia – Turgo turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turgo_turbine); [OSTI – Small Hydro Power in China](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/20187230)).
- **Applicable flow rate range:** Medium flow—higher than Pelton for the same runner diameter; specific speed 20–70 ([OSTI – Small Hydro Power in China](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/20187230); [Wikipedia – Turgo turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turgo_turbine)).
- **Design-point efficiency range:** ~87% in operational service; up to 90% in factory and laboratory tests ([Wikipedia – Turgo turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turgo_turbine)). Some sources cite a broader range of 80–87% for field conditions and 85–92% peak for well-optimised units ([FST Generator](https://www.fstgenerator.com/news/development-history-and-characteristics-of-the-turgo-turbine/)).
- **Pressure regulation:** Uses the same **spear-jet (needle nozzle)** mechanism as the Pelton for flow regulation, with deflectors for emergency jet diversion. Fixed-jet configurations are also available for constant-flow sites, though these sacrifice regulation capability ([Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/pelton-and-turgo-turbines/); [Wikipedia – Turgo turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turgo_turbine)).
- **Specific speed (n_s):** 20–70 for single-jet units, placing it between Pelton (12–30) and Francis (80–300) ([OSTI – Small Hydro Power in China](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/20187230)).
- **Suitability for urban water supply networks:** Moderate. The Turgo's higher flow capacity and lower cost make it more adaptable than Pelton for small hydro applications, but like all impulse turbines it discharges at atmospheric pressure and cannot inherently maintain downstream pipeline pressure. It is better suited to run-of-river or canal-drop settings than to in-pipe WDN installations ([Wikipedia – Turgo turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turgo_turbine); [Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)).

#### Crossflow (Banki-Michell-Ossberger) Turbine

The **crossflow turbine**—also known as the **Bánki-Michell** or **Ossberger** turbine—has a distinctive cylindrical, drum-shaped runner through which water passes transversely (across the blades) rather than axially or radially. The development history begins with **Anthony Michell**, an Australian engineer who obtained patents in 1903; the Hungarian engineer **Donát Bánki** subsequently improved the design, and the German engineer **Fritz Ossberger** was granted patents in 1922 (No. 361593) and 1933 (No. 615445). The company founded by Ossberger remains the leading manufacturer of this turbine type today ([Wikipedia – Cross-flow turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-flow_turbine); [US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines)).

Water enters through a nozzle and passes through the runner blades twice—first from outside to inside, then from inside to outside—transferring kinetic energy on both passes. Approximately two-thirds of the power is transferred on the first pass and one-third on the second. The blade channels are self-cleaning, as debris is flushed out with the exiting water ([Wikipedia – Cross-flow turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-flow_turbine)).

- **Applicable head range:** 2–200 m, with optimal performance below ~40 m and reduced effectiveness above ~130 m ([Wikipedia – Cross-flow turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-flow_turbine); [OSTI – Small Hydro Power in China](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/20187230); [Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)).
- **Applicable flow rate range:** Higher flow than Pelton; typically 0.04–5 m³/s (≈1.5–175 cfs) for small units, with larger systems reaching up to ~10 m³/s ([ResearchGate – Crossflow Banki turbines review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360934718_Optimal_design_process_of_crossflow_Banki_turbines_Literature_review_and_novel_expeditious_equations); [Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)).
- **Design-point efficiency range:** 75–85% at BEP, with a maximum around 80% for well-designed units. Some sources report 60–80% for smaller or less optimised installations, with the majority of cases below 10 m head ([ResearchGate – Crossflow Banki turbines review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360934718_Optimal_design_process_of_crossflow_Banki_turbines_Literature_review_and_novel_expeditious_equations); [Wikipedia – Cross-flow turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-flow_turbine); [ScienceDirect – Low-head inverted cross-flow turbine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S019689042400462X)). The defining characteristic is a **flat efficiency curve** under varying load: efficiency remains high from ~1/6 of maximum flow up to full flow, outperforming Pelton and Francis at partial load ([Wikipedia – Cross-flow turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-flow_turbine)).
- **Pressure regulation:** Flow is regulated by **inlet guide vanes** (typically two, arranged so the water jets do not interfere). The guide vanes can direct water onto a portion of the runner during low-flow conditions or the entire runner when flows are higher, providing flexible operation at 33%, 66%, or 100% output. The guide vanes also serve as shut-off valves between penstock and turbine ([Wikipedia – Cross-flow turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-flow_turbine); [Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)).
- **Specific speed (n_s):** 20–80, overlapping with the lower end of the Turgo range ([OSTI – Small Hydro Power in China](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/20187230)).
- **Suitability for urban water supply networks:** Moderate to good. The crossflow turbine's ability to handle higher flows at lower heads, combined with its flat efficiency curve under variable flow and self-cleaning design, makes it more adaptable to the variable flow conditions encountered in WDNs than Pelton or Turgo. Its simple construction, low maintenance requirements (only two bearings and three rotating elements), and standardised componentry are advantageous for small-scale installations. However, like all impulse turbines, it operates at atmospheric pressure and cannot directly maintain downstream pipeline pressure ([Wikipedia – Cross-flow turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-flow_turbine); [Wyoming Small Hydropower Handbook](https://wyomingrenewables.org/renewable-technologies/hydroelectric/wyoming-small-hydropower-handbook/evaluating-resources/electromechanical-equipment2/)).

#### Comparative Summary and Turbine Selection Logic

The three impulse turbine types form a continuum across the head-flow parameter space:

| Parameter | Pelton | Turgo | Crossflow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head range (m) | 15–1,800 | 15–300 | 2–200 |
| Flow rate | Low | Medium | Medium–high |
| BEP efficiency (%) | 80–90+ | 80–90 | 75–85 |
| Partial-load efficiency | Good (multi-jet: down to 10%) | Good | Excellent (1/6 to max flow) |
| Regulation | Needle nozzle + deflector | Needle nozzle + deflector | Guide vanes (33/66/100%) |
| Specific speed (n_s) | 12–30 | 20–70 | 20–80 |
| Self-cleaning | No | No | Yes |

For urban water supply network applications, impulse turbines face a fundamental limitation: they discharge at atmospheric pressure and cannot maintain downstream pipeline pressure, which is a core requirement in municipal water distribution. Among the three, the **Crossflow turbine** offers the best combination of partial-load efficiency, variable-flow tolerance, and self-cleaning operation, making it the most adaptable impulse type for WDN energy recovery—though still requiring external pressure regulation (e.g., series-parallel control valves) to replace or complement a pressure reducing valve. The **Pelton** is best reserved for high-head, low-flow scenarios (e.g., mountain reservoirs or high-pressure transmission mains), while the **Turgo** occupies a middle ground where medium heads and moderate flows prevail. None of the impulse types is as well-suited to in-pipe WDN energy recovery as reaction-type turbines (Francis, PAT), which can operate under pressure and integrate more naturally with existing pipeline infrastructure ([US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines); [Canyon Hydro Guide](https://www.canyonhydro.com/guide/HydroGuide4.html); [Savree – Hydro Turbine Comparison](https://savree.com/en/product/hydro-turbine-comparison-model)).

### Reaction Turbines: Francis, Kaplan, Semi-Kaplan, Bulb, Tubular, Archimedean Screw

Reaction turbines generate power from the combined action of pressure and moving water, with the runner placed directly in the water stream so that water flows over the blades rather than striking each individually. They are generally used for sites with lower heads and higher flows than impulse turbines and constitute the most common turbine category in modern hydropower ([US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines)). Six key reaction turbine types are examined below.

**Francis Turbine.** The Francis turbine is an inward-flow reaction turbine that combines radial and axial flow concepts, making it a mixed-flow runner. It is the most common water turbine in use today ([Wikipedia – Francis turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_turbine)). Francis turbines are primarily used for medium- to high-head applications, with the best performance typically seen at heads between 100–300 m, although they have been deployed across a wider range of approximately 40–600 m ([Wikipedia – Francis turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_turbine); [US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines)). The DOE cites the typical range as 130–2,000 ft (approximately 40–600 m), noting they have also been used for lower heads. Design-point efficiencies typically reach 85–92%, with some large modern units exceeding 95% at prototype scale ([Wikipedia – Francis turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_turbine); [Firgelli Automation](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/mechanisms/turbine-and-gate)). The Francis runner has fixed blades (usually nine or more), and flow regulation is achieved through **wicket gates** (also called guide vanes) — a ring of adjustable vanes surrounding the runner that control the rate and angle of water entering the turbine ([Wikipedia – Francis turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_turbine); [US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines)). The specific speed of Francis turbines is classified as medium, typically in the range of 50–250 (metric), which positions them between the low-speed Pelton and high-speed Kaplan designs ([Savree](https://savree.com/en/product/hydro-turbine-comparison-model)). Francis turbines can operate across a wide range of heads and flows and do not reduce markedly in efficiency until the load drops to roughly 40% of rated output ([Savree](https://savree.com/en/product/hydro-turbine-comparison-model)).

**Kaplan Turbine.** The Kaplan turbine is an axial-flow reaction turbine with adjustable runner blades, developed by Viktor Kaplan in 1913. It combines automatically adjusted propeller blades with automatically adjusted wicket gates to achieve high efficiency over a wide range of flow and head conditions — a feature known as "double regulation" ([Wikipedia – Kaplan turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaplan_turbine); [US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines)). Kaplan turbines are used for low-head, high-flow applications, with typical head ranges of approximately 2–70 m and outputs from 5 to 200 MW ([Wikipedia – Kaplan turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaplan_turbine)). Smaller units can operate at heads as low as 1.5 m with flow rates of 3–30 m³/s ([Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/kaplan-turbines/)). Design-point efficiencies typically exceed 90%, with some units reaching 94% ([Wikipedia – Kaplan turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaplan_turbine); [Scribd – Kaplan Turbine Lecture](https://www.scribd.com/document/472329049/Lecture-15-Kaplan-Turbine-pdf)). The inlet guide-vanes regulate the amount of flow passing through the turbine and introduce varying amounts of swirl, while the rotor blade pitch is adjustable from a flat profile for very low flows to a heavily pitched profile for high flows. This dual adjustability yields a very wide flow operating range and a flat efficiency curve ([Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/kaplan-turbines/)). The specific speed is high, typically exceeding 250 ([Savree](https://savree.com/en/product/hydro-turbine-comparison-model)).

**Semi-Kaplan Turbine.** Semi-Kaplan turbines are simplified variants of the full Kaplan design in which only one of the two regulation mechanisms is retained — either adjustable inlet guide-vanes (with fixed rotor blades) or adjustable rotor blades (with fixed guide-vanes), but not both ([Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/kaplan-turbines/)). This reduces manufacturing complexity and cost but compromises performance across a wide flow range. For applications where flow does not vary significantly, semi-Kaplan turbines can be a more cost-effective choice than full Kaplan units. The efficiency curve for a semi-Kaplan with adjustable blades (but fixed guide-vanes) falls between the full-Kaplan curve and the fixed-propeller curve, while a semi-Kaplan with adjustable guide-vanes (but fixed blades) shows a similar intermediate performance profile ([Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/kaplan-turbines/)). Modern semi-Kaplan designs can achieve efficiency plateaus of approximately 90–93% across 40–110% of rated flow, substantially outperforming Francis turbines in run-of-river applications with variable flow ([Eureka PatSnap](https://eureka.patsnap.com/report-comparison-study-of-francis-turbine-versus-semi-kaplan-turbine-regarding-adjustability-performance-in-run-of-river-plants)). The applicable head and flow ranges are broadly similar to those of full Kaplan turbines.

**Bulb Turbine.** Bulb turbines are a variation of the Kaplan turbine in which the generator, wicket gates, and runner are all housed inside a streamlined bulb nacelle that sits directly within the water flow passage, enabling in-pipe installation ([US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines); [Wikipedia – Kaplan turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaplan_turbine)). They are designed for very low head applications, with operational head ranges of approximately 1–25 m (ANDRITZ reports a range of 0.5–30 m) and high flow rates ([ANDRITZ Hydro](https://www.andritz.com/hydro-en/hydronews/hydro-news-29/hy-news-hn29-13-bulb-turbines-hydro); [US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines)). Peak efficiencies can reach above 90% for larger units, though ultra-low-head designs may achieve peak efficiencies around 74% ([ScienceDirect – Bulb Turbines](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/bulb-turbines); [ResearchGate – Ultra-low head bulb turbine](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335343555_Performance_evaluation_of_a_bulb_turbine_designed_for_ultra-low_head_applications)). Pressure regulation is provided by wicket gates (guide vanes) similar to Kaplan turbines, and the bulb housing contains the generator and runner assembly. Bulb turbines are fully axial designs, whereas standard Kaplan turbines have a radial wicket gate arrangement ([Wikipedia – Kaplan turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaplan_turbine)). They are particularly suited for tidal and run-of-river applications.

**Turbular Turbine.** Tubular turbines are axial-flow reaction turbines installed inside a cylindrical casing (draft tube), with the water passing through the tube in a straight line. They are designed for low-head applications, typically operating in the head range of 2–30 m, with flow rates of approximately 1–60 m³/s and conversion efficiencies reaching up to 90% or more ([micro-hydro-power.com – Tubular Turbine](https://www.micro-hydro-power.com/tubular-turbine-bulb-type.htm); [ScienceDirect – Tubular Turbines](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/tubular-turbines)). The blades may be fixed or manually adjustable. Compared with vertical-axis Kaplan units, tubular turbines offer higher efficiency, require less excavation for the powerhouse (reducing civil works investment by 10–20%), and reduce equipment investment by 5–10% ([micro-hydro-power.com – Tubular Turbine](https://www.micro-hydro-power.com/tubular-turbine-bulb-type.htm)). They can be installed on horizontal or inclined axes and are commonly used in small-scale hydroelectric systems, tidal power, and extremely low-head applications with large flow rates ([ScienceDirect – Tubular Turbines](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/tubular-turbines); [DLLP Power](https://dlldpower.com/tubular-turbine/)). The key distinction from bulb turbines is that tubular turbines route the water through a bent or S-shaped passage with the generator typically outside the flow stream (connected via a shaft), whereas bulb turbines place the generator inside the flow within a streamlined nacelle ([US DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines); [Wikipedia – Kaplan turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaplan_turbine)).

**Archimedean Screw Turbine.** The Archimedean screw turbine operates on the reverse principle of the ancient Archimedean screw pump: water enters at the top of the inclined helical screw, and the weight of the water pushes on the flights, causing the screw to rotate and drive a generator ([Renewables First – Archimedean Screw](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/archimedean-screw-hydro-turbine/)). It is classified as a reaction turbine and is particularly well-suited for very low head applications. The typical head range is 1–10 m (with single screws operating up to ~8 m and multiple screws used above that), and flow rates range from approximately 0.25 m³/s for the smallest 1-m-diameter screws up to ~14.5 m³/s for 5-m-diameter units ([Renewables First – Archimedean Screw](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/archimedean-screw-hydro-turbine/); [ScienceDirect – Archimedes screw](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148116302282)). Design-point efficiencies typically range from approximately 70–85%, with some smaller units reaching up to 90% under optimal conditions ([Firgelli Automation](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/mechanisms/archimedian-screw-water-lift); [H2O Team](https://h2oenergy.com.tr/en/archimedean-screw-turbines-i-2338)). The screw normally rotates at approximately 26 rpm, requiring a gearbox to increase speed to 750–1500 rpm for standard generators ([Renewables First – Archimedean Screw](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/archimedean-screw-hydro-turbine/)). Pressure regulation is achieved through variable-speed operation (adjusting rotational speed with flow rate) rather than internal mechanical devices; fixed-speed screws use automated sluice gates, which incur higher head losses. A significant advantage for urban water supply applications is the screw's high debris tolerance — large debris can pass through unharmed, eliminating the need for fine intake screens. The design life is approximately 30 years with major overhaul ([Renewables First – Archimedean Screw](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/archimedean-screw-hydro-turbine/); [MDPI – Sustainable Power Generation](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/23/15948)).

**Summary of Reaction Turbine Suitability for Urban Water Supply Networks.** Among the six reaction turbine types, the Archimedean screw and bulb/tubular designs are the most directly applicable to urban water supply networks due to their ability to operate at very low heads (1–30 m) typical of pressure-reducing valve (PRV) dissipation nodes. Francis and Kaplan turbines, while more efficient at their design points, generally require higher heads than are available in most urban WDN settings. The Archimedean screw's debris tolerance, fish-friendly operation, and simplified civil works make it particularly attractive for retrofit installations in existing water infrastructure, though its large physical footprint may constrain deployment in space-limited urban manholes.

### PAT and Special In-Pipe Turbines: Spherical, In-Line, H-Darrieus

**Pump as Turbine (PAT) — Derived Parameters in Turbine Mode**

PATs are reaction-type machines that operate by running a standard centrifugal pump in reverse. Their technical parameters in turbine mode are not independently specified but are derived from the pump's best efficiency point (BEP) in pump mode through well-established correlations. According to Nasir et al. (2024), radial flow pumps can function as hydraulic turbines within a head range of approximately 10–150 m and a discharge range of 0.003–0.5 m³/s, though the applicable head in urban water distribution networks is typically below 80 m given the limited pressure excess at dissipation nodes ([Nasir et al. 2024, *Heliyon* 10(4)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024021157)). The BEP efficiency in turbine mode is approximately equal to the pump-mode BEP efficiency, generally falling in the 65–75% range for radial-flow pumps suitable for PAT operation ([Carravetta et al. 2022, *Water* 14(10), 1526](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). A critical relationship is that turbine-mode BEP flow rate and head are always greater than the corresponding pump-mode BEP values for the same pump, a fact documented in KSB's technical guidance on turbine-mode operation ([KSB, Centrifugal Pump Lexicon](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/turbine-mode-1115998)). The specific speed (n_s) of the selected pump strongly influences PAT performance and the accuracy of BEP prediction methods, which include theoretical approaches (Stepanoff), CFD numerical simulation, non-dimensional analytical methodologies, and experimental testing per ISO 9906 ([Nasir et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024021157); [Zhao et al. 2025, *FDMP* 21(6), 1261–1298](https://www.techscience.com/fdmp/v21n6/62781/html)). Because PATs lack internal mechanical flow-regulation devices, their usable flow operating range is approximately 70–130% of BEP flow, beyond which efficiency declines sharply ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).

**Spherical Turbines — Lift-Based Cross-Flow In-Pipe Design**

Spherical turbines are a relatively new class of lift-based cross-flow turbine designed for direct insertion into pressurized water pipes. The concept was commercialized as the Lucid Spherical Turbine (LST), which modified the Gorlov helical turbine into a spherical configuration optimized for gravity-fed vertical water channels ([PMC8662452](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8662452/)). The spherical rotor consists of multiple curved blades arranged so that the rotor rotates transversely (perpendicular to the flow direction) within a cylindrical pipe, creating a compact axial-length design suited for confined in-pipe installations. A five-bladed spherical rotor was modeled by Rostamani et al. (2025), who noted that spherical cross-flow turbines with NACA0018 airfoil profiles achieve larger power coefficients than alternative designs ([Rostamani et al. 2025, *Energy Reports*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590123025024314)). The spherical helical turbine has demonstrated a maximum power coefficient of approximately 0.188 under tested conditions ([ResearchGate, 2021](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356834297_Design_and_Analysis_of_In-Pipe_Hydro-Turbine_for_an_Optimized_Nearly_Zero_Energy_Building)). A six-wing crossed-blade spherical turbine with airfoil cross-sections selected through model tests was also investigated, confirming the lift-based operating principle ([MDPI, *Energies* 12(20), 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/12/20/3998)).

**Airfoil Profile Discrepancy.** Sources disagree on the specific NACA airfoil profile used in spherical turbine blades, and this variation appears to reflect genuine design evolution rather than citation error:

| Source | Airfoil Profile |
|--------|----------------|
| Oladosu & Koya (2018) | NACA 0020 ([*Engineering Science and Technology*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2215098616312459)) |
| Rostamani et al. (2025) | NACA0018 ([*Energy Reports*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590123025024314)) |
| Original design specification (Lucid Spherical Turbine) | NACA0018 ([*JREE*](https://www.jree.ir/article_230955_80e406dd0a5aef7c88f7f717aca9d00c.pdf)) |
| Comparative study (Rostamani et al.) | NACA0015, NACA0018, and NACA4415 tested ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393869634_Towards_Efficient_Hydropower_Harvesting_Design_Modification_and_Performance_Optimization_of_a_Spherical_Lift-Based_In-Pipe_Turbine)) |

The shift from NACA0020 (a 20% thick symmetric airfoil) to NACA0018 (18% thick) likely reflects optimization for the low-Reynolds-number, high-blockage conditions encountered in small-diameter water pipes, where thinner profiles may reduce drag and improve lift-to-drag ratio. Researchers have tested multiple profiles including cambered sections (NACA4415), but symmetric airfoils remain the standard for spherical cross-flow turbines because they perform equally well under reversed flow directions inherent to cross-flow operation. The discrepancy should be noted as an active design variable rather than a factual error.

Spherical turbines provide no internal pressure regulation; their fixed-geometry cross-flow design means flow regulation must be achieved through external valves or bypass arrangements. Their compact spherical shape is the primary advantage, minimizing axial length within the pipe and enabling installation in existing manholes or PRV vaults with minimal civil works — a key consideration for urban water distribution networks where space is constrained ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).

**In-Line Turbines (ILT) — Direct Pipe Insertion**

In-Line Turbines (ILT) are purpose-built reaction turbines designed for direct insertion into pressurized conduits, sharing the pipe as the pressure boundary. The most documented commercial ILT series is manufactured by Canyon Hydro (USA), with specifications as follows:

| Parameter | Canyon Hydro ILT Series |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Pipe sizes | 6–24 inches (150–600 mm) |
| Net head range | 50–570 feet (15–174 m) |
| Flow rate range | 500–30,000 GPM (0.032–1.89 m³/s) |
| Regulation | Wicket gates (guide vanes) |
| Installation | Direct pipe insertion, conduit power recovery |

Source: [Canyon Hydro ILT product page](https://www.canyonhydro.com/products/in-line-hydro-turbine.html); [Soar Hydro equipment page](https://soarhydro.com/equipment/).

The ILT series uses wicket gates (guide vanes) to maintain high efficiency across the full flow range — a significant operational advantage over PAT and spherical turbine designs. Detailed specification tables for individual models (Canyon 751, 9513, 1220, 1525, 2435) show power outputs ranging from approximately 0.3 HP at 20 ft head to over 248 HP at 350 ft head, with flow rates from 0.2 to 3.3 CFS depending on model and head ([Canyon Hydro turbine specifications](https://www.canyonhydro.com/micro/microspecs.html)). The turbines are site-specific for maximum efficiency but share common parts for competitive pricing and quick lead times, and are manufactured and assembled in the USA under US Patent No. 10,584,673.

Canyon Hydro also offers pump-turbine packages (6–24" pipe, 50–500 ft head, 500–9,000 GPM) for less demanding applications where consistent flow rates exist ([Soar Hydro](https://soarhydro.com/equipment/)). At the pico scale, the M300 Pico Hydro Turbine generates approximately 300 W at 24 V DC from pipes of 2 inches or larger, designed for powering RTUs and SCADA systems in remote water network locations ([Soar Hydro](https://soarhydro.com/equipment/)).

**H-Darrieus Vertical-Axis In-Pipe Turbines**

H-Darrieus turbines are vertical-axis, lift-based hydrokinetic turbines adapted for in-pipe pico-hydro applications. A comprehensive CFD optimization study was conducted by Sartore (University of Padova, 2024/2025), who investigated a three-blade H-Darrieus rotor using Large Eddy Simulation (LES) within Ansys. The key design parameters studied were the rotor blockage ratio (B), rotor solidity (σ), and tip speed ratio (λ). The salient finding was a fundamental trade-off between maximum power extraction and maximum hydraulic efficiency:

- **Maximum power configuration**: Power coefficient C_P,max = 4.23 at high blockage and high solidity, yielding 3.95 kW, but inducing a severe head loss of 6.97 m and a low hydraulic efficiency of 34.88%.
- **Maximum efficiency configuration**: Hydraulic efficiency η_Hy,max = 54.94% at moderate blockage and lower solidity, producing 1.86 kW with a minimal head loss of 2.39 m.

Source: [Sartore, A., *CFD optimization of an in-pipe H-Darrieus pico water turbine*, University of Padova thesis, 2024/2025](https://thesis.unipd.it/retrieve/d9eb8f8c-b602-4f0f-8ad7-fe55a38756c2/Sartore_Andrea.pdf).

This trade-off is critical for urban water network applications: the high-power configuration imposes excessive head loss that could compromise downstream pressure requirements, while the efficient configuration sacrifices power output. The hydraulic efficiency values (34.9–54.9%) are substantially lower than those of conventional micro-hydroturbines or PATs, reflecting the inherent challenges of extracting energy from confined pipe flows with high blockage. H-Darrieus turbines have no internal pressure regulation mechanism and rely on fixed-geometry blade arrangements; their vertical-axis configuration allows omnidirectional flow acceptance, which is advantageous in pipes with variable flow directions or transient conditions. These turbines are best suited for very small-scale energy harvesting (pico-hydro, <10 kW) to power monitoring equipment rather than grid-connected generation.

**Pressure Regulation and Installation Case Studies**

None of the special in-pipe turbines discussed here — PAT, spherical, or H-Darrieus — possess internal mechanical pressure regulation devices. This is a shared limitation that distinguishes them from conventional turbines such as Francis (wicket gates) or Kaplan (adjustable blades + wicket gates). Pressure regulation for these in-pipe designs must be achieved through external means:

- **PAT**: Hydraulic regulation (HR) with series-parallel control valves or electrical regulation (ER) with variable speed drives, as detailed in [Carravetta et al. 2013, *Energies* 6(1), 411](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/6/1/411). The Variable Operating Strategy (VOS) enables PAT operation under variable flow/head conditions with plant efficiency up to 50% ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).
- **Spherical turbine**: Fixed-geometry cross-flow design with no regulation; pressure management requires external bypass or throttling valves. The compact spherical form factor enables installation in existing PRV vaults with minimal civil works, but coordination with upstream/downstream pressure control is essential ([AIP Conference Proceedings 2386, 2022](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AIPC.2386d0042J/abstract)).
- **ILT (Canyon Hydro)**: Equipped with wicket gates providing internal flow regulation — the only in-pipe design among those reviewed with this capability — enabling efficient operation across a wide flow range without external valves ([Canyon Hydro ILT](https://www.canyonhydro.com/products/in-line-hydro-turbine.html)).
- **H-Darrieus**: Fixed-geometry vertical-axis design with no regulation; the trade-off between power extraction and head loss must be managed through careful site selection and system-level pressure management ([Sartore 2024/2025](https://thesis.unipd.it/retrieve/d9eb8f8c-b602-4f0f-8ad7-fe55a38756c2/Sartore_Andrea.pdf)).

The PRS turbine data-driven digital twin approach, developed by Sinagra et al. (2023), represents an advanced strategy for pressure regulation management at turbine installation nodes in real water networks, using real-time data to optimize the balance between energy recovery and pressure safety ([Sinagra et al. 2023](https://kh.aquaenergyexpo.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Energy-Recovery-Optimization-by-Means-of-a-Turbine-in-a-Pressure-Regulation-Node-of-a-Real-Water-Network-Through-a-Data%E2%80%91Driven-Digital-Twin.pdf)). This approach is applicable to all in-pipe turbine types and is particularly valuable for PAT and spherical turbine installations where no internal regulation exists.

**Notes on classification and sources:**

- **Impulse vs. Reaction**: Pelton, Turgo, and Crossflow are impulse turbines — water pressure remains constant across the runner and energy transfer occurs via kinetic energy of a free jet ([DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/types-hydropower-turbines); [Wikipedia/Cross-flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-flow_turbine)). Francis, Kaplan, Semi-Kaplan, Bulb, Tubular, and Archimedean screw are reaction turbines — water changes pressure as it passes through the runner. PATs are reaction-type reversed pumps. The Spherical turbine uses lift-based airfoil blades (NACA profiles), classifying it as reaction despite its cross-flow geometry.

- **Head/flow ranges**: Values span from large hydroelectric installations to micro-hydro units. For urban water supply applications, the relevant ranges are typically at the low end: heads of 2–80 m and flow rates of 0.001–10 m³/s. Discrepancies across sources (e.g., Kaplan head range cited as 2–40 m by [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/kaplan-turbines) vs. 2–70 m by [Renewables First](https://renewablesfirst.co.uk/renewable-energy-technologies/hydropower/hydropower-learning-centre/kaplan-turbines/)) reflect differences between large-plant and small-unit designs.

- **Efficiency ranges**: Conventional turbines (Pelton, Francis, Kaplan) achieve 85–93% at BEP. PATs are limited to 65–75% at BEP due to operating pumps in reverse mode away from their design point ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). Crossflow turbines have lower peak efficiency (~80%) but maintain it across a wider flow range ([Quaranta et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0029801822009489)).

- **Pressure regulation**: Conventional turbines possess mature internal regulation — wicket gates (Francis), adjustable blades + wicket gates (Kaplan), and needle nozzles + deflectors (Pelton, Turgo). PATs and Spherical turbines lack any internal mechanical regulation and require external systems (HR, ER, or variable-speed drives), adding system complexity and cost.

**Suitability ranking for urban water supply networks:**

For urban water distribution networks, where available heads are typically low (2–80 m), flows are variable, and space is constrained, the most suitable turbine types are:

1. **PAT** — Best suited due to low cost, compact size, standardized supply chain, and ability to operate at heads typical of WDN pressure regulation nodes (7–50 m). Limited by lack of internal regulation and lower BEP efficiency ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).
2. **Crossflow (Banki)** — Well-suited for low-to-medium head (2–80 m) with flat efficiency curve under variable flow, making it robust against diurnal flow variations in WDNs ([Quaranta et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0029801822009489)).
3. **Kaplan / Semi-Kaplan** — Suitable for low-head sites with higher flow rates; dual regulation provides excellent operating range, but higher cost and civil works requirements limit urban deployment.
4. **Bulb / Tubular** — Designed for very low head (1–25 m) and in-pipe installation; useful for large-diameter transmission mains but limited regulation capability.
5. **Archimedean screw** — Suitable for very low head (1–10 m) with simple, robust construction; often used in water treatment plant outfalls rather than pressurized distribution networks.
6. **Spherical** — Emerging technology with compact in-pipe design; promising for retrofitting into existing manholes but limited performance data and no internal regulation.
7. **Pelton / Turgo** — Generally unsuitable for urban WDNs due to high head requirements (>15–50 m) rarely available at individual dissipation nodes, though applicable at reservoir inlets or steep-gradient transmission lines where heads exceed 50 m.
8. **Francis** — Typically requires heads of 40–600 m, making it unsuitable for most urban WDN dissipation nodes where recoverable power is generally ≤20 kW ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).

## Economic and Technical Challenges in Urban Water Supply Networks

### Economic Challenges

The deployment of energy recovery equipment in urban water supply networks faces a constellation of systemic and institutional economic barriers that extend well beyond the direct technology-to-technology cost differences examined in S1.2. These challenges collectively constrain investment, limit scalability, and in many cases render otherwise technically feasible projects financially unviable.

**1. High Upfront Capital Costs and the Civil Works Burden**

The single most frequently cited economic barrier to installing energy recovery equipment in water distribution networks (WDNs) is the cost of civil works required to house the equipment within existing infrastructure. Carravetta et al. (2022) identify civil works as "a great barrier to the diffusion of a small hydro power plant in water distribution," noting that existing pressure reducing valves (PRVs) are typically installed in small manholes beneath street pavement, and that enlarging these manholes or adding waterproof closures to protect electrical components represents "a further unmanageable cost" ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). The Energy Booster (EB) concept was specifically developed to address this bottleneck by encapsulating PATs in a water booster that can be placed under an existing manhole with minimal excavation, thereby "drastically cutting down the cost of additional civil works" ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). For conventional micro-hydroturbines, installation costs in the range of $1,500–$4,000/kW have been reported ([NCAT](https://www.ncat.org/publication/micro-hydro-power-a-beginners-guide-to-design-and-installation/)), and these figures can escalate significantly when site-specific civil works—such as powerhouse construction, penstock reinforcement, and tailrace modifications—are required in confined urban environments.

**2. Low Recoverable Power at Individual Dissipation Nodes**

The hydropower potential at individual PRV dissipation nodes in urban WDNs is inherently limited. Carravetta et al. (2022) state that "the hydropower potential at the dissipation nodes does not generally exceed 20 kW" and that in peripheral network branches, "the recoverable energy can be significantly low, thus the problem of the plant cost becomes compelling" ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). This low per-node power output means that even modest absolute equipment costs translate into high cost-per-kW figures, undermining project economics. Lydon et al. (2017) demonstrated that up to 40% of the gross power potential of an existing PRV can be converted to electrical energy using a PAT while simultaneously controlling pressure ([Lydon et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148117307498)), but even this conversion efficiency applied to a small absolute power base yields limited revenue. The Hamlehdar et al. (2022) Tehran case study found a total network-wide potential of 168.21 kW across all PRVs—sufficient to provide on average 42.3% of the power consumed by the network's pumps—but this aggregate figure is distributed across many individual nodes, each contributing only a few kilowatts ([Hamlehdar et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544222009276)). International case studies corroborate this pattern: a US assessment identified 500 kW across six PRVs (~83 kW average per site), a Brazilian study found 2.6–40 kW per PRV, an Italian network yielded 9.5 kW from PRV substitution, and a Dublin study identified approximately 100 kW from PRVs, pressure reduction tanks, and reservoirs combined ([Hamlehdar et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544222009276)).

**3. Payback Period Variability and Investment Deterrence**

Reported payback periods for PAT installations in WDNs range from as little as 2 years to over a decade, depending on site conditions, energy prices, and regulatory context. Yang et al. (2022) report that for the same capacity, the investment payback period of PAT can be shortened to 2 years, compared to as long as 15 years for conventional hydraulic turbines ([Yang et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.1003449/full)). A European case study documented by the NetZeroCities platform reported annual cost savings of €36,600, a total system cost of €373,000, and a resulting payback period of 9 years ([NetZeroCities](https://netzerocities.app/resource-778)). The wide variation in payback periods is driven by differences in local electricity prices, the magnitude of available head and flow, the cost of civil works at each site, and the availability of subsidies or tax incentives. The US federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC) of 30% for qualifying hydropower investments, extended through 2032 under the Inflation Reduction Act, can substantially improve project economics for US-based installations ([EPA IRA Summary](https://www.epa.gov/green-power-markets/summary-inflation-reduction-act-provisions-related-renewable-energy)), though many water utilities may not have the tax liability to fully utilize this credit.

**4. Energy Price Uncertainty and Revenue Projection Risk**

Revenue from energy recovery installations depends on the price of electricity, which is subject to market volatility, regulatory changes, and the evolving energy mix. In regions with low grid electricity prices—as is common in countries with abundant hydropower, nuclear, or subsidized fossil fuels—the revenue generated from micro-hydropower in WDNs may be insufficient to cover even modest operational costs. Conversely, in regions with high electricity prices, the same installation may achieve payback within a few years. This uncertainty complicates investment decision-making for water utilities, which typically operate under long-term budgetary planning horizons and are reluctant to commit capital to projects with variable revenue streams.

**5. Regulatory and Administrative Barriers**

Installing energy recovery equipment in urban water networks involves navigating a complex web of permits and regulatory requirements. These include water rights and abstraction permits (even though the water is not consumed, its pressure is altered), electrical grid connection approvals, environmental impact assessments, and compliance with water utility procurement processes. In many jurisdictions, energy is not regulated within water and wastewater utilities, creating conflicts between water regulations and energy regulations that can delay or block projects ([University of Queensland regulatory barriers analysis](https://espace.library.uq.edu.au/view/UQ:55a99f5/UQ55a99f5.pdf)). Grid connection for small-scale distributed generation (typically ≤20 kW per node) may require compliance with IEEE 1547-2018 interconnection standards, anti-islanding protection, and power quality requirements (THD <5%), adding further cost and administrative burden. Water utility procurement processes, which are designed for water infrastructure rather than energy generation equipment, may lack frameworks for evaluating and approving energy recovery projects, leading to protracted internal approval cycles.

**6. Misalignment of Incentives Between Water and Energy Departments**

A fundamental institutional barrier is the misalignment of incentives within municipal governance structures. In many cities, the water department bears the cost of installing and maintaining energy recovery equipment, while the revenue from electricity generation accrues to the energy department or the general municipal budget. This cross-departmental disconnect eliminates the direct financial incentive for water utilities to invest in energy recovery, since they bear the capital and operational costs without receiving the energy revenue. Energy Service Company (ESCO) and Public-Private Partnership (PPP) business models have been proposed to overcome this misalignment. Under an ESCO arrangement, a private energy service company finances, installs, and operates the energy recovery equipment and is paid a share of the actual energy savings or revenue generated, effectively internalizing the benefit within the project structure ([UNEP CCC ESCO market analysis](https://c2e2.unepccc.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/12/government-actions-to-foster-the-development-of-esco-markets.pdf)). PPP models can similarly mobilize private capital and expertise while sharing risks between public and private actors ([ResearchGate PPP for energy efficiency](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319915103_Public-Private_Partnerships_for_Energy_Efficiency_Projects_A_Win-Win_Model_to_Choose_the_Energy_Performance_Contracting_structure)). However, these models introduce their own complexity, including contract negotiation, performance verification, and long-term liability allocation, which can be particularly challenging for small-scale, distributed projects in WDNs.

**7. Competition with Cheap Grid Electricity**

In many urban contexts, the baseline grid electricity price is low enough that the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) from micro-hydropower installations in WDNs is not competitive. IRENA's cost analysis indicates that small hydropower projects typically have an LCOE of $0.02–$0.10/kWh in developing countries, while pico-hydro systems can reach $0.27/kWh or more ([IRENA 2012](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2012/RE_Technologies_Cost_Analysis-HYDROPOWER.pdf)). For PAT installations in WDNs, where per-node power is low (≤20 kW) and civil works costs are high, the effective LCOE may exceed local grid prices, particularly in regions with subsidized electricity or abundant generation capacity. The Bideris-Davos & Vovos (2024) comprehensive review of energy recovery technologies in WDSs concludes that economic viability remains a critical constraint, noting that while PATs offer lower capital costs than conventional turbines, the combination of low energy output per node, high civil works costs, and competition with inexpensive grid power often renders marginal projects uneconomic without supplementary policy support ([Bideris-Davos & Vovos 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/16/15/2129)). Fecarotta et al. conducted a techno-economic assessment of PAT replacement of pressure reduction stations (PRSs) using EPANET modeling across six scenarios, demonstrating that installation of micro-hydropower systems can greatly diminish leakage and produce a positive net present value (NPV), but the economic outcome is highly sensitive to the number of PATs installed, the hydraulic conditions, and the valuation of both energy and water savings ([Hamlehdar et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544222009276)). Fecarotta & McNabola further developed optimization models with linear and non-linear constraints to reduce wasted water and energy in PRVs, providing a framework for identifying the most economically viable sites within a network ([Hamlehdar et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544222009276)).

**8. Synthesis of Economic Barriers**

The economic challenges constraining large-scale deployment of energy recovery equipment in urban WDNs are mutually reinforcing. Low per-node power output (≤20 kW) inflates cost-per-kW; civil works costs—exacerbated by space constraints in existing manholes—can represent a disproportionate fraction of total project cost; long and variable payback periods (2–15 years) deter risk-averse water utilities; and institutional incentive misalignment removes the internal financial motivation for investment. While PATs offer a procurement cost advantage of approximately 50% or less compared to conventional turbines ([NCAT](https://www.ncat.org/publication/micro-hydro-power-a-beginners-guide-to-design-and-installation/)), this advantage is often insufficient to overcome the systemic barriers of civil works costs, regulatory complexity, and competition with cheap grid electricity. The most promising pathways to improved economic viability include: (a) the Energy Booster concept and similar installation innovations that reduce civil works costs ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)); (b) ESCO and PPP business models that realign incentives and mobilize private capital; (c) policy support mechanisms such as the US federal ITC and equivalent incentives in other jurisdictions; and (d) optimization models that identify the most economically favorable sites within a network to maximize NPV across a portfolio of installations rather than evaluating sites individually.

### Technical Challenges

The installation of energy recovery equipment—whether conventional micro-hydroturbines or PATs—in urban water distribution networks (WDNs) confronts a set of interrelated technical challenges that stem from the inherent variability of water demand, the physics of turbomachinery, and the constraints of existing infrastructure. These challenges can be grouped into three sub-categories: operational matching, equipment safety, and system integration.

#### Operational Matching

1. **Highly variable flow rates and head drops.** Urban WDNs exhibit pronounced diurnal and seasonal fluctuations in both flow rate and available head, driven by consumer demand patterns. Carravetta et al. (2013) illustrated that pressure head and flow rate at a given node can vary widely over a 24-hour cycle, with upstream pressure head (*PH*) and backpressure (*BP*) both fluctuating significantly ([Carravetta et al. 2013](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/6/1/411)). This variability forces energy recovery devices to operate far from their Best Efficiency Point (BEP) for extended periods, causing substantial efficiency losses. The Hamlehdar et al. (2022) Tehran case study confirmed that flow and pressure fluctuations directly affect generated power output, with the EPANET model showing recoverable potential of 168.21 kW across the network but with highly variable power at individual nodes ([Hamlehdar et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544222009276)).

2. **PAT's lack of internal regulation and limited operating flow range.** Unlike conventional turbines (Francis with wicket gates, Kaplan with adjustable blades), PATs have no internal mechanical regulation devices. Flow regulation must be achieved entirely through external valves—either hydraulic regulation (HR) via series-parallel control valves or electrical regulation (ER) via variable speed drives ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526); [Yang et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.1003449/full)). PAT efficiency drops sharply when operating away from BEP, with a usable flow operating range of approximately 70–130% of BEP flow. Outside this narrow band, efficiency declines precipitously, limiting the PAT's ability to track variable WDN conditions. The Manservigi et al. (2025) study found that PAT operation is recommended at or near BEP to avoid excessive efficiency reduction, and that off-design operation may severely limit recovered energy ([Manservigi et al. 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-prisms-water/article/hydraulic-and-electrical-regulations-of-pumps-as-turbines-for-energy-recovery-in-water-distribution-networks-energy-and-economic-analysis/11A8E6728AE92434D1A085B84378FC64)).

3. **Pressure regulation vs. energy recovery trade-off.** A fundamental tension exists between maintaining the downstream service pressure required by water consumers and maximizing energy extraction. A PAT installed in place of a PRV must ensure a minimum downstream pressure (*P_set*) while extracting as much head as possible. The Viana et al. (2023) study demonstrated that PATs can mimic pressure control when operating near BEP, but under variable conditions, pressure regulation becomes problematic—one PAT in their study exhibited negative outlet pressure, adversely affecting downstream DMA pressure ([Viana et al. 2023](https://www.rbciamb.com.br/Publicacoes_RBCIAMB/article/view/1551)). The Carravetta et al. (2013) Variable Operating Strategy (VOS) addresses this by selecting PAT geometry based on the flow-head distribution pattern and network back-pressure, achieving plant efficiencies up to 50% under variable conditions ([Carravetta et al. 2013](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/6/1/411)). More recently, the PRS turbine with a data-driven digital twin approach (ARTC—Advanced Real Time Control) has been proposed, enabling real-time optimization of turbine and valve configurations to balance hydropower generation with pressure regulation, showing improved robustness in modified operational scenarios ([Sinagra et al. 2023](https://kh.aquaenergyexpo.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Energy-Recovery-Optimization-by-Means-of-a-Turbine-in-a-Pressure-Regulation-Node-of-a-Real-Water-Network-Through-a-Data%E2%80%91Driven-Digital-Twin.pdf)).

4. **Mitigation strategies for partial-load efficiency decline.** Several strategies have been developed to address PAT's narrow operating range. The VOS method selects PAT geometry for a given flow-head distribution pattern ([Carravetta et al. 2013](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/6/1/411)). Electrical regulation (ER) with variable-speed drives allows the PAT rotational speed to be adjusted so the machine operates closer to BEP under varying conditions, though this adds cost and complexity ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). Multi-pump parallel configurations using ON/OFF valves offer a simplified hydraulic regulation alternative, though with coarser control resolution. The Energy Booster (EB) concept simplifies installation while maintaining regulation capability by encapsulating two submersible PATs in a water booster placed under existing manholes ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)).

#### Equipment Safety

5. **Water quality issues: sediment, corrosion, and cavitation.** Water supply systems present several water quality challenges for energy recovery equipment. Sediment in the water can cause abrasive wear on impeller blades and runner surfaces, progressively degrading hydraulic efficiency. Chlorine and other chemical treatments used in water supply can accelerate corrosion of metallic components. Critically, PATs operating in reverse mode are more prone to cavitation than in pump mode, as the flow patterns and pressure distributions differ significantly between the two modes. Kan et al. (2022) found that PAT reverse mode is more susceptible to cavitation, though the damage mechanisms differ from conventional pump-mode cavitation ([Kan et al. 2022, Renewable Energy, 168, 12786](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/rensus/v168y2022ics1364032122006700.html)). Cavitation risk is exacerbated under variable operating conditions, where instantaneous pressure drops at the impeller inlet can fall below the vapor pressure threshold.

6. **Water hammer and transient pressure risks.** The installation of energy recovery equipment in pressurized pipe networks introduces water hammer risks during transient events such as pump trips, valve closures, or turbine load rejections. Pressure surges can damage both the energy recovery equipment and the surrounding pipe infrastructure. Protection measures—including surge tanks, air valves, and penstock anchoring per ANSI/ASCE standards—must be incorporated into the system design, adding cost and complexity. These transient risks are particularly acute in WDNs with long pipe runs and significant elevation changes ([Hamlehdar et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544222009276)).

7. **Equipment failure and water supply safety.** A paramount concern is ensuring that failure of the energy recovery equipment does not compromise water supply safety. If a PAT or turbine seizes, leaks, or malfunctions, it must not block water flow or cause dangerous pressure excursions. This requires fail-safe valve arrangements, bypass piping, and pressure relief systems that can isolate the equipment and revert to conventional PRV operation. The Viana et al. (2023) study highlighted that PAT2 in their case study exhibited negative outlet pressure under certain conditions, demonstrating that improper PAT selection can directly threaten downstream pressure adequacy ([Viana et al. 2023](https://www.rbciamb.com.br/Publicacoes_RBCIAMB/article/view/1551)). The PRS turbine digital twin approach addresses this by continuously calibrating the hydraulic model against real-time measurements and providing robust pressure regulation even under equipment degradation scenarios ([Sinagra et al. 2023](https://kh.aquaenergyexpo.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Energy-Recovery-Optimization-by-Means-of-a-Turbine-in-a-Pressure-Regulation-Node-of-a-Real-Water-Network-Through-a-Data%E2%80%91Driven-Digital-Twin.pdf)).

#### System Integration

8. **Space constraints in urban environments.** Existing PRVs in urban WDNs are typically installed in small manholes located under street pavement. Retrofitting these sites with energy recovery equipment—whether PATs or conventional turbines—requires additional space for the electro-mechanical equipment, control valves, and electrical components. The need to enlarge manholes, install waterproof closures to protect electrical components, and ensure accessibility for maintenance represents a major cost and logistical barrier. Carravetta et al. (2022) identified civil works as a primary barrier to the diffusion of small hydropower in WDNs, noting that the existing PRVs are "generally installed in small manholes" and that increasing manhole size represents "a further unmanageable cost" ([Carravetta et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/10/1526)). The Energy Booster concept directly addresses this by encapsulating PATs in a compact booster unit that fits under existing manholes with minimal modification.

9. **Electrical grid connection and power quality.** Connecting small-scale generation (typically ≤20 kW per dissipation node) to the electrical grid presents several challenges. Grid interconnection must comply with standards such as IEEE 1547-2018, which establishes requirements for distributed energy resources including anti-islanding protection, voltage and frequency ride-through, and total harmonic distortion (THD) limits ([IEEE 1547-2018](https://standards.ieee.org/standard/1547-2018.html)). For very small installations, the cost of grid connection equipment—including inverters, protection relays, and metering—can represent a disproportionate fraction of total project cost. Power quality must be maintained to avoid injecting harmonics or causing voltage fluctuations into the local distribution network. In remote or rural WDN locations, grid connection may be entirely unavailable, necessitating standalone operation with battery storage or direct load supply.

10. **SCADA and control system integration.** Energy recovery equipment must be integrated with existing SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) systems used by water utilities to monitor and control network operations. This integration faces challenges including communication protocol compatibility, real-time data acquisition from flow and pressure sensors, and coordination with existing network control algorithms. The Sinagra et al. (2023) digital twin approach demonstrates how real-time sensor data can be fed into a continuously calibrated hydraulic model to optimize both pressure regulation and energy production, offering a pathway toward autonomous, unmanned operation of energy recovery plants ([Sinagra et al. 2023](https://kh.aquaenergyexpo.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Energy-Recovery-Optimization-by-Means-of-a-Turbine-in-a-Pressure-Regulation-Node-of-a-Real-Water-Network-Through-a-Data%E2%80%91Driven-Digital-Twin.pdf)). The Fecarotta and McNabola optimization model provides a mixed integer non-linear programming framework for optimal PAT and PRV placement within WDNs, incorporating linear and non-linear constraints to simultaneously reduce wasted water and energy at PRV sites ([Hamlehdar et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544222009276)).

11. **PRV-PAT coordination control.** When PATs are installed in parallel with existing PRVs (bypass mode) rather than as direct replacements, sophisticated coordination control is required to manage the division of flow between the PAT and the PRV, ensure pressure stability under all demand conditions, and provide fail-safe reversion to PRV-only operation during PAT maintenance or malfunction. The Viana et al. (2023) study investigated coordinated PRV-PAT pressure control under low-demand conditions and found that effective pressure management requires careful selection of PAT operating characteristics and, in some cases, variable speed operation ([Viana et al. 2023](https://www.rbciamb.com.br/Publicacoes_RBCIAMB/article/view/1551)).

#### Summary

The most critical technical bottlenecks currently constraining large-scale deployment of energy recovery equipment in urban WDNs are: (1) the combination of highly variable flow/head conditions with PAT's narrow operating range and lack of internal regulation, which together cause significant off-design efficiency losses; (2) the pressure regulation versus energy recovery trade-off, which limits the extractable energy at any given node while requiring complex external regulation systems; and (3) space constraints and civil works requirements in existing urban infrastructure, which disproportionately inflate project costs at the small power levels (generally ≤20 kW) typical of individual dissipation nodes. The Bideris-Davos and Vovos (2024) comprehensive review confirms that these technical challenges—variable operating conditions, machine selection difficulty, and absence of hydraulic regulation in PATs—remain the principal barriers to wider adoption ([Bideris-Davos & Vovos 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/16/15/2129)). Similarly, the Giudicianni et al. (2024) holistic review identifies common issues including limited awareness among utility managers, safety and reliability concerns, and the need for scalable, standardized solutions ([Giudicianni et al. 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1573062X.2023.2212271)). Emerging solutions—including the Energy Booster concept, PRS turbine with digital twin control, and advanced optimization models—show promise but have yet to achieve widespread commercial deployment.
