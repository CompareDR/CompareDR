# Energy Recovery in Urban Water Supply Systems: Techno-Economic Analysis of Micro-Hydroturbines and Pumps as Turbines (PATs)

Urban water supply networks (UWSNs) and water distribution networks (WDNs) are energy-intensive municipal infrastructures where potable water is transported across variable topography under high hydrostatic pressure[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). To protect pipe infrastructure from cyclic fatigue and prevent excessive background leakage, water utilities traditionally manage network pressures using Pressure Reducing Valves (PRVs)[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). While PRVs successfully reduce excessive pressure by dissipating hydraulic energy through localized head loss, this energy is lost as heat and turbulence[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).

In-conduit energy recovery technologies offer a sustainable alternative by converting surplus hydraulic head into electrical energy while maintaining necessary network pressure limits[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). Selecting the optimal conversion technology requires balancing hydraulic efficiency, flow variability, installation constraints, and life-cycle economics[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321).

## Detailed Comparative Analysis: Conventional Micro-Hydroturbines vs. Pump as Turbine (PAT) Systems

In-conduit energy recovery in municipal water networks primarily relies on two mechanical configurations: custom-engineered conventional micro-hydroturbines and commercial centrifugal pumps operated in reverse mode as turbines (Pumps as Turbines, or PATs)[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).

### Operational Efficiency and Characteristic Curves

Conventional micro-hydroturbines (such as miniaturized Francis, Kaplan, Pelton, or Turgo designs) feature hydraulic profiles designed specifically for turbine-mode extraction[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). At their Best Efficiency Point (BEP), high-grade micro-turbines achieve hydraulic efficiencies between $80\%$ and $92\%$[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). A primary advantage of conventional turbines is their part-load and off-design efficiency[[8]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291387762_Development_of_the_Turgo_Impulse_turbine_Past_and_present). Equipped with dynamic flow regulation mechanisms—such as variable wicket gates in Francis units, adjustable runner blades in Kaplan units, or needle spear nozzles in impulse turbines—conventional machines maintain flat efficiency curves across wide operating envelopes[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). Even when network flow decreases to $40\%\text{--}50\%$ of the nominal design discharge, conventional reaction and impulse turbines sustain operating efficiencies between $70\%$ and $85\%$ of their peak value[[10]](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/586584).

In contrast, PAT technology operates standard centrifugal pumps in reverse, directing high-pressure water into the pump discharge nozzle and discharging it through the suction eye[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). In reverse mode, peak efficiencies at the BEP typically range between $60\%$ and $85\%$, depending on runner geometry, size, and specific speed ($N_s$)[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). However, because commercial pumps feature fixed internal casing geometry without adjustable guide vanes or variable runner pitch, their performance is sensitive to off-design conditions[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321).

When flow deviates from the BEP, fixed blade angles induce hydraulic shock losses, boundary layer separation, and recirculating vortex formations within the impeller channels[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). This results in an immediate efficiency drop of $3\%$ to $10\%$ for moderate flow variations, with efficiency declining steeply when discharge drops below $60\%$ of $Q_{\text{BEP}}$[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). Additionally, in turbine mode, the pump impeller requires higher head and flow to reach peak efficiency than it does during standard pumping mode at identical rotational speeds ($H_{\text{turb}} > H_{\text{pump}}$ and $Q_{\text{turb}} > Q_{\text{pump}}$)[[10]](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/586584).

### Capital, Installation, and Life-Cycle Economics

Capital expenditure (Capex) and operating expenditure (Opex) profiles differ significantly between purpose-built turbines and PATs:

- **Procurement and Electromechanical Capex**: Conventional micro-turbines are low-volume, engineered-to-order units with custom hydraulic profiles and dedicated governors[[6]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606). Consequently, their procurement costs range from $1,500\text{ \euro{}}/\text{kW}$ to $2,500\text{ \euro{}}/\text{kW}$ for sub-$50\text{ kW}$ schemes[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). In contrast, PATs leverage globally mass-manufactured pump components, reducing bare-shaft equipment costs to $200\text{--}400\text{ \euro{}}/\text{kW}$[[6]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606). Complete PAT skids (including standard induction generators, basic power electronics, and control valves) typically cost $350\text{--}800\text{ \euro{}}/\text{kW}$, delivering upfront electromechanical equipment savings of $50\%$ to $70\%$ compared to custom micro-turbines[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).
- **Civil Works and Installation**: Conventional micro-turbines often require specialized anchor blocks, enlarged underground vaults, and custom piping configurations[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). PATs match standard pipeline pipe flanges and pump footprints, reducing civil retrofit and mechanical fit-out costs by $15\%$ to $30\%$[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).
- **Operation, Maintenance, and Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE)**: The Levelized Cost of Electricity for PAT installations in water networks typically ranges from $0.025\text{ \euro{}}/\text{kWh}$ to $0.065\text{ \$/kWh}$, compared to $0.09\text{--}0.13\text{ \$/kWh}$ for conventional micro-turbines[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). Annual maintenance for PATs is roughly $3\%\text{--}5\%$ of Capex because replacement parts (such as standard mechanical seals and ball bearings) are readily available from local industrial suppliers[[6]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606). Conventional turbines require specialized hydro technicians and bespoke components that increase long-term maintenance costs[[7]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331699316_Cost_Model_for_Pumps_as_Turbines_in_Run-of-River_and_In-Pipe_Microhydropower_Applications). Consequently, PAT systems typically achieve simple economic payback within $1.5$ to $3.5$ years, compared to $5.5$ to $8$ years for purpose-built turbines[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).

### Pressure Regulation and Network Control Capability

A primary design constraint for energy recovery devices in municipal networks is ensuring that downstream pressure remains stable under variable consumer demand[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).

Conventional turbines with adjustable wicket gates or needle valves provide continuous, dynamic pressure regulation[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). By altering flow passage geometry, these units adjust head dissipation to track downstream pressure setpoints across varying flow rates without relying on auxiliary throttling valves[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321).

Because standard PATs have fixed internal geometry, their head-drop profile follows an unalterable quadratic relationship with flow ($\Delta H \propto Q^2$)[[12]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267400077_Pumps_yielding_power). As a result, PATs cannot modulate pressure independently and require external regulation strategies[[15]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259296613_PAT_Design_Strategy_for_Energy_Recovery_in_Water_Distribution_Networks_by_Electrical_Regulation):

- *Hydraulic Regulation (HR)* uses a bypass layout with an automated PRV in parallel to divert excess flow during high-demand periods, paired with an automated throttle valve in series to dissipate excess head during low-demand periods[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).
- *Electrical Regulation (ER)* pairs the PAT generator with a Variable Frequency Drive (VFD) and regenerative inverter. Adjusting the rotational speed ($N$) shifts the turbine's characteristic head-discharge curve according to affinity laws, providing pressure control across fluctuating demand without mechanical throttling[[15]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259296613_PAT_Design_Strategy_for_Energy_Recovery_in_Water_Distribution_Networks_by_Electrical_Regulation).

In closed, pressurized urban networks, units must also operate under positive downstream backpressure[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). Reaction machines (Francis turbines, PATs, and Kaplan units) operate naturally within sealed pipe flows[[7]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331699316_Cost_Model_for_Pumps_as_Turbines_in_Run-of-River_and_In-Pipe_Microhydropower_Applications). Impulse turbines (Pelton, Turgo, and Crossflow units) require atmospheric discharge chambers; installing them in pressurized pipelines requires pressurized air-cushion counter-pressure vessels or draft tubes, which adds complexity and increases maintenance demands[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).

### Design Complexity, Mechanical Robustness, and Expected Service Life

Conventional micro-turbines contain multiple internal moving parts, including synchronizing rings, guide-vane linkages, and actuator seals, all operating within the pressurized flow[[7]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331699316_Cost_Model_for_Pumps_as_Turbines_in_Run-of-River_and_In-Pipe_Microhydropower_Applications). While these mechanisms provide hydraulic flexibility, they increase mechanical complexity and require regular inspection[[7]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331699316_Cost_Model_for_Pumps_as_Turbines_in_Run-of-River_and_In-Pipe_Microhydropower_Applications). Despite this complexity, high-grade conventional hydro-turbines are built for long operational lifespans ($20\text{ to }35\text{ years}$)[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321).

PATs use a single rotating assembly without internal variable linkages, simplifying the mechanical design[[6]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606). However, standard centrifugal pump bearings and mechanical seals are designed for forward pumping loads[[20]](https://www.scribd.com/document/559000000/Pumps-as-Turbines-Fundamentals-and-Applications-PDFDrive). Operating in reverse alters internal hydrodynamic axial and radial thrust profiles[[20]](https://www.scribd.com/document/559000000/Pumps-as-Turbines-Fundamentals-and-Applications-PDFDrive). If the unit operates far from its BEP or under transient conditions, increased bearing wear can limit the operating lifespan of a standard PAT to $10\text{ to }15\text{ years}$ before major bearing overhauls or rotating assembly replacements are required[[6]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606).

### Market Availability and Supply Chain Logistics

The industrial supply chains for these two technologies differ markedly:

- **Conventional Micro-Hydroturbines**: Turbines designed for in-pipe municipal installations remain specialized products. Procurement typically requires custom casting, site-specific runner design, and factory testing, resulting in lead times of $6\text{ to }14\text{ months}$[[6]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606). Sourcing replacement runners or specialized guide vanes during maintenance outages can cause extended downtime[[7]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331699316_Cost_Model_for_Pumps_as_Turbines_in_Run-of-River_and_In-Pipe_Microhydropower_Applications).
- **Pumps as Turbines**: Standard centrifugal pumps (single-stage end-suction, horizontal split-case, vertical inline, and multi-stage configurations) are mass-manufactured globally to ISO and ANSI standards[[7]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331699316_Cost_Model_for_Pumps_as_Turbines_in_Run-of-River_and_In-Pipe_Microhydropower_Applications). Procurement lead times are short ($1\text{ to }4\text{ weeks}$), and utility maintenance teams can service, overhaul, or replace standard pump components using existing municipal supply channels[[6]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2244606).

## Technical and Economic Challenges in Urban Water Supply Networks

Integrating energy recovery equipment into urban water distribution networks introduces operational, hygiene, and financial complexities distinct from traditional open-channel hydropower systems[[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/11/2034).

### Hydraulic Transients, Runaway Overspeed, and Water Hammer Dynamics

The primary mandate of a municipal water utility is delivering uninterrupted water service at specified pressures; power generation remains a secondary objective[[4]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318832074_Pressure_management_and_energy_recovery_in_water_distribution_networks_Development_of_design_and_selection_methodologies_using_three_pump-as-turbine_case_studies). Installing in-conduit turbomachinery introduces operational risks linked to sudden emergency load rejection events, such as electrical grid trips, generator faults, or rapid inverter shutdowns[[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/11/2034).

When a generator trips, counter-electromagnetic torque drops instantly to zero[[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/11/2034). The fluid drives the unconstrained runner to its *runaway speed* ($N_{\text{runaway}}$), which can reach $2.0\text{ to }2.5\text{ times}$ the rated operating speed ($N_R$) in low-specific-speed centrifugal PATs and reaction turbines[[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/11/2034). As the runner overspeeds, centrifugal forces within the impeller channels choke throughput flow ($Q$)[[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/11/2034).

This sudden reduction in discharge generates an upstream *water hammer* pressure wave ($\Delta P = \rho c \Delta v$, according to Joukowsky’s relation)[[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/11/2034). The resulting overpressure spikes can exceed $40\%\text{ to }65\%$ of nominal working pressure, risking pipe ruptures, joint displacement, and structural damage to aging distribution mains[[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/11/2034). Additionally, reversible pump-turbines and PATs can experience "S-curve" hydraulic instability, where multiple speeds and torques occur at the same head, producing low-frequency pressure pulsations and mechanical vibration[[12]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267400077_Pumps_yielding_power).

Preventing transient damage requires multi-tiered safety systems: fast-acting fail-open electro-hydraulic bypass valves, high-inertia mechanical flywheels, and surge protection vessels[[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/11/2034).

### Potable Water Hygiene and Regulatory Compliance

Turbomachinery installed directly in drinking water pipelines must meet stringent statutory materials certifications, including NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 and NSF/ANSI/CAN 372 in North America and WRAS in the United Kingdom[[27]](https://rentricity.com/rentricity-hydropower-nsf-edge/).

- **Materials and Leaching**: All wetted components (volutes, impellers, guide vanes, shafting, and wear rings) must use approved non-leaching materials, such as 304 or 316 stainless steel, lead-free bronze, or ductile iron lined with certified fusion-bonded epoxy[[27]](https://rentricity.com/rentricity-hydropower-nsf-edge/). Under NSF/ANSI 372, all wetted surfaces must maintain a weighted average lead content of $\le 0.25\%$[[27]](https://rentricity.com/rentricity-hydropower-nsf-edge/).
- **Sealing and Lubrication**: Mechanical face seals, elastomeric O-rings, and gaskets (typically food-grade EPDM or PTFE) must not leach volatile organic compounds or support bacterial and biofilm growth[[29]](https://die-pat.co.uk/pedestal-drinking-fountain-benefits/). Because conventional pump lubricants risk contaminating potable water during seal failures, installations require food-grade NSF H1 lubricants, dry canned rotors, or external bearing housings isolated from the flow stream[[27]](https://rentricity.com/rentricity-hydropower-nsf-edge/).
- **System Certification**: Standard industrial pumps lack potable water certification out of the factory. Integrators (such as Rentricity and InPipe Energy) assemble pre-tested, certified packages that fulfill municipal drinking water regulations[[27]](https://rentricity.com/rentricity-hydropower-nsf-edge/).

### Diurnal Demand Variability and Hydraulic Matching

Municipal water consumption exhibits significant daily variation, typically following a bimodal curve with prominent morning and evening peaks separated by lower daytime and nighttime demand[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).

This consumption profile creates an inverse relationship between available head and flow:

1. **Low-Demand Periods (Night)**: Low flow rates reduce pipeline friction losses, causing hydrostatic head upstream of the recovery station to peak while available flow drops to its daily minimum[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).
2. **Peak-Demand Periods (Morning and Evening)**: High flow rates increase dynamic friction losses across upstream pipelines, reducing available differential head across the energy recovery station[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).

Because a PAT's fixed internal geometry defines a single quadratic resistance curve, the unit cannot track this shifting locus of daily head-flow points on its own[[12]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267400077_Pumps_yielding_power). If flow falls below the runaway threshold, the PAT can stall and act as an unwanted obstruction[[12]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267400077_Pumps_yielding_power).

Maintaining reliable operation across variable flows requires coordinated control systems[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384):

- *Hydraulic Regulation (HR)* uses programmable logic controllers (PLCs) to manage motorized upstream throttle valves and parallel bypass PRVs, diverting excess flow or dissipating surplus head[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).
- *Electrical Regulation (ER)* dynamically alters generator rotational speed via a four-quadrant regenerative VFD, shifting the turbine's characteristic head-discharge curves to maximize energy recovery without mechanical throttling[[15]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259296613_PAT_Design_Strategy_for_Energy_Recovery_in_Water_Distribution_Networks_by_Electrical_Regulation).

### Spatial Constraints, Grid Interconnection, and Small-Scale Economics

- **Subsurface Vault Footprints**: Most potential energy recovery sites in urban networks are co-located with existing PRV chambers in compact underground vaults beneath roadways[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). Conventional hydro-turbines often require significant vault enlargements, separate draft tubes, and overhead hoists, leading to costly street excavations[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). Compact vertical inline PAT packages integrate more easily into standard pipe layouts, minimizing civil modification costs[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).
- **Electrical Interconnection vs. Behind-the-Meter Use**: Interconnecting small ($<50\text{ kW}$) generators to the electrical grid involves interconnection studies, anti-islanding switchgear, and utility permitting fees that can inflate project costs[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321). Water utilities often bypass grid connection by using energy on-site ("behind-the-meter") to power local infrastructure, such as chlorination dosing pumps, water quality analyzers, SCADA telemetry, and reservoir mixers[[22]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29EY.1943-7897.0000818).
- **Pico-Scale Economic Viability**: At smaller District Metering Area (DMA) inlets where available power is low ($1\text{ to }10\text{ kW}$), balance-of-plant costs—including VFD power electronics, automated control valves, and certified instrumentation—can account for over $60\%$ of total capital expenditures[[4]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318832074_Pressure_management_and_energy_recovery_in_water_distribution_networks_Development_of_design_and_selection_methodologies_using_three_pump-as-turbine_case_studies). Achieving acceptable economic returns on pico-scale sites requires modular, pre-engineered turnkey packages[[7]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331699316_Cost_Model_for_Pumps_as_Turbines_in_Run-of-River_and_In-Pipe_Microhydropower_Applications).

## Conclusions and Strategic Outlook

Recovering surplus hydraulic energy in urban water supply networks allows utilities to generate clean power while managing system pressure and reducing distribution leakage[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). Technology selection depends on site-specific hydraulic conditions:

- **High-Capacity Transmission Mains ($>50\text{--}100\text{ kW}$)**: Purpose-built reaction turbines (Francis or Kaplan) or multi-jet Turgo machines justify their higher initial capital cost ($1,500\text{--}2,500\text{ \euro{}}/\text{kW}$)[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). Their adjustable guide vanes maintain high operating efficiencies ($>80\%$) across wide seasonal and diurnal flow ranges, maximizing long-term energy generation[[5]](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid8.0321).
- **In-Conduit PRV Replacement and Distribution Vaults ($<50\text{ kW}$)**: Pumps as Turbines (PATs) provide the most cost-effective solution[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). Lower procurement costs ($200\text{--}500\text{ \euro{}}/\text{kW}$) and off-the-shelf availability shorten payback periods to under $3.5\text{ years}$[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384). When paired with Variable Frequency Drives (Electrical Regulation) and automated bypass manifolds, PAT systems maintain required downstream pressures while generating consistent renewable power[[15]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259296613_PAT_Design_Strategy_for_Energy_Recovery_in_Water_Distribution_Networks_by_Electrical_Regulation).

Future adoption in municipal networks will be driven by standardized, pre-engineered skids that combine NSF/ANSI 61 certified PAT units, regenerative inverters, smart SCADA controls, and automated transient protection into modular plug-and-play packages[[27]](https://rentricity.com/rentricity-hydropower-nsf-edge/). This approach allows water utilities to convert conventional pressure dissipation points into active energy recovery assets without compromising network reliability[[1]](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29WR.1943-5452.0000384).
