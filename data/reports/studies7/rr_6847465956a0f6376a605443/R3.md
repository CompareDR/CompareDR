# Age Limits on the Right to Vote: A Structured Debate

## TL;DR

- The case for a **minimum voting age of 16** is now genuinely contested and evidence-rich: developmental science shows the "cold cognition" voting requires is mature by roughly 15–16, real-world experience in Austria, Scotland and elsewhere shows 16–17-year-olds vote as competently as older first-timers and may form more durable voting habits — but critics (Chan & Clayton; Bergh's Norway trial) find genuine political-maturity gaps and warn the reform is partly partisan.
- The case for any **maximum voting age** is marginal and largely rejected: no established democracy imposes one, chronological age is a poor proxy for capacity, most older voters have no impairment, and disenfranchising them violates equal-citizenship norms and would strip voice from those most dependent on state programs; the live policy question is narrow — capacity to vote for people with advanced dementia or under guardianship — where the trend (Doe v. Rowe; the UN CRPD) is toward *fewer* exclusions.
- The disagreement is partly **empirical** (do 16-year-olds vote competently? does an older electorate really starve the young of resources?) and partly **irreducibly value-based** (is voting a competence-gated privilege or an equal-citizenship entitlement, and how should we weight inclusion against informed decision-making?). The mainstream, evidence-supported reform is lowering the minimum; the maximum is a philosophers' provocation, not a policy on any serious agenda.

## Key Findings

1. 1. **Every age line is philosophically arbitrary but administratively defensible.** Both sides concede a bright line at any age is imperfect; the debate is whether that justifies moving it, abolishing it, or replacing it with a capacity test. The near-universal answer for adults is that competence tests are illegitimate — a principle forged by the racist abuse of literacy tests, abolished in the US by the Voting Rights Act.
2. 2. **Developmental neuroscience cuts toward 16, not against it.** The Steinberg/Cauffman dual-systems model distinguishes "cold" deliberative cognition (adult-like by ~15–16) from "hot," emotionally aroused, peer-pressured decision-making (maturing into the 20s). Voting is a cold-cognition task; this is the strongest scientific plank for votes-at-16, though critics note the mapping of voting onto "cold cognition" is a contestable interpretive move.
3. 3. **Real-world evidence is encouraging but not decisive.** Austria (votes-at-16 since 2007) and Scotland (2014 onward) show 16–17-year-olds cast votes of comparable "quality" and turn out at higher rates than 18–24 first-timers, with some evidence of durable habit formation — but a Norwegian trial and UK panel data (Chan & Clayton) find real maturity gaps, and the Scottish engagement boost faded.
4. 4. **The UK is the major live case:** a Labour government elected in 2024 has introduced legislation (the Representation of the People Bill 2024–26, introduced 12 February 2026, with second reading 2 March 2026) to lower the voting age to 16 for all UK elections — enfranchising an estimated 1.7 million 16- and 17-year-olds (per The Constitution Society). Per the Scottish Parliament's SPICe (11 March 2026), the Bill "is expected to become law in time for the next UK general election which must be held by 15 August 2029."
5. 5. **A maximum age has essentially no institutional foothold** — only fringe/academic proposals (Douglas Stewart 1970; occasional op-eds) and the Vatican's rule barring cardinals 80+ from papal conclaves. US public opinion opposes a maximum voting age by 82%–18% (YouGov, March 2025), even as ~75% support a *maximum age for the presidency* — a legally and philosophically distinct question.

## Details

### 1. Democratic principles: the theory of the franchise

**The expansionary arc and the "all-affected" presumption.** The history of the franchise is a one-way ratchet of expansion — from propertied men to the propertyless, across race (US 15th Amendment), to women (19th Amendment; Switzerland only in 1971), and downward in age (US 26th Amendment, 1971, lowering the age from 21 to 18). Two theoretical principles underwrite this: the **all-affected-interests principle** (those whose interests are affected by collective decisions should have a say) and the **all-subjected principle** (those bound by the law should author it). Children and adolescents are plainly affected and subjected — they are taxed if they work, bound by criminal law, and will live longest with today's decisions — which is why theorists like John Wall argue the burden of proof lies on *excluders*, not includers. [Childism Institute][ref-01] [Springer][ref-02] The most-cited empirical anchor for expansion's real consequences: Lott and Kenny (1999, *Journal of Political Economy*) found US women's suffrage "coincided with immediate increases in state government expenditures and revenue," and Miller-style work links suffrage extensions to higher social and child-health spending — evidence that enfranchisement changes whose interests policy serves.

**Bright lines vs. arbitrariness.** Defenders of a fixed age concede no bright line perfectly tracks competence but argue bright-line rules are administratively necessary, predictable, non-intrusive, and protective — they avoid case-by-case competence adjudication, which is both costly and dangerous (see literacy tests). Critics (Wall; Vita Wallace's 1991 *Nation* essay "Give Children the Vote") reply that arbitrariness plus a heavy justificatory burden should push toward abolishing the minimum, not defending an arbitrary number.

**Right, privilege, or entitlement — and the "no competence test for adults" principle.** The pivotal doctrinal fact is that democracies do **not** competence-test adult voters. The US banned literacy tests nationwide via the Voting Rights Act (1965 for covered jurisdictions; a nationwide ban in the 1970 amendments, upheld in *Oregon v. Mitchell*), precisely because such tests had been instruments of racial disenfranchisement. This establishes a powerful consistency argument that cuts both ways: if we do not test 80-year-olds or poorly informed 40-year-olds for competence, on what principled basis do we test — via a blunt age proxy — 16-year-olds? Epistocrats like Jason Brennan (*Against Democracy*, 2016) bite the bullet and argue for competence-weighted franchises; their critics (Estlund's "qualified acceptability requirement"; Landemore's *Democratic Reason* on the epistemic value of cognitive diversity) argue expertise does not translate into a right to rule and that "informed" voters carry their own systematic biases.

**Equal-protection and disability framings.** The disability-rights lens (below, §3) reframes capacity exclusions as discrimination, and the same non-discrimination logic is invoked by age-based challenges. Consistency arguments over the **"age of majority" package** — criminal responsibility, military service, marriage, driving, alcohol, contracts, taxation — feature heavily: UK PM Keir Starmer justified votes-at-16 on the ground that "if you can work, if you can pay tax, if you can serve in your armed forces … then you ought to be able to vote." Opponents note the package is incoherent everywhere (the UK lets 16-year-olds vote but not drink, drive or buy fireworks; the US drinking age is 21 but voting 18), so "align the franchise with the age of majority" has no single answer.

### 2. Cognitive development: the minimum-age question

**Dual-systems neuroscience.** Laurence Steinberg's and BJ Casey's labs (2008) independently proposed the dual-systems / maturational-imbalance model: a socio-emotional incentive-processing system (ventral striatum, vmPFC) that peaks in mid-adolescence, and a cognitive-control system (lateral prefrontal, parietal, ACC) that matures more slowly into the twenties. The applied claim is quantified in Icenogle et al. (2019, *Law and Human Behavior* 43(1):69–85): "cognitive capacity reached adult levels around age 16, \[while\] psychosocial maturity reached adult levels beyond age 18, creating a 'maturity gap.'" As a 2024 review in *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience* summarizes, in **"cold" contexts** — unhurried, low-arousal, no peer pressure — "adolescents' capacities for decision making are indistinguishable from adults at and before age 16, the target age for most voting expansion initiatives," whereas **"hot" contexts** (emotionally charged, time-pressured, peer-present) still favor reward-seeking over control into the twenties. Researchers argue voting is a paradigmatic cold-cognition task ("the antithesis of the impulsive behavior" that justifies treating juveniles differently for criminal culpability or driving), so the neuroscience that supports a *higher* age for driving/criminal responsibility supports a *lower* age for voting. **Caveat:** the mapping of "voting = cold cognition" is an interpretive judgment, not an experimental result; a voter facing a charismatic populist in a charged election is arguably in a "hot" state, and the model was built to explain risk-taking, not civic reasoning.

**Political knowledge, competence and "voting correctly."** The empirical literature is genuinely mixed:

- **Wagner, Johann & Kritzinger (2012, *Electoral Studies*)**, using Austrian survey data, found under-18s' lower turnout "cannot be explained by a lower ability or motivation," and that the **quality of their vote choices** (congruence between preferences and party chosen) "is similar to that of older voters." (Caveat: small N for the 16–17 cell.)
- **Hart & Atkins (2011)** on US teens found 16–17-year-olds comparable to (sometimes exceeding) 18-year-olds on efficacy, knowledge, interest and tolerance.
- **Chan & Clayton (2006, *Political Studies*)**, using the British Household Panel Survey, reached the opposite conclusion: 16–17-year-olds are "significantly less mature" — lower political knowledge, interest and stable preferences — and the voting age should **not** be lowered. [Sage Journals][ref-03] [Oxford University Re…][ref-04]
- **Bergh (2013, *Electoral Studies*)**, exploiting Norway's 2011 municipal voting-age trial, found a "significant gap in maturity" between 16–17-year-olds and older voters and, crucially, **no evidence that maturity rises when the vote is granted** [ScienceDirect][ref-05] — a direct test that undercuts the "enfranchisement itself matures voters" hypothesis.

**Real-world jurisdictions with votes-at-16 or 17.** National votes-at-16: **Austria** (2007, first EU state), **Brazil** (optional at 16; compulsory 18–70), **Argentina** (16, optional until 18), [WorldAtlas][ref-06] **Ecuador**, **Cuba**, **Nicaragua** (since the 1980s), **Malta** (2018). [UNICEF][ref-07] Subnational/other: **Scotland** (2014 independence referendum, then Holyrood and local elections), [Springer][ref-08] **Wales** (Senedd and local since 2022), [Calcmyage][ref-09] **Isle of Man, Jersey, Guernsey**, **Estonia** (local), several **German Länder** and local elections plus the **2024 European Parliament** elections (Germany and **Belgium** allowed 16-year-olds to vote for the EP in 2024), **Greece** (17), **Indonesia** (17, or any age if married). [Archynewsy][ref-10] US municipalities: **Takoma Park, Maryland** (first, 2013), followed by **Hyattsville, Greenbelt, Mount Rainier, Riverdale Park** and others, [Maryland Matters][ref-11] plus **Berkeley and Oakland** school-board elections in California. [Umd][ref-12]

**Turnout and habit formation.** Franklin's "impressionable years"/habit thesis (voting is habit-forming; the first vote is pivotal) undergirds the strongest pro-16 argument: 16-year-olds are still embedded in home and school (stable address, civic-education context), whereas 18 coincides with residential churn (leaving home, university, first jobs), so first-voting-at-16 should yield higher lifelong turnout. Evidence:

- **Scotland:** an estimated 75% of registered 16–17-year-olds voted in the 2014 referendum — below the 85% overall but far above the 54% of 18–24-year-olds. [Oxford Academic][ref-13] Eichhorn & Hübner's longitudinal work (Edinburgh) found the engagement boost (petitions, demonstrations) **did not last**, but the **turnout habit did**: those first enfranchised at 16/17 were more likely to keep voting than those who first voted at 18.
- **Austria:** Zeglovits & Aichholzer (2014) found a first-time-voting boost among 16–17-year-olds.
- **Takoma Park, MD:** 16–17-year-olds turned out at ~44% in 2013 (roughly 4× the rate of registered over-18s), [Center for Informatio…][ref-14] ~70% in the 2020 municipal election (vs. 54% overall) [CNSMaryland.org][ref-15] [Umd][ref-16] — though these are tiny electorates (≈191 registered 16–17-year-olds in 2020) [Umd][ref-16] with severe generalizability limits.

**Counterarguments.** Opponents stress: susceptibility to parental/teacher/social-media influence; uneven civic education; the finding (Chan & Clayton; Bergh) of genuine maturity deficits; the paradox that **18–24-year-olds already have the lowest turnout**, so lowering the floor mechanically worsens headline turnout statistics; and the **partisan-motivation objection** — that center-left parties push votes-at-16 expecting to benefit. On the last point the evidence is thin and contested: one analysis cited by Northeastern's Gergana Dimova suggests "about 13% more of the youngest voters prefer left- to right-wing parties," but "it is not clear whether these effects go beyond the first vote," and 16–17-year-olds do not reliably vote as a distinct bloc.

**Radical alternatives on the minimum side.** (a) **No minimum at all / self-demonstrated interest:** John Wall (*Give Children the Vote*, 2021), Robin Chen, and Vita Wallace argue suffrage cannot be legitimately age-limited and propose "proxy-claim" suffrage — any child may claim their own vote whenever they wish, with a proxy until then. [Childism Institute][ref-01] (b) **Demeny / proxy voting:** parents cast fractional votes on behalf of minor children (Paul Demeny, 1986; discussed in France in the 1920s, near-adopted; seriously floated in Hungary and Japan amid low fertility; echoed by US figures including JD Vance's 2021 "thought experiment" and Ross Douthat). [National Catholic Re…][ref-17] Serious objections: it violates the **secret ballot** and **one-person-one-vote**, treats children as parental property, and doubles the weight of parents rather than representing children's own views. (c) **Competence tests instead of age:** near-universally rejected because of the literacy-test history and the epistocracy critiques above.

### 3. Cognitive decline: the maximum-age question

**Prevalence and heterogeneity.** Dementia is strongly age-related but affects a minority even at advanced ages. Per the Alzheimer's Association *2025 Alzheimer's Disease Facts and Figures*: "Five percent of people age 65 to 74, 13.2% of people age 75 to 84, and 33.4% of people age 85 or older have Alzheimer's dementia." The decisive point for the franchise debate: even in the oldest band, **two-thirds have no Alzheimer's dementia**, and decline is highly heterogeneous — so **chronological age is a poor proxy for voting capacity**. Prevalence is also falling in some cohorts (a Swedish study of 85-year-olds found dementia prevalence dropped from 29.8% in 1986–87 to 21.7% in 2008–10, attributed to higher education and better stroke care). [nih][ref-18]

**Crystallized vs. fluid intelligence.** Aging typically erodes fluid abilities (processing speed, working memory) while **crystallized** knowledge (vocabulary, accumulated political and institutional knowledge, experience of past elections) is preserved or grows — a strong argument that older voters' accumulated civic knowledge offsets processing-speed decline, and that "competence" is not monotonically decreasing with age.

**The real, narrow policy question — capacity and guardianship.** The live issue is not a maximum age but whether people with advanced dementia or under guardianship may vote. Landmarks:

- ***Doe v. Rowe* (US District Court, Maine, 2001):** struck down Maine's constitutional bar on voting by persons "under guardianship for reasons of mental illness" as violating the Equal Protection Clause, due process, and the ADA. [ElderLawAnswers][ref-19] It adopted the narrow **"Doe standard":** a person may be excluded only if they "lack the capacity to understand the nature and effect of voting such that they cannot make an individual choice." [ResearchGate][ref-20]
- **CAT-V (Competence Assessment Tool for Voting):** Appelbaum, Bonnie & Karlawish operationalized the Doe standard; their 2005 *American Journal of Psychiatry* study of Alzheimer's patients found voting capacity correlated with MMSE and dementia stage — mild-to-moderate patients generally retained capacity, severe did not — and that the *desire* to vote was independent of capacity. [nih][ref-21]
- Many US **state constitutions still exclude persons "of unsound mind"** or under guardianship, in tension with these standards.
- **UN CRPD Article 29 and General Comment No. 1 (2014):** the CRPD Committee holds that capacity assessments for voting are themselves discriminatory — Article 29 "does not foresee any reasonable restriction" for persons with disabilities, [The Small Places][ref-22] and Article 12(2) requires recognizing legal capacity "on an equal basis with others." [GPIL][ref-23] Germany's Federal Constitutional Court (29 Jan 2019) struck down voting exclusions for those under full guardianship as unconstitutional, [GPIL][ref-23] while declining to fully adopt the CRPD Committee's absolutist reading. Canada and others have abolished guardianship-based disenfranchisement.

**Assisted and long-term-care voting.** Enfranchising impaired-but-capable older voters raises operational issues: absentee-ballot assistance, nursing-home and mobile polling (used in Canada and some US/European jurisdictions), and documented risks of **undue influence / "ballot harvesting"** in care settings — which is precisely why "family voting" (one person completing others' ballots) is treated as electoral fraud, [The Week][ref-24] and why the secret ballot matters.

**Why no democracy imposes a maximum age.** The arguments against are overwhelming and largely symmetrical with the pro-inclusion case: a maximum age (a) disenfranchises a competent majority to catch an impaired minority (massively over-inclusive); (b) is simultaneously under-inclusive (misses impaired younger people); (c) removes voice precisely from those **most dependent on state programs** (pensions, healthcare); (d) violates equal-citizenship norms; and (e) invites a slippery slope. Historical/fringe proposals exist — **Douglas Stewart (1970)** proposed disenfranchisement at retirement or age 70; the idea resurfaced after Brexit [Springer][ref-25] and in occasional op-eds — and the philosophical literature (Poama & Volacu 2023, *Politics, Philosophy & Economics*) analyzes but ultimately rejects age-weighted voting, showing "equal influence" reasons actually cut against it (most older citizens are affected by most election outcomes; "not all older electors die on election day"). [Sage Journals][ref-26] Public opinion is decisive: **82% of Americans say there should be no maximum voting age; only 18% support one** (YouGov, March 17–19, 2025, n=1,140, MoE ~4%) — with support at 25% among under-30s and just 7% among those 65+.

**Contrast with candidate/office age limits.** Running for office is legally and philosophically distinct from voting. The US Constitution sets *minimum* ages for office (25 House, 30 Senate, 35 President) but no maximum, [Shortform][ref-27] and ***U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton* (1995)** held that neither states nor Congress may add qualifications for federal office beyond those in the Constitution — so a statutory maximum age for members of Congress would require a constitutional amendment. Crucially, voters retain the choice to reject an aged candidate, whereas a maximum *voting* age strips the citizen's own voice. This explains the polling asymmetry: **75% of Americans support a maximum age to serve as president** (YouGov, March 2025), and a separate YouGov poll found 58% support a maximum age limit for elected officials generally (of whom 39% named 70 as the cap) — even while 82% reject a maximum for voters. Mandatory retirement ages for judges and commercial pilots (age 65, under periodic review) are the closest analogues — but they gate *powered authority over others*, not the equal expression of one's own preference.

### 4. Intergenerational equity

**Demographic weight.** Electorates are aging fast. **Japan** — the world's oldest society — had **29.4% of its population aged 65+** as of September 2025 (Statistics Bureau of Japan / Ministry of Internal Affairs), projected toward **~35% by 2040** and roughly **37% by 2050** (UN World Population Prospects 2024). In Japan's 2021 lower-house election the **median age of actual voters was 59** (East Asia Forum), and turnout was ~37% among people in their 20s versus ~62% among those 70+. Projected 65+ shares by 2050 (UN WPP 2024 / national statistics offices): **Italy ~36.8%**, **South Korea ~40%** (already a "super-aged" society at 20.3% in 2025, Statistics Korea), **Germany** rising from ~23% (2024) toward the ~30% range, **UK** from ~19–20% toward the mid-20s%, **US** from 17% (2022) to **~23% by 2050** (Population Reference Bureau). The OECD projects the area-wide **old-age dependency ratio roughly doubling to ~52% by 2050**, exceeding 70 persons 65+ per 100 working-age adults in Italy, Japan, Korea and Spain by 2054 (OECD *Pensions at a Glance 2025*). In the US, **Pew projected 65+ voters would be ~23% of the electorate in 2020** — the highest share since at least 1970 — and Census data show **74.7% of over-65s voted in 2024** vs. 47.7% of 18–24-year-olds.

**The "gerontocracy" thesis.** The worry: an older electorate skews spending toward pensions and healthcare and away from education, childcare, housing and climate. Evidence is real but contested:

- **Supporting:** Poterba (1997) found the share of elderly residents negatively associated with public education spending (replicated by Brunner & Balsdon and others). Klien, Melki & Pickering (2021, *Journal of Comparative Economics*), exploiting Austria's repeal of compulsory voting, found education spending **falls when the elderly exceed ~21% of the electorate**. Vlandas (2023, *The Political Quarterly*) documents an OECD-wide "age cleavage" in education-spending support larger than the income cleavage.
- **Contrary:** the "myth of gerontocracy" critique (Boston Review) notes US per-pupil funding **rose** across the very decades of electoral aging; older voters support school spending for grandchildren and from civic motives; and families internalize intergenerational altruism. Vanhuysse's Intergenerational Justice Index and the Intergenerational Foundation (IF, a UK advocacy group with a stake in the framing) argue the young are systematically disadvantaged, but the causal link from electoral age structure to policy is mediated by turnout, not just population share.

**The disenfranchisement of the future.** Unborn generations cannot vote at all, which is most acute for climate. Institutional responses that **do not touch the franchise** dominate: **Wales' Well-being of Future Generations Act (2015) and its Future Generations Commissioner** (currently Derek Walker); [Wikipedia][ref-28] **Hungary's Ombudsman for Future Generations**; **Israel's former Commission for Future Generations** (2001–2006); **Finland's Committee for the Future** (a parliamentary committee); and, at global level, the **UN Special Envoy for Future Generations** and the **Declaration on Future Generations adopted at the September 2024 Summit of the Future**. [Futuregenerations][ref-29] Proposals for **youth quotas** or a legislative "house of the future" remain largely aspirational.

**Weighted voting.** Proposals to weight votes by remaining life expectancy (giving the young more electoral weight) are analyzed and rejected in the philosophical literature (Poama & Volacu 2023) on equal-influence grounds; they also violate one-person-one-vote.

**The turnout counterpoint.** A powerful rejoinder to the whole gerontocracy frame: the binding constraint is **low youth turnout, not the absence of age limits**. If 18–29-year-olds voted at the rate of the over-65s, the "grey power" imbalance would shrink without touching anyone's franchise. This reframes the remedy toward turnout levers — automatic registration, civic education, and **compulsory voting** (Australia's ~90%+ turnout; also Belgium, Brazil), which compresses age-turnout gaps — rather than age limits.

### 5. Inclusivity vs. informed decision-making

**The competence objection applies symmetrically — and self-defeatingly.** Converse's classic finding of low information among the median voter of *any* age implies that a serious competence bar would disenfranchise much of the adult electorate, not just the young or old. The **"miracle of aggregation"** and the **Condorcet Jury Theorem** (under its assumptions: voters better than chance, voting independently) suggest large electorates can aggregate to good collective decisions even when individuals are poorly informed — an argument for inclusion at both ends of the age range. **Rational ignorance** (Downs: it is individually rational not to invest in political information given a vanishing probability of being decisive) undercuts competence-based exclusion arguments *symmetrically*: it afflicts 45-year-olds as much as 16-or 85-year-olds, so it cannot single out an age group.

**Legitimacy and consent.** Maximal inclusion is defended on consent-of-the-governed grounds: exclusion tends to produce **policy neglect** of the excluded (the suffrage-and-spending literature — Lott & Kenny 1999; Husted & Kenny 1997, who found franchise expansion to lower-income voters raised welfare spending; the Swiss women's-suffrage study finding a 28% rise in social spending). If exclusion causes neglect, then excluding the young (who then get under-served on education/housing/climate) or the old (who would be under-served on pensions/care) is self-reinforcing and hard to justify on democratic-legitimacy grounds.

The single most important structural fact: **the voting age has, in modern history, only ever been lowered, never raised**, [Calcmyage][ref-09] and **no democracy has ever adopted a maximum**.

- **US, minimum age:** A Hill-HarrisX poll (2019) found **75% opposed** letting 17-year-olds vote and **84% opposed** votes-at-16, with opposition in every age group. [The Hill][ref-30] YouGov (March 17–19, 2025, n=1,140): **56%** want to keep the minimum at 18; only **25%** would lower it below 18 or abolish it; **19%** would raise it. [Western New Englan…][ref-32] The Vote 16 Research Network (an advocacy-aligned network led by the University of Maryland) finds support around one-third, rising ~10 points with pro-competence messaging [Substack][ref-33] — this is a stakeholder, not a neutral source.

## Recommendations

These are staged, conditional recommendations for a decision-maker weighing reform — not an endorsement of one value framework.

**On the minimum age:**

1. 1. **If the goal is turnout and habit formation, lowering to 16 is defensible — but pair it with mandatory civic education and automatic/school-based registration.** The habit evidence (Scotland turnout persistence; Austria; Takoma Park) is the strongest empirical plank, and it works precisely because 16-year-olds are reachable in schools. Lowering the age *without* the civic-education scaffolding forfeits most of the expected benefit and validates the "they're not ready" critique.
2. 2. **Treat single-jurisdiction results as provisional.** Austria and Scotland are the best cases but are still few, and the Norwegian trial (Bergh) and Chan & Clayton show the maturity question is not settled. **Benchmark to watch:** whether 16–17-year-old turnout in the UK's first post-reform general election exceeds 18–24-year-old turnout (as in Scotland/Austria) and whether the habit persists into the *second* election. If it does not, the habit-formation rationale weakens materially.
3. 3. **Reject competence tests and proxy/Demeny voting.** The former is tainted by the literacy-test history and fails the "no test for adults" consistency principle; the latter violates the secret ballot and one-person-one-vote. If a polity wants to represent children's interests, prefer non-franchise institutions (a Future Generations Commissioner) or, at most, lowering the age — not proxy votes that double parents' weight.

**On the maximum age:** 4. **Do not adopt a maximum voting age.** It is over- and under-inclusive, violates equal citizenship, and has no institutional precedent. The legitimate concern it gestures at — voters with severe cognitive impairment — is far better handled by the narrow, capacity-based *Doe* standard than by a blunt age cut-off. 5. **Modernize capacity and assisted-voting rules toward inclusion.** Replace blanket "unsound mind"/guardianship exclusions with the *Doe*/CAT-V functional standard (or, following the CRPD, no capacity test at all), and invest in mobile polling and audited assistance in care settings with strong anti-undue-influence safeguards. **Threshold that would change this:** credible, systematic evidence of large-scale ballot manipulation in care settings would justify tightening *procedural* safeguards — but still not an age cap.

**On intergenerational equity (the real underlying worry):** 6. **Address gerontocracy through turnout and future-generations institutions, not disenfranchisement.** Compulsory voting (Australian model) and automatic registration compress age-turnout gaps directly; statutory Future Generations bodies (Wales, Finland) give the unborn and the young institutional voice without weighting or removing anyone's vote. **Benchmark:** if youth turnout rises to parity with older cohorts and policy still skews sharply against the young, the case for structural reform (e.g., a house of the future) strengthens; if the gap is mostly turnout-driven, turnout levers suffice.

## Caveats

- **Single-case and small-sample limits.** Much votes-at-16 evidence rests on Austria and Scotland; the US municipal cases (Takoma Park ≈191 registered 16–17-year-olds) are far too small to generalize. Survey studies (Wagner et al.) have small cells for the 16–17 group. Findings conflict (Wagner/Hart & Atkins vs. Chan & Clayton/Bergh), and this conflict is not resolved.
- **Contested causation in the gerontocracy literature.** Poterba-style negative age-education associations coexist with evidence that per-pupil spending rose during electoral aging; the causal chain from electorate age structure to policy is mediated by turnout and is disputed.
- **Advocacy stakes.** Vote16USA / Vote 16 Research Network, the Electoral Reform Society, FairVote, and the Intergenerational Foundation all have positions in this debate; AARP and pensioner lobbies have the opposite stake. Their framings should not be read as neutral fact, and I have flagged them where cited.
- **Projections are not facts.** Demographic figures for 2050 (UN WPP 2024; OECD *Pensions at a Glance 2025*; PRB) are model projections dependent on fertility, mortality and migration assumptions and use language like "projected to."
- **Fast-moving legal status.** The UK Representation of the People Bill 2024–26 was introduced 12 February 2026 (second reading 2 March 2026) and had not completed passage as of this writing; the voting age for UK general elections remains 18 until the Bill is enacted, though it is "expected to become law in time for the next UK general election which must be held by 15 August 2029" (Scottish Parliament SPICe, March 2026). CRPD-driven capacity reforms are proceeding unevenly and some states retain declarations/reservations.
- **The core disagreement is partly unresolvable by evidence.** Whether voting is a competence-gated privilege or an equal-citizenship entitlement, and how to weight inclusion against informed decision-making, are value questions. Evidence can settle whether 16-year-olds vote competently or whether older electorates starve the young of resources; it cannot settle whether competence *should* gate the franchise at all.

## Synthesis: where the disagreement really lies

**Empirical (resolvable, in principle, with evidence):**

- Do 16–17-year-olds vote as competently as older first-time voters? (Mixed: Austria/Hart & Atkins say yes; Chan & Clayton/Bergh say no.)
- Does first-voting-at-16 raise lifelong turnout durably? (Scotland says the turnout habit persists even as broader engagement fades; needs replication.)
- Does an older electorate causally skew policy against the young? (Contested; mediated by turnout.)
- Is chronological age a good proxy for voting capacity in old age? (No — the prevalence and heterogeneity data are clear.)

**Value-based (irreducible):**

- Is the franchise a **competence-gated privilege** (which licenses both minimums and, in principle, capacity screens) or an **equal-citizenship entitlement** (which pushes toward abolishing both minimums and any maximum)?
- How should a democracy weigh **inclusion** (legitimacy, consent, avoiding neglect of the excluded) against **informed decision-making** (decision quality)?
- Whose interests count and how much across time — the **all-affected principle** applied to children and the unborn versus the **equal present influence** of living citizens?

The asymmetry is the headline. The case for **lowering the minimum** is mainstream, actively legislated (UK), and supported by a real if contested body of neuroscience and turnout evidence; the burden it must meet — that 16-year-olds can vote competently and that inclusion is worth a possible short-run turnout-statistics dip — is at least arguably met. The case for **any maximum** must establish something far stronger and empirically false: that age is a good proxy for incapacity (it is not), that disenfranchising millions of competent citizens is proportionate to excluding an impaired minority (it is not), and that equal citizenship may be revoked for the living (a proposition democracies have never accepted). That is why votes-at-16 is on statute books and a maximum voting age exists nowhere but in thought experiments.
