# Debate whether there should be age limits - minimum or maximum - for voting

## Democratic Principles and the Normative Foundation of Suffrage

### Universal Suffrage, "One Person One Vote," and Age as a Suspect Classification

**The Philosophical and Legal Architecture of Political Equality**

The principle that each person's vote should carry equal weight — "one person, one vote" (OPOV) — stands as the foundational commitment of modern democratic legal orders. Its canonical U.S. expression came in *Reynolds v. Sims* (1964), where the Supreme Court held that the Equal Protection Clause requires state legislative districts to be composed of roughly equal populations, ensuring that "the weight and worth of the citizens' votes as nearly as is practicable must be the same" ([*Reynolds v. Sims*, 377 U.S. 533 (1964)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/377/533/)). Historically, the OPOV doctrine was wielded to dismantle property qualifications, racial disenfranchisement, malapportionment favoring rural over urban districts, and plural voting arrangements in which university graduates and property owners could cast multiple votes ([Wikipedia, "One man, one vote"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_man,_one_vote)). In the United Kingdom, successive Reform Acts by 1950 had extended the franchise to nearly all adult citizens and eliminated plural voting for Westminster elections, with plural voting for local government finally abolished by the Representation of the People Act 1969 ([Wikipedia, "One man, one vote"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_man,_one_vote)). Yet throughout this long line of cases dismantling exclusions, **age was never subjected to OPOV scrutiny** in the way that race, property, or gender were — a signal that age occupies a different normative category in constitutional doctrine (on the historical trajectory from 21 to 18, see S1.2; [Wikipedia, "One man, one vote"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_man,_one_vote)).

Daniel Wodak (2025) argues in "One Person, One Vote" that the slogan, despite its iconic status, has been philosophically ambiguous and undertheorized. Wodak distinguishes two dimensions of OPOV: a *formal* requirement that each voter cast one ballot, and a *substantive* requirement that each ballot carry equal weight in determining outcomes. The formal dimension has been exhaustively applied; the substantive dimension, which would raise questions about whose preferences are institutionally empowered, remains less explored. Wodak suggests that OPOV's historical power came from its operation as a slogan in *democratic movements* rather than as a fully specified philosophical principle, and that its deployment to new contexts (including age-based restrictions) requires careful philosophical work rather than automatic extension ([Wodak, "One Person One Vote," *Oxford Studies in Political Philosophy* 11 (2025): 32–59](https://philarchive.org/archive/WODOPO); [Wodak, "What Is the Point of Political Equality?" (2024)](https://philarchive.org/archive/WODWIT)).

**Democratic Theories of Political Equality and Suffrage**

Charles Beitz (1990) provides one of the most systematic treatments of political equality, distinguishing three families of theory. *Best results* theories hold that political equality is required because equal consideration produces substantively better decisions. *Popular will* theories hold that equality is required because governance must reflect the preferences of the governed. *Procedural* theories treat political equality as an intrinsic right of citizenship, independent of outcomes ([Beitz, *Political Equality* (Princeton University Press, 1990)](https://philarchive.org/archive/WODWIT); see also [Poama & Volacu (2023), citing Beitz's tripartite distinction](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14748851211062604)). Robert Dahl's *Democracy and Its Critics* (1989) grounds democratic legitimacy in "effective participation" — the requirement that citizens have "ample and equal opportunities for discovering and affirming what choice would best serve their interests" ([Dahl, *Democracy and Its Critics* (Yale University Press, 1989)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/270032826_Democracy_and_Its_Critics)). Dahl's framework treats universal suffrage as near-permissive: exclusions must be justified by a compelling showing that the excluded group cannot participate effectively, and the burden rests on the excluder, not the excluded.

Yet democratic theory is not monolithic. *Epistocratic* theories contend that suffrage restrictions — potentially including age limits — may be justified where voters lack sufficient knowledge (Brennan 2016; López-Guerra 2014; see S5.1 for detailed treatments of the "right to a competent electorate" argument and the competence-based framework for electoral exclusions). These theories stand in tension with the inclusivity principle, which treats suffrage as a badge of equal citizenship — the "badge of equal citizenship" concept articulated by Karst (1977) in the *Kentucky Law Journal* — and imposes a stigma of political inferiority on any excluded group.

**The Affected-Interest Principle and Its Limits**

The "all-affected interests" principle (AAP) holds that all those affected by political decisions have a right to participate in making them. The principle has intuitive force in the context of youth suffrage: if climate policy, education funding, and debt burdens are decided by elected officials, then young people who will bear the long-term consequences of those decisions are presumptively affected and thus presumptively entitled to a voice. The AAP provides perhaps the strongest philosophical argument for *lowering* the minimum voting age: no one, the argument runs, is more affected by long-horizon policy decisions than young people, who will live longest with their consequences ([Warren, "The All Affected Interests Principle in Democratic Theory," 2017](https://www.oidp.net/docs/repo/doc211.pdf); [Fung, "The Principle of Affected Interests"](http://www.archonfung.net/papers/FungAffectedInterestsChap11.pdf)).

Can the AAP coherently support a *maximum* voting age? Here the principle encounters a significant asymmetry. On one view, older citizens are equally affected by policy decisions and retain the franchise on the same ground as any affected voter. On another, intergenerational-justice view, older voters' long tenure and higher turnout give them disproportionate influence over policies whose costs will fall disproportionately on younger and future generations — meaning that the AAP, properly applied to *future* affected persons, might require *reducing* older voters' influence rather than preserving it ([Angell, "Should We Increase Young People's Voting Power?", *Ethical Theory and Moral Practice* 28 (2024): 617–634](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-024-10443-2); [Bengtson & Albertsen, "The Voting Rights of Senior Citizens," *Ethical Theory and Moral Practice* (2023)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w)). The AAP thus cannot straightforwardly generate a maximum age without collapsing into the parallel problem of future generations — those most affected by current decisions who have no voice at all. This structural asymmetry between the young (affected and voiceless) and the elderly (affected and over-represented) is fertile ground for theoretical contestation, as discussed in S3 and S4 below.

**The "Babes in Arms" Stress Test**

The most radical extension of the affected-interests principle comes from Goodin and Lau (2011), who argue that if affected interests alone ground the franchise, then even infants should be enfranchised — "babes in arms" — by proxy through their parents or guardians. Their argument serves as a logical stress test: if the unadorned AAP entails infant enfranchisement, then either the AAP is insufficient as a standalone criterion, or the boundary problem (who counts as affected, and how directly) must be resolved by something other than affectedness alone ([Goodin & Lau (2011), cited in Angell (2024)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379372555_Should_We_Increase_Young_People's_Voting_Power); [ResearchGate, "Why the voting age should be lowered to 16"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/316497525_Why_the_voting_age_should_be_lowered_to_16)). Most democratic theorists stop short of this conclusion, treating the AAP as a necessary but not sufficient condition, supplemented by requirements of *competence* or *proximity of interest* — the idea that a proxy voter must share the dependent's interests. The "babes in arms" position thus reveals a tension within the affected-interests framework: it demonstrates the principle's *scope* (affectedness is not inherently age-limited) but also its *limits* (affectedness alone cannot determine boundary questions without additional principles of competence and relationality).

**International Human Rights Law and "Evolving Capacities"**

International human rights law leaves the age threshold for suffrage to state discretion — neither the ICCPR nor the UDHR sets an age floor or ceiling — while the CRC's "evolving capacities" framework and the Committee on the Rights of the Child's praise for states that lowered the voting age create normative pressure against categorical exclusion based on childhood status alone ([UNICEF Innocenti, "Should Children Vote?" (orientation only)](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote); see S1.3 for detailed treaty-by-treaty analysis of ICCPR Article 25, UDHR Article 21, CRC Articles 12 and General Comment No. 20, and ECHR Article 14).

**Conceptions of Childhood and the Case for Youth Disenfranchisement**

Gillett and Heyward (2023) argue that the philosophical case for youth disenfranchisement rests on implicit conceptions of childhood that deserve scrutiny. They examine whether the dominant Western conception — childhood as a period of developmental *becoming*, marked by dependence and incomplete rationality — is the only coherent account, or whether alternative conceptions of childhood as a *mode of being* with its own forms of agency would weaken the case for exclusion ([Gillett & Heyward, "Let Them Be Children? Age Limits in Voting and Conceptions of Childhood" (Oxford Academic, 2023)](https://academic.oup.com/book/46504/chapter/407849330)). If childhood is construed primarily as a status of *institutional dependence* — a relationship to adult guardians rather than a deficiency of the child — then the premises for exclusion (that children lack autonomy and require adult representation) blur into the question of whether institutional dependency should translate to political silence. Hamilton (2012) similarly argues that the age of majority entangles cognitive, moral, and civic considerations that cannot be reduced to a single competence threshold, and that the philosophical literature on democratic inclusion has not adequately addressed the *intersection* of cognitive development theory with normative theories of political equality ([Hamilton, "Democratic Inclusion, Cognitive Development, and the Age of Majority," *William & Mary Law Review* (2012)](https://scholarship.law.wm.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2505&context=facpubs)).

**Age, Rights, and the Distinctiveness of Age as a Qualification**

Why has age been treated differently from other suffrage restrictions in democratic theory? The historical pattern is striking: property qualifications, racial bars, and gender exclusions were each dismantled by democratic movements appealing to the equal moral standing of persons — to the idea that no one's political voice may be silenced on grounds that reflect caste, class, or immutable identity. Age differs in several respects. First, age is *internal to the excluded group* in a way that race and sex are not: every person who reaches voting age (or loses it, in age-cap proposals) is a "transitional" member of the excluded category, whereas racial and sexual identities are (ordinarily) lifelong. Second, age can serve as a *proxy* for characteristics that are themselves democratically relevant — cognitive maturity, life experience, stake in long-horizon outcomes — in a way that race and sex are not. This does not make age *innocent* as a qualification, but it makes the burden of proof different: the question is not merely "is this category arbitrary?" but "does the age threshold track a genuinely relevant difference, or is it an administrable shortcut for a trait that varies widely within the age band?" Third, age operates on a *gradient* simultaneously: it can mark the onset of capacity (minimum age) and its decline (maximum age), creating a structural tension that no other classification reproduces. The ECtHR's approach under Article 14, which applies lighter proportionality scrutiny to age-based distinctions than to race or sex discrimination and affords states a wide margin of appreciation in setting voting-age thresholds, further illustrates this asymmetry (see S1.3 for detailed analysis; [ECtHR, Guide on Article 14](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_14_art_1_protocol_12_eng); [ECtHR, Guide on Article 3 of Protocol No. 1](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_3_protocol_1_eng)).

The upshot is that age *does not behave like* race, gender, or property as a suffrage classification, but the reasons for its distinctiveness are substantive rather than structural. Age can proxy for capacities that democratic legitimacy takes seriously; the challenge for democratic theory is to determine whether a fixed age threshold is a proportionate proxy for those capacities or an administratively convenient line that obscures the very variation it purports to manage. The CRC concept of "evolving capacities," the philosophical literature on childhood, and the affected-interests principle converging around the "babes in arms" limit case together press toward the conclusion that *no* age threshold can be philosophically innocent — that whatever lines democracies draw, they must be justified by reasons that can withstand scrutiny across the life course.

### Historical Evolution of Voting Age Thresholds: From 21 to 18 to 16

The voting age has never been a fixed natural boundary. It is a politically contested line that has moved in one direction only — downward — across the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, in three distinct waves: from 21 to 18 after World War II, from 18 to 16 in a nascent but accelerating contemporary wave, and through subnational experimentation that has created asymmetrical franchises in multiple democracies. No democracy on record has ever *raised* its minimum voting age.

#### The Pre-WWII Norm of 21+

Before the Second World War, a voting age of twenty-one was the near-universal standard, rooted in British colonial practices that had been carried into American constitutional tradition. The U.S. Constitution, adopted in 1787, left the definition of the elective franchise to the states, and all retained the age of twenty-one ([EBSCO, "U.S. Voting Age Is Lowered to Eighteen"](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/us-voting-age-lowered-eighteen/)). The first cracks appeared during World War II, when the draft-age argument — that those old enough to fight should be old enough to vote — surfaced intermittently in the U.S. Congress, beginning in the 1940s. In 1943, Georgia became the first U.S. state to lower its voting age to eighteen by referendum, followed by Kentucky in 1955 ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)). These were wartime measures rather than principled suffrage reform, and most states held the line at twenty-one.

#### The First Wave: 21 → 18

The first national-level lowering to eighteen occurred in **Czechoslovakia in 1946**, setting a precedent that would take two decades to diffuse widely. By 1968, seventeen states had reduced the voting age to eighteen, of which eight were in Latin America and eight were communist countries ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)). The movement accelerated in the late 1960s and 1970s across the established democracies.

The **United Kingdom** passed the Representation of the People Act 1969, becoming the first major Western democracy to lower the voting age from twenty-one to eighteen. Harold Wilson's Labour government framed the change as completing the journey toward full adult suffrage, with the Second World War's conscription precedent and the maturing baby-boom cohort providing political momentum ([History and Policy, "'The Last Milestone' on the Journey to Full Adult Suffrage"](https://historyandpolicy.org/policy-papers/papers/the-last-milestone-on-the-journey-to-full-adult-suffrage/)).

The **United States** followed with the **26th Amendment**, ratified on 1 July 1971 with extraordinary speed — Congress proposed it in March 1971 and the states ratified it in barely three months, the fastest constitutional amendment in American history. The catalyst was the Vietnam War: by 1970, nearly half of American combat casualties were under twenty-one, making the slogan "old enough to fight, old enough to vote" politically unassailable. The amendment enfranchised approximately eleven million new voters ([National WWII Museum, "'Old Enough to Fight, Old Enough to Vote'"](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/voting-age-26th-amendment); [EBSCO, "U.S. Voting Age Is Lowered to Eighteen"](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/us-voting-age-lowered-eighteen/)). The amendment was preceded by the Supreme Court's fractured 5–4 decision in *Oregon v. Mitchell* (1970), which upheld Congress's power to lower the voting age in federal elections but cast doubt over state and local elections — a constitutional gap the 26th Amendment closed.

Canada (1970), West Germany (1970), Australia (1973), France (1974), and Sweden (1975) all followed within the same decade. By the end of the twentieth century, eighteen had become the overwhelming global standard. Within the European Union, **Italy** represented the last holdout: until July 2021, voting for the Senate (upper house) required age twenty-five, while the Chamber of Deputies had been at eighteen since 1975. The Italian Parliament voted in July 2021 to lower the Senate voting age to eighteen, enfranchising approximately four million additional young adults ([PBS NewsHour, 8 July 2021](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/italian-senate-endorses-lowering-voting-age-from-25-to-18); [Reuters, 8 July 2021](https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/italian-parliament-lowers-voting-age-senate-elections-2021-07-08/)). This was a downward move; no state moved upward.

What distinguishes the 21→18 wave from the current 16-voting-age campaigns is the conjunction of *military conscription* and *demographic pressure* (the baby-boom cohort) as the primary mobilizing forces. The "old enough to fight" argument was powerful precisely because it linked suffrage to a specific civic obligation — military service — that states already imposed on eighteen-year-olds, creating an asymmetry that was intellectually and politically destabilizing. The contemporary 16-voting-age campaigns largely lack this conscription footing; they rest instead on principles of affected interests, intergenerational justice, and civic-education benefits, as well as the climate activism of the school-strike generation, which brought demands for a lowered voting age to Germany and the UK in the late 2010s ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)).

#### The Second Wave: 18 → 16 — National Adoptions

The first moves toward a voting age of sixteen began at the *subnational* level. In **Lower Saxony, Germany**, the municipal voting age was lowered to sixteen in 1995 — the first jurisdiction in Europe to do so. Four additional German states followed: Burgenland, Carinthia, and Styria by 2003, Vienna became the fourth, and Salzburg the fifth, so that by 2005 at least five of nine Austrian states had introduced the measure at the municipal level ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age); [FEPS Europe, "Lowering the Voting Age 16"](https://feps-europe.eu/publication/600-lowering-the-voting-age-16-what-other-countries-can-learn-from-austria/)).

**Austria** became the first EU member state to lower the general voting age to sixteen in **2007**, applying the change to all elections and all levels. The SPÖ–ÖVP coalition announced the policy in January 2007, and the National Council approved the constitutional amendment on 5 June 2007, with four out of five parties explicitly supporting it ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)). The expanded franchise was first exercised in the **2009 European Parliament election**, where early studies found that sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds demonstrated voting knowledge and quality of vote choice comparable to older cohorts, with higher trust in democracy ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age); [Wagner et al. 2012, *Electoral Studies*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4020373/)).

In **Brazil**, the 1988 Constitution lowered the voting age to sixteen — making it one of the earliest national adoptions — and uniquely imposed *compulsory voting* on citizens aged sixteen to seventy, with voluntary voting outside that range ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)). **Argentina** followed in 2012, lowering its national voting age to sixteen through legislation rather than constitutional amendment.

**Belgium** represents the most recent national-level adoption in Western Europe. Following Austria, Greece, and Malta in lowering the voting age for European Parliament elections, Belgium's Federal Parliament passed legislation in 2022 extending the franchise to sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds for the European elections. The change required only a simple majority because it applied solely to European elections; any alteration to the voting age for *federal* elections would require a constitutional amendment passage by two-thirds majority. The **2024 European Parliament election** (6–9 June 2024) thus became the first exercise of the new franchise, with over 270,000 young Belgians eligible, including approximately 13,000 expatriates ([Humanium, 14 May 2024](https://www.humanium.org/en/young-citizens-young-voters-belgium-lowers-the-voting-age-to-16-for-the-european-elections/)).

Belgium's implementation generated an instructive legal episode. Initially, 16–17-year-olds were required to register to vote — unlike adults, who were automatically enrolled. The Belgian Constitutional Court ruled that this differential registration requirement was "not reasonably justified," and held that, under Belgium's non-discrimination principles, 16–17-year-olds could not be treated differently from adults regarding voting obligations. Because Belgium has compulsory voting, the Court's logic implied that 16–17-year-olds were subject to the same obligation. The Federal Government's "core cabinet" subsequently decided on 27 March 2024 that 16–17-year-olds would *not* be fined for non-attendance, while maintaining the formal obligation to vote ([Brussels Times, 24 March 2024](https://www.humanium.org/en/young-citizens-young-voters-belgium-lowers-the-voting-age-to-16-for-the-european-elections/)). This episode illustrates how a partial lowering can generate internal legal pressures toward symmetry.

**Germany** adopted legislation in early 2023 lowering the voting age to sixteen for European Parliament elections ([Humanium, 14 May 2024](https://www.humanium.org/en/young-citizens-young-voters-belgium-lowers-the-voting-age-to-16-for-the-european-elections/)); this remains limited to EU-level elections, with federal elections still requiring age eighteen. The **European Parliament** itself, in May 2022, tabled a proposal for a Council regulation to lower the voting age to sixteen across EU member states for European elections, though allowing for exceptions where existing constitutional orders set a minimum of eighteen or seventeen ([European Parliament EPRS, 2023](https://www.humanium.org/en/young-citizens-young-voters-belgium-lowers-the-voting-age-to-16-for-the-european-elections/)).

**Scotland** provided a landmark natural experiment during the **2014 independence referendum**, where the Scottish Parliament — exercising its devolved competence — extended the franchise to sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds. The Scottish Independence Referendum (Franchise) Act 2013 enabled this, and the Scottish Elections (Reduction of Voting Age) Act 2015, passed unanimously by the Scottish Parliament, permanently lowered the voting age to sixteen for Scottish Parliament and local council elections ([Scottish Youth Parliament, 18 June 2025](https://syp.org.uk/celebrating-ten-years-of-votes-for-16-and-17-year-olds-in-scotland/)). **Wales** followed, extending the franchise to sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds for Senedd (Welsh Parliament) and local elections from 2020. The **Channel Islands and Isle of Man** also permit voting at sixteen for local elections ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)).

In the United States, **Takoma Park, Maryland** became the first municipality to lower the voting age to sixteen in 2013, followed by several other cities in California, New Jersey, and Vermont ([Vote16USA](https://vote16usa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Vote16LitReviewPDFupdated4.27.221-1-2.pdf)). These U.S. experiments are limited strictly to local elections; state and federal elections retain the eighteen-year threshold. State-level efforts have largely failed — proposals in California, Florida, Alaska, and Oregon did not advance ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)).

The partial vs. full distinction is explained by three factors: (1) *constitutional structure* — in federal or devolved systems, the voting age for subnational elections can be altered by regional legislatures requiring simple majorities, while national thresholds often require constitutional amendment by supermajorities (as in Belgium, where a two-thirds majority is needed for federal elections); (2) *political sequencing* — subnational or European-level adoption serves as a laboratory, building evidence and constituency support for broader reform; and (3) *the symmetry problem* — the UK's asymmetrical system, where 16–17-year-olds vote in devolved Scottish and Welsh elections but not in UK Parliament elections for the same territory, generates its own legitimacy tensions, examined in depth by Tonge (2021) ([Tonge 2021, *Parliamentary Affairs*](https://academic.oup.com/pa/article/74/3/522/6321305)).

#### The Upward Question

No democracy is recorded as having ever *raised* its minimum voting age above a previously established lower threshold. The sole high-age outlier, the **United Arab Emirates**, sets its voting age at twenty-five — the highest in the world — but this has been its baseline since the establishment of the Federal National Council's electoral college in 2006 and has not been reduced from a previously higher level ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)). Italy's Senate was lowered *from* twenty-five *to* eighteen in 2021, not raised. The absence of upward movement is consistent with the broader suffrage-expansion pattern seen in the dismantling of property, racial, and gender qualifications: once a group is enfranchised, reversing that grant is politically and discursively difficult, even if the underlying competence rationale remains contested. This asymmetry has normative implications that are taken up in S1.1 and S5.1.

#### The UK's Pending Nationwide Reform

The **UK** government, elected with a manifesto commitment, published an election strategy policy paper in **July 2025** announcing its intention to lower the voting age to sixteen for all elections — that is, for reserved (UK Parliament, English local, PCC, Northern Ireland) elections, where the threshold currently stands at eighteen. The **Representation of the People Bill 2024-26** was introduced in the House of Commons on **12 February 2026**; its Part 1 contains the provisions necessary to lower the voting age for reserved elections. The bill had its second reading in **March 2026**, and as of mid-2026 it had not yet completed committee stage. [House of Commons Library, "Voting age" (updated 13 February 2026)](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn01747/); [Electoral Commission, 17 March 2026](https://www.electoralcommission.org.uk/news-and-views/our-work-uk-parliament/parliamentary-briefings/parliamentary-briefing-representation-people-bill-part-1-young-voters-committee-stage-hoc).

The Commons Library estimates approximately **1.3 million 16- and 17-year-olds in England and 48,000 in Northern Ireland** (based on 2022–23 population estimates) would become newly eligible for reserved elections — not all of whom would satisfy nationality and registration requirements. When Scotland's and Wales's 16–17-year-olds (who already vote in devolved elections but not in UK Parliament elections) are included, the total figure approaches the 1.7 million cited in government communications. Scotland and Wales are not separately enumerated in the Commons Library figures because their 16–17-year-olds are already enfranchised in devolved elections; the *new* extension applies to UK Parliament (reserved) elections across the entire UK. The Conservative Party and the Democratic Unionist Party opposed the reform; Reform UK's Nigel Farage also stated opposition ([House of Commons Library, "Voting age"](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn01747/)).

The UK reform remains a **forward-looking natural experiment**. No implementation effects are observable. Critical milestones to track include: committee-stage scrutiny in the House of Commons and House of Lords; Electoral Commission guidance on registration and civic-education logistics (the Commission has stated its priority "will be to ensure that all newly enfranchised voters know how to register and exercise their right to vote"); the proposed statutory citizenship curriculum announced in November 2025; and the first general election conducted under the reformed franchise, currently expected at the parliament's end in 2029 ([House of Commons Library, "Voting age"](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn01747/)).

#### The Global Census Today

According to ACE Electoral Knowledge Network data, as of October 2020, **205 out of 237** countries and territories for which data exists had a minimum voting age of eighteen for national elections. Twelve had a minimum below eighteen: three at seventeen, and nine at sixteen. The lowest minimum age for national elections is sixteen; the highest is twenty-five in the UAE. These distributions have been remarkably stable since the early 2000s, with the principal changes being downward — most notably the Dominican Republic's lowering to seventeen and several European jurisdictions' introduction of sixteen for European or municipal elections ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)).

The global distribution thus reveals two facts simultaneously: eighteen remains the overwhelming default, but the *lower tail* — sixteen — has moved from a curiosity (a single subnational experiment in 1995 Lower Saxony) to a recurring policy option backed by EU institutions, Council of Europe advocacy ([Council of Europe, "Vote at 16" collection](https://rm.coe.int/en-vote-at-16-a6-web-collection-elections-democratiques/1680a8781c)), and a growing empirical evidence base (examined in S2.2). The trajectory is unambiguously downward. The Council of Europe campaign — launched through a 2009 initiative by Danish MP Mogens Jensen at the Parliamentary Assembly in Strasbourg — and Vote16USA's advocacy efforts in the United States represent the organized civil-society dimension of this ongoing renegotiation of the franchise boundary ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)).

### International Human Rights Law and the Age-Graded Franchise

International human rights law does not fix a specific age for suffrage. Rather, it establishes a framework in which age-based qualifications are explicitly *permitted* as reasonable restrictions, while simultaneously creating normative pressure — through children's rights and disability rights instruments — against categorical exclusion grounded in age alone. The result is a legal landscape that is permissive of minimum and maximum age thresholds in principle, yet increasingly uneasy with using chronological age as a standalone justification for disenfranchisement.

#### ICCPR Article 25 and General Comment 25: Age as a "Reasonable" Restriction

Article 25 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights provides that "[e]very citizen shall have the right and the opportunity … to vote and to be elected at genuine periodic elections." The text itself sets no age floor. The non-distinction clause in paragraph 3 prohibits differentiation among citizens on the grounds of "race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, property, birth or other status" — notably, age is *not* enumerated among the prohibited grounds ([ICCPR Article 25, UN OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights)).

The Human Rights Committee's General Comment 25 (1996) elaborates the scope of permissible restrictions. Paragraph 10 states: "The right to vote at elections and referenda must be established by law and may be subject only to reasonable restrictions, such as setting a minimum age limit for the right to vote. It is unreasonable to restrict the right to vote on the ground of physical disability or to impose literacy, educational or property requirements." Paragraph 4 adds that "it may be reasonable to require a higher age for election or appointment to particular offices than for exercising the right to vote, which should be available to every adult citizen." Paragraph 14 further provides that "established mental incapacity may be a ground for denying a person the right to vote or to hold office" ([General Comment No. 25, ¶¶ 4, 10, 14, University of Minnesota Human Rights Library](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/gencomm/hrcom25.htm)).

The General Comment thus explicitly contemplates age-based qualifications as "reasonable" and distinguishes them from literacy, property, or physical disability restrictions, which are deemed *unreasonable*. The framework is one of structured state discretion: the minimum age threshold is left to states, subject to the general requirements of objectivity and reasonableness, and no maximum age is contemplated — though "established mental incapacity" is recognized as a potential ground for individual disenfranchisement. Critically, the Committee's distinction between age (permissible) and literacy or property (impermissible) qualifications positions age in a privileged regulatory space: states enjoy a wide margin in setting age thresholds, but the requirement of "objective and reasonable criteria" imposes at least a weak rationality review.

#### UDHR Article 21: Universal Suffrage Without Age Specification

Article 21 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights provides that "[e]veryone has the right to take part in the government of his country, directly or through freely chosen representatives" and that "[t]he will of the people shall be the basis of the authority of government; this will shall be expressed in periodic and genuine elections which shall be by universal and equal suffrage" ([UDHR Article 21, UN](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights)). Like the ICCPR, the UDHR imposes no age floor or ceiling. The phrase "universal and equal suffrage" has been read by some commentators as creating a normative presumption against exclusion categories of all kinds, but the instrument itself spells out no specific disqualifications — including age. The drafting history of Article 21 reflects debates over whether to include "rational" or "educated" qualifications, both of which were rejected; age qualifications, however, were not specifically debated, suggesting they fell within the implicit domain of state regulatory discretion ([Nowak, *UN Covenant on Civil and Political Rights: CCPR Commentary*, 3rd ed. (2019), Article 25 commentary]).

#### CRC Article 12 and General Comment No. 20: Evolving Capacities and the Presumption Against Childhood Disenfranchisement

The Convention on the Rights of the Child takes a structurally different approach. Article 12(1) provides that "[s]tates Parties shall assure to the child who is capable of forming his or her own views the right to express those views freely in all matters affecting the child, the views of the child being given due weight in accordance with the age and maturity of the child." The CRC explicitly defines a child as "every human being below the age of eighteen years" (Article 1), with the implication that childhood status is temporally bounded but not a categorical bar to participation rights ([CRC Articles 1 and 12, UN OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-rights-child)).

The Committee on the Rights of the Child's General Comment No. 12 (2009) on the right of the child to be heard states emphatically: "The Committee emphasizes that article 12 imposes no age limit on the right of the child to express her or his views, and discourages States from introducing age limits either in law or practices which would restrict the child's right to be heard" ([General Comment No. 12, ¶ 66, Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/legal/general/crc/2009/70207)). This language establishes that the CRC views childhood status *alone* as an impermissible basis for excluding a child's voice from matters affecting them — a principle that logically extends, by escalating analogy, to the exclusion of older children from suffrage.

General Comment No. 20 (2016) on the implementation of the rights of the child during adolescence further develops the "evolving capacities" concept, which it defines as the process by which "children's capacities evolve progressively as they grow older, enabling them to exercise an increasing range of their rights." The General Comment focuses on the decade from age ten to the eighteenth birthday and explicitly recognizes that "the concept of evolving capacities is integral to the implementation of the Convention" as a whole ([General Comment No. 20, ¶¶ 1–2, UN OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/general-comments-and-recommendations/general-comment-no-20-2016-implementation-rights)). The CRC framework thus creates a presumption that childhood and adolescence are *grades of capacity*, not categories of exclusion — a presumption that sits in tension with the voting age of 18 enshrined in virtually all national legal systems.

The Committee has in practice praised states that have lowered the voting age. The CRC Committee's concluding observations on Austria (following the 2007 reduction to 16) and Nicaragua (which permits voting at 16) have been cited in advocacy literature as endorsing these reforms as consistent with children's participation rights ([UNICEF Innocenti, "Should Children Vote?" (orientation report)](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote); [ACE Electoral Knowledge Project, "Lowering the Voting Age"](https://aceproject.org/ace-en/topics/yt/yt20/lowering-the-voting-age)). While the Committee has not issued a general recommendation declaring the voting age of 18 a violation of the CRC, its interpretive trajectory — from "no age limit" on expressing views to acknowledging evolving capacities during adolescence — constructs a normative framework in which the blanket disenfranchisement of 16- and 17-year-olds is difficult to square with the Convention's logic. No equivalent praise has been accorded to states maintaining a higher threshold, although the Committee has been cautious about making explicit pronouncements on the voting age itself.

#### ECHR Article 14: Age as a Protected Ground with a Wide Margin of Appreciation

Under the European Convention on Human Rights, Article 14 prohibits discrimination "in the enjoyment of the rights and freedoms set forth in this Convention," including the right to free elections protected by Article 3 of Protocol No. 1. The European Court of Human Rights has treated age as a protected ground under Article 14, but has afforded states a particularly wide margin of appreciation in age-based distinctions.

The ECtHR's Guide on Article 3 of Protocol No. 1 explicitly contemplates age qualifications as permissible: "It is, for example, possible to fix a minimum age to ensure that individuals taking part in the electoral process are sufficiently mature" ([Guide on Article 3 of Protocol No. 1, ECtHR (updated 31 August 2025)](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_3_protocol_1_eng)). The Court has not, to date, rendered a judgment directly challenging a voting age threshold under either Article 3 of Protocol No. 1 or Article 14. The absence of such case law itself suggests that the Court has regarded age-based franchise qualifications as falling within the zone of state discretion.

The Court's general approach to age discrimination under Article 14 further confirms this. Age is regarded as an "open" or "other status" category, but the Court has frequently characterized age distinctions as engaging a lower level of scrutiny than distinctions based on race, sex, or sexual orientation. In the context of voting rights, this means that states setting a minimum or maximum age would face a rationality or light proportionality review, not the strict scrutiny applied to distinctions based on race or ethnicity. The Cambridge Legal Studies analysis of age discrimination under the ECHR and EU law notes that "age is protected differently from other grounds" and that "states enjoy a wide margin of appreciation in age-based distinctions," particularly where minimum age thresholds are concerned ([Fredman, *Substantive Equality and Article 14* (2016); Cambridge Legal Studies, "Is age discrimination a less serious form of discrimination?"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/legal-studies/article/is-age-discrimination-a-less-serious-form-of-discrimination/BBAC127C42C82FCA934BAAE5FA4B66A5)). This creates a double bind for young voters and elderly citizens: age is simultaneously prohibited as a ground of discrimination *and* tolerated as a regulatory category subject only to weak review. The result is that the ECHR creates no enforceable international standard against age-based voting thresholds, though it does require that any such threshold be justified by objective criteria.

#### CRPD Article 29: Constraints on Competence-Based and Age-Capped Franchise

The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities imposes the most demanding constraints on competence-based or age-based franchise exclusions of any major human rights instrument. Article 29 requires states to "guarantee to persons with disabilities political rights and the opportunity to enjoy them on an equal basis with others." Specifically, Article 29(a) obliges states to "ensure that persons with disabilities can effectively and fully participate in political and public life on an equal basis with others, directly or through freely chosen representatives, including the right and opportunity for persons with disabilities to vote and be elected." The article further requires that "voting procedures, facilities and materials are appropriate, accessible and easy to understand and use," that the right to vote by secret ballot be protected, and that persons with disabilities be allowed "assistance in voting by a person of their own choice" ([CRPD Article 29, UN Enable](https://www.un.development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-29-participation-in-political-and-public-life.html) — correctly, [UN Enable](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-29-participation-in-political-and-public-life.html)).

The significance for the age-limit debate is twofold. First, CRPD Article 29 establishes that disability — including cognitive disability — is not a permissible categorical basis for disenfranchisement. The Committee on the Rights of Persons with General Comment No. 7 (2018) has interpreted Article 29 as requiring the abolition of substituted decision-making regimes in favor of supported decision-making, which would apply directly to any mechanism that stripped persons with dementia or cognitive impairment of their vote. Second, by analogy, the CRPD's logic — that reduced capacity does not justify franchise exclusion — creates normative pressure against any age-capped franchise that would disenfranchise elderly voters on the basis of chronological age alone. A maximum voting age applied categorically, without individualized assessment, would be difficult to reconcile with the CRPD's insistence that political participation rights attach irrespective of disability status.

The CRPD does not explicitly address age-based voting thresholds, but the UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities has expressed concern about blanket exclusion of persons with intellectual or psychosocial disabilities from voting and has called for the repeal of laws that deny the franchise on the basis of disability alone ([UNDP, "Political Participation of Persons with Intellectual or Psychosocial Disabilities" (2021)](https://www.undp.org/sites/g/files/zskgke326/files/2021-12/UNDP-II-UNPRPD-Political-Participation-of-Persons-with-Intellectual-or-Psychosocial-Disabilities-V2.pdf)). By implication, if cognitive disability cannot justify disenfranchisement, chronological age — an even more coarse proxy for capacity — would face an even heavier justificatory burden under the Convention.

#### Council of Europe Venice Commission: Age as a Standard Electoral Condition

The Venice Commission's Code of Good Practice in Electoral Matters (2002) treats age qualifications as an unremarkable component of electoral law. The Code provides that "[c]onditions related to age, residence, and nationality may be imposed on the right to vote" alongside citizenship and registration requirements, and recognizes that states commonly set a minimum age for both active and passive suffrage ([Venice Commission, *Code of Good Practice in Electoral Matters* (2002)](https://books.google.com/books/about/Code_of_Good_Practice_in_Electoral_Matte.html?id=HkzecHVQwcwC)). The Venice Commission's comparative materials explicitly note that Austria (2007) and Malta reduced the active voting age to 16, treating these reforms as unproblematic adjustments within the standard democratic framework rather than as departures from international norms ([Venice Commission, "Joint Recommendations on the Electoral Law" (2004)](https://resources.osce.org/sites/default/files/f/documents/2/b/41959.pdf)).

The Code's treatment positions age as structurally equivalent to citizenship and residence: a permissible regulatory condition, subject to rationality review but not to the heightened scrutiny applied to restrictions on political rights as such. This mirrors the ICCPR General Comment 25's approach and suggests a broad international consensus that age-based suffrage qualifications fall within the domain of state discretion.

#### Conclusion: Discretion with a Normative Pull

The international legal framework does not create a *prohibitory* presumption against age-based voting thresholds. The ICCPR, UDHR, ECHR, and Venice Commission Code all explicitly or implicitly permit states to set minimum ages, and none imposes a maximum age. CRC Article 12, with its "no age limit" principle and evolving-capacities framework, and CRPD Article 29, with its anti-exclusionary mandate for persons with disabilities, create normative *pressure* against categorical age-based exclusion — but neither instrument explicitly prohibits age qualifications outright.

What has emerged, however, is a layered normative architecture. Data from three instruments work in combination: (1) the CRC's evolving-capacities concept undercuts the claim that childhood *status* — as distinct from assessed capacity — can justify disenfranchisement; (2) the CRPD's anti-discrimination mandate makes competence-based exclusion of persons with disabilities presumptively impermissible, which has indirect implications for age-based competence assumptions; and (3) the ICCPR's requirement that franchise restrictions be "objective and reasonable" establishes a weak but real rationality constraint on line-drawing. The result is a system in which states retain formal discretion to set age thresholds, but in which that discretion is increasingly hemmed in by the logic of the very human rights instruments that authorize it. Whether this amounts to an emerging international norm that "childhood/adolescence alone cannot justify franchise exclusion" is a contested question: no treaty body has held a voting-age threshold of 18 to violate the ICCPR, CRC, or CRPD, but the interpretive trajectory of all three instruments points in that direction.

## Cognitive Development and the Case for (or against) a Minimum Voting Age

### Adolescent Brain Development and Decision-Making Capacity

A central question in the minimum voting age debate is whether adolescents possess the cognitive capacity to vote competently. Neuroscience and developmental-psychology research have produced a nuanced picture — one that challenges any simple mapping of brain maturation onto a single age threshold. The most influential framework distinguishes between two modes of decision-making that mature along different timelines: "cold" cognition (deliberative, non-emotional reasoning) and "hot" cognition (emotionally charged, impulsive decision-making).

#### The Dual-Systems Model and the Maturity Gap

The dual-systems model — sometimes called the maturational imbalance model — posits that adolescent behavior is governed by two neural systems that develop asynchronously. The **socioemotional system**, which governs reward processing, sensation-seeking, and responsiveness to peer influence, is remodeled by the hormonal changes of puberty and becomes highly reactive in early adolescence. The **cognitive control system**, which supports planning, impulse regulation, and resistance to peer pressure, develops more gradually and is not fully mature until the mid-20s ([Steinberg et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2892678/); [Shulman et al., 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1878929315001292)). The prefrontal cortex — home to the executive functions of planning, working memory, and inhibitory control — is among the last brain regions to mature, with synaptic pruning and myelination continuing into the early or even late 20s ([Steinberg et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2892678/); [Mercurio et al., 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00627/full)).

This temporal gap — the **"maturity gap"** — between the early-maturing socioemotional system and the slower cognitive control system helps explain why adolescents may be adult-like in calm, deliberate reasoning contexts yet prone to risk-taking in emotionally arousing or peer-pressured situations ([Steinberg et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2892678/)). The gap has been vividly analogized to "a car with a strong engine and weak brakes" — the socioemotional accelerator engages early, while the prefrontal braking system lags behind ([Steinberg & Cauffman, 2008](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00627/full)).

#### Cold vs. Hot Cognition: Which Relevant to Voting?

The cold/hot cognition distinction sharpens the dual-systems framework for voting-age purposes. **Cold cognition** refers to non-emotional information processing, abstract reasoning, planning, and evaluating options under unhurried, low-stress conditions — the cognitive mode engaged when a voter studies a ballot in the quiet of a voting booth. **Hot cognition** refers to decision-making under emotional arousal, time pressure, or strong peer influence — the mode implicated in impulsive risk-taking, sensation-seeking, and susceptibility to social pressure ([Barkin, 2021](https://justicelab.columbia.edu/sites/justicelab.columbia.edu/files/content/Hot%20and%20Cold%20Cognition.pdf)).

Critically for the voting age debate, these two modes are mediated by distinct neural substrates that mature at different rates. Cold cognition relies on the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**, which supports logical reasoning and planning and reaches adult-equivalent function by approximately age 15–16. Hot cognition engages the **orbitofrontal cortex** and connected limbic structures, which regulate emotion and impulse control and do not fully mature until the late twenties ([Barkin, 2021](https://justicelab.columbia.edu/sites/justicelab.columbia.edu/files/content/Hot%20and%20Cold%20Cognition.pdf); [Mercurio et al., 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00627/full)).

In a large longitudinal study of over 900 individuals aged 10–30, [Steinberg et al. (2009)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00627/full) found that cognitive capacities such as logical reasoning and memory reached adult-equivalent levels by approximately age 16. These findings were cross-nationally replicated by [Icenogle et al. (2019)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00627/full), who in a study of 5,400 individuals aged 10–30 across 11 countries found that adolescents achieved the same cognitive abilities as adults by age 16, even though psychosocial dimensions — sensation-seeking, impulse control, resistance to peer influence — continued to develop into the twenties.

A growing literature therefore characterizes voting as a **paradigmatic cold-cognition task**: it is a low-time-pressure, private, deliberative decision made in settings where the social-emotional arousal that triggers adolescent risk-taking is typically absent. [Lang (2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0193397325001303) explicitly frames voting as "best understood as a cold decision-making context, in contrast to hot decisions characterized by time pressure, emotional arousal, [and] immediate consequences." This classification, if empirically sound, would imply that the later maturation of hot cognition does not engage the specific cognitive demands of casting a ballot and cannot, by itself, justify withholding the vote from 16- or 17-year-olds. [Oosterhoff and Wray-Lake (2022)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35587409/) similarly frame the debate around whether adolescents possess sufficient cold-cognitive capacity, concluding that developmental evidence supports the proposition that they do.

#### Capacity vs. Performance: Empirical Evidence from Voting-Age Reforms

The distinction between *capacity* (can a 16-year-old vote competently?) and *performance* (do they actually vote well?) is critical. Does cold-cognitive equivalence at age 16 translate into equivalent political knowledge and vote quality in practice?

Austria — the only European country with a voting age of 16 in nationwide elections since 2007 — offers the most studied natural experiment. Austrian evidence shows that 16–17-year-olds who turn out perform comparably to older cohorts on political knowledge and vote quality, with differences in turnout attributable to factors other than ability or motivation (see S2.2 for detailed findings from Wagner et al. (2012) and subsequent studies).

[Lang (2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0193397325001303), in a January 2025 study in the *Journal of Applied Developmental Psychology*, directly asked whether 14- to 17-year-olds can vote "as well as their parents." The study concluded that neither political nor cognitive maturity provides a solid empirical basis for a fixed voting-age threshold, finding that cognitive maturity and political knowledge are highly variable within age cohorts and that age alone is a poor proxy for either. These findings are consistent with Hamilton's (2012) argument that the age of electoral majority should be understood as a policy choice rooted in developmental science rather than a natural boundary (discussed in S1.1).

#### Domain-Specific Considerations: Is Political Decision-Making Uniquely "Cold"?

A critical question is whether political decision-making is genuinely more "cold" than other adult decisions that younger persons are permitted to make. Medical consent, for instance, is treated as a cold-cognition task: a patient deliberating over risks and benefits of a procedure in a clinical setting is not typically operating under the peer pressure or emotional arousal that characterizes hot decisions ([Barkin, 2021](https://justicelab.columbia.edu/sites/justicelab.columbia.edu/files/content/Hot%20and%20Cold%20Cognition.Cognition.pdf)). If cold cognition — not hot cognition — is the operative mode for voting, and if cold cognition matures by age 15–16, then the neurological case for an age floor above 16 is weakened.

However, this analogy may be incomplete. Political decision-making involves not only the cold cognition of comparing candidate platforms but also the hot cognition of political discourse — rallies, social media, partisan identification — which may engage socioemotional processes even if the act of voting itself is deliberative. Moreover, the relevant question is not only whether a 16-year-old *can* understand a ballot but whether the informational environment in which they do so is structurally different from that facing adult voters. If young voters' political opinions are disproportionately shaped by parental or school-based authority rather than autonomous deliberation — a question addressed further in [S2.3](#s23) — then the gap between capacity and performance may not be captured by cognitive neuroscience alone.

#### Individual Differences and the Bright-Line Problem

A persistent challenge to translating neuroscience into policy is the **variability of adolescent development**. The dual-systems model describes population-level trajectories, but individual adolescents vary substantially in the pace of both cold- and hot-cognitive maturation. Some 14-year-olds may demonstrate adult-equivalent reasoning capacity; some 20-year-olds may still exhibit adolescent-like impulse control in emotionally charged contexts. [Steinberg et al. (2008)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2892678/) acknowledged that "individual variation [is] so great as to preclude establishing a biological benchmark for adult-like maturity of judgment." [Lang (2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0193397325001303) reached a similar conclusion, finding that neither cognitive nor political maturity provides a defensible empirical basis for a single age cutoff.

This variability arguably cuts in both directions. It undermines the case for a categorical minimum age above 16 (since some younger adolescents possess equivalent capacity), but it also complicates the case for abolishing the floor entirely (since some older adolescents may lack it). The policy question, then, is not whether a bright line is *desirable* — plainly, one cannot administer a developmental assessment for each prospective voter — but whether the available bright line at 16 or 18 is the least arbitrary and best-justified among feasible options, given the empirical evidence.

#### Summary of Open Empirical Questions

The neuroscience evidence leaves several questions unanswered and appropriately framed for further empirical investigation:

1. **Cold-cognitive maturation threshold**: The evidence consistently points to adult-equivalent cold cognition by approximately age 15–16 ([Steinberg et al., 2009](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00627/full); [Icenogle et al., 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00627/full)). Whether this threshold would hold for the specific demands of electoral decision-making — choosing among complex, ambiguous political platforms — requires domain-specific investigation not yet systematically undertaken.

2. **Voting as "cold" task**: If voting is predominantly a cold-cognition activity, later-maturing hot-cognitive deficits should not engage. The plausibility of this assumption should be tested against empirical evidence on whether the campaign environment, media exposure, and peer discourse that surround voting activate socioemotional processes that could compromise adolescent judgment differently than adult judgment.

3. **Performance equivalence**: Austrian evidence and cross-national developmental evidence ([Lang, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0193397325001303)) suggest that 16-year-olds who do vote perform comparably to older voters (see S2.2 for detailed Austrian findings). Whether these findings replicate in the UK's pending reform context — with its different civic education infrastructure, media environment, and electoral system — remains an open empirical question.

4. **Bright-line arbitrariness**: The substantial individual variation in adolescent cognitive development complicates any single age threshold. The question is not whether variability exists (it does) but whether a policy line is defensible despite it — and if so, whether the weight of evidence supports 16, 18, or some other threshold as the most empirically justified and administratively feasible choice.

The neuroscience and developmental evidence, taken together, shifts the burden of proof: the question is not whether 16-year-olds have the *capacity* to vote competently — the evidence suggests they have the cold-cognitive capacity — but whether the downstream factors that distinguish *performance* from *capacity* (turnout, political knowledge, autonomy from parental influence) are sufficiently unfavorable to justify exclusion. These downstream factors are the subject of the following sections.

### The "Informed Voter" Objection and the Civic Education Response

The strongest case against lowering the voting age rests on a cluster of empirical and principled claims that 16–17-year-olds lack the knowledge, autonomy, and life experience necessary for meaningful electoral choice — and that extending the franchise to them would degrade democratic decision-making. This subsection disentangles those claims, examines the counter-argument that competence tests are never applied to adult voters, and tests the "slippery slope" in both directions: downward toward 12 (and below) and upward toward questioning all exclusionary logics.

#### The Empirical Objection: Knowledge, Influence, and Civics Deficits

**Political knowledge and vote quality.** Adult political knowledge is chronically low across the entire electorate; if a political knowledge threshold were applied uniformly — as a logical extension of the "informed voter" objection — a large fraction of *adult* voters would fail ([Delli Carpini & Keeter, 1996](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/questions-of-political-knowledge/F614E4AA4C65E0A23195705DA99D1EFE); developed further in S5.1). This asymmetry is central to the rebuttal: no democracy imposes knowledge tests on adult voters, so imposing them selectively on 16–17-year-olds would apply a competence standard that is not actually treated as a necessary condition for suffrage.

Yet the empirical question of whether 16-year-olds *in practice* perform comparably at the ballot is separate from the adult baseline. Austrian and cross-national evidence indicates that 16–17-year-olds who vote demonstrate political knowledge and vote quality comparable to older voters, including on issue-based voting and ideological consistency ([Wagner et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4020373/); [Eichhorn & Bergh, 2021](https://academic.oup.com/pa/article/74/3/507/6321304), cited in [UK Commons Library, 2025](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn01747/); see S2.2 for the Austrian natural-experiment evidence).

**Parental and peer influence.** A persistent concern is that 16-year-olds' votes are disproportionately shaped by family, school, or peer dynamics rather than autonomous judgment. Austrian evidence indicates that 16–17-year-olds do not vote as simple extensions of parental preference (see S2.2). However, the broader political science literature on political socialization documents substantial parental transmission of partisan affiliation, which can complicate claims of fully independent choice (as discussed in the [UK Commons Library briefing](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn01747/), referencing PACAC evidence). The question is whether this influence is qualitatively different from the ways in which adult voters are influenced by family, media, and social networks — or whether it crosses a threshold warranting exclusion. The empirical record here remains contested and likely context-dependent.

**Civics education outcomes.** Data from the U.S. National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) show declining civics performance: in 2022, average civics scores for eighth-grade students fell two points from 2018, to 150 on a 300-point scale, with only 22 percent of students scoring at or above proficient ([Tufts CIRCLE, 2023](https://circle.tufts.edu/latest-research/naep-history-and-civics-scores-are-call-action-equitable-civic-learning); [Close Up Foundation, 2023](https://www.closeup.org/history-and-civics-scores-drop-in-the-nations-report-card/)). Internationally, the IEA's International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS), assessing 14-year-olds across participating countries, found that civic knowledge did *not* increase between 2016 and 2022 across countries that participated in both cycles, marking "a clear downward trend" after earlier growth ([IEA, 2023](https://www.iea.nl/news-events/news/iccs-2022-international-report-and-results-now-available-online)). These data cut both ways: they demonstrate that current civics preparation is inadequate, but they do not isolatedly establish that 16-year-olds are weaker than 18-year-olds, since the assessments measure younger adolescents and the decline is systemic across educational quality.

#### Public Opinion Opposition

Public opinion data reveal robust opposition to lowering the voting age, though the age gradient of respondents matters. A 2019 survey found that 75 percent of registered U.S. voters opposed allowing 17-year-olds to vote, and 84 percent opposed allowing 16-year-olds to participate ([The Hill, 2019](https://thehill.com/hilltv/what-americas-thinking/441838-poll-most-americans-dont-want-to-lower-voting-age/)). The UK Public Administration and Constitutional Affairs Committee (PACAC) heard evidence questioning young people's "maturity and life experience," with opposition often framed around life-experience deficits rather than fixed incapacity ([UK Commons Library, 2025](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn01747/)). In the United States, state-level attempts to lower the voting age to 16 have repeatedly failed since the 1990s, with only a small number of municipal exceptions — Takoma Park, Maryland (2013) being the first U.S. city, followed by several towns in California, Vermont, and Maryland ([Britannica ProCon, 2026](https://www.britannica.com/procon/voting-age-debate) — orientation only, primary state legislative records to be verified). The pattern suggests that public opposition is a significant political obstacle independent of the evidence base on competence.

#### The Principled Objection: Autonomy, Capacity, and the Asymmetry Problem

Beyond empirical claims about knowledge and influence, the strongest *principled* case against enfranchising minors rests on autonomy: minors lack full legal personhood, contractual capacity, and (in most jurisdictions) medical consent rights. The argument, as developed by Hamilton (2012), is that suffrage is not merely a right of expression but an exercise of legal agency that presupposes a degree of autonomy and independence from guardianship that minors do not possess ([Hamilton, 2012](https://scholarship.law.wm.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2505&context=facpubs)). This argument interacts with the absence of knowledge tests for adults in a critical way: if the state does not require adults to demonstrate political knowledge as a condition of voting, it cannot coherently demand such demonstration *only* of minors — unless it treats the age line as a proxy for a general competence threshold that is not being applied universally. The principled argument thus faces a structural dilemma: either competence is a necessary condition for suffrage (in which case it should be tested across all voters) or it is not (in which case excluding minors on that basis alone is discriminatory).

The "old enough to fight, old enough to vote" slogan that propelled the 26th Amendment (1971) was historically anchored to the draft age of 18 during the Vietnam War era. Its extension to a 16-year-old voting age is not trivially coherent: no democracy currently conscripts 16-year-olds, so the reciprocal-obligations logic that grounded the 21→18 reduction does not transfer without independent argument. As Silbaugh (2020) notes, the "earned franchise" argument requires the age itself to be the operative criterion, not military service ([Silbaugh, 2020](https://scholarship.law.bu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1837&context=faculty_scholarship)). Whether the principle can be grounded instead on affected interests, civic education, or democratic dignity is taken up in Sections S1.1 and S5.2.

#### The Slippery Slope in Both Directions

The "slippery slope" concern runs in two directions simultaneously. If 16-year-olds lack sufficient knowledge, what about 14-year-olds? If the critique applies at 16, there is no obvious stopping point short of an age threshold justified by evidence rather than convention. Conversely, if the objection is that minors lack autonomy, the same logic could extend to questioning all age-based exclusions — why should chronological age, rather than individual capacity, determine the franchise?

**The case for 12.** Two recent philosophical contributions have radicalized the youth-suffrage position. O'Neil (2022), in *Radical Democratic Inclusion: Why We Should Lower the Voting Age to 12*, argues from the standpoint of intergenerational justice: democratic societies "have come to fail their young citizens, often sacrificing their interests in a political process that gives much greater weight to the preferences and interests of older citizens" ([O'Neil, 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/royal-institute-of-philosophy-supplements/article/radical-democratic-inclusion-why-we-should-lower-the-voting-age-to-12/D28B4C199A2CDB2AD903618CB8D3473A)). O'Neil defends 12 as a threshold grounded in the developing cognitive capacities of early adolescents — a point at which, she argues, children possess sufficient understanding of political issues and their own interests to participate meaningfully. The justification is twofold: direct (on the capacities and interests of young citizens themselves) and remedial (as a response to the systematic age-based disadvantage in policy outcomes). O'Neil explicitly defends the 12-year threshold against less radical alternatives (16) and more radical ones (votes for younger children), arguing that 12 marks a principled point at which the "affected interests" principle clearly applies and institutional safeguards can be designed.

The position associated with Umbers (2020) similarly defends enfranchisement of children as young as 12, though the specific publication could not be independently verified through the sources accessible for this review. The theoretical argument, as referenced in the [FRfG policy paper on intergenerational justice and youth voting rights](https://intergenerationaljustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/PP_Voting-Rights.pdf) and the [Harvard Law Review symposium on age discrimination](https://journals.law.harvard.edu/hrj/2022/11/childrens-rights-and-voting-age-discrimination/), holds that if the franchise is grounded in affected interests rather than demonstrated competence, there is no principled basis for excluding 12-year-olds while including 18-year-olds — and potentially strong grounds for extending it further.

**Could the cold/hot cognition framework support a 12-year threshold?** The developmental-psychology evidence, reviewed in Section S2.1, indicates that "cold" deliberative cognition — abstract reasoning, information processing, long-term thinking, the capacities most directly engaged by ballot completion — reaches adult-equivalent levels around age 16, while "hot" cognition (impulse control, resistance to peer influence) continues developing into the early-to-mid 20s. On this framework, 12-year-olds would likely fall below the threshold for cold-cognitive capacity, making the case for a 12-voting age difficult to ground in cognitive science alone. However, this cuts both ways: if cold cognition matures at 16 but hot cognition does not until the 20s, then the argument for excluding 16–17-year-olds on grounds of hot-cognitive deficits would also logically support excluding 20–25-year-olds — a conclusion no democracy has embraced. The framework thus raises the question of whether the *specific cognitive demands of voting* are "cold" (in which case 16 suffices) or "hot" (in which case the deficit is shared with older cohorts). Whether domain-specific political decision-making is more "cold" than "hot" remains an under-researched empirical question.

#### Civic Education as the Mediating Variable

A framework reading of the empirical and principled objections reveals that the "informed voter" critique may be better addressed by improving civic education than by maintaining an age floor. The UK PACAC recommended in its scrutiny of voting-age reform that "political education in the UK, particularly in England, be overhauled," treating the franchise extension as "a good opportunity for the development of a new school curriculum for political education" ([UK Commons Library, 2025](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn01747/)). In November 2025, the UK government announced proposals for reforming the school curriculum in England, including a new statutory requirement to teach citizenship at key stages 1 and 2, with expanded content on democracy, law, rights, media literacy, financial literacy, and climate education ([Teaching Citizenship, 2025](https://www.teachingcitizenship.org.uk/citizenship-strengthened-in-the-national-curriculum-what-this-means-for-schools/); [Electoral Commission, 2025](https://www.electoralcommission.org.uk/media-centre/changes-curriculum-opportunity-transform-democratic-education-schools)). The Electoral Commission simultaneously committed to ensuring "all newly enfranchised voters know how to register and exercise their right to vote" if Parliament approves the reform ([UK Commons Library, 2025](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn01747/)). This policy linkage — tying franchise expansion to civic curriculum investment — is a concrete institutional response to the objection that young voters lack the knowledge to participate meaningfully.

The deeper question, however, is whether the knowledge deficit is a *reason for exclusion* or a *reason for education*. Delli Carpini and Keeter's baseline — showing that adult political knowledge is chronically low across the entire electorate (developed further in S5.1) — suggests that the problem is systemic rather than age-specific. If the remedy for low civic knowledge is civic education, then the objection applies to all age cohorts, and the age floor serves as a proxy for a systemic failure that education policy, not suffrage policy, is better positioned to address. Conversely, if the objection is principled rather than empirical — that suffrage requires a form of autonomy that minors categorically lack — then the education response does not fully answer it, because autonomy is not merely a function of knowledge acquisition.

#### Unresolved Questions

Several empirical and normative questions remain open. First, the durability of any civic-engagement gains from lowering the voting age — whether the Austrian and Brazilian evidence showing comparable vote quality and sustained turnout persists across generational novelty effects — requires further longitudinal study (see S2.2). Second, the question of whether 16-year-olds' votes are *disproportionately* influenced by parents, compared to the influence profiles of adult voters, lacks a clean comparative benchmark in the existing literature. Third, the cold/hot cognition framework has not been adequately tested against domain-specific political decision-making — whether voting is paradigmatically "cold" (as cognitive load framing suggests) or involves sufficient "hot" elements (emotional arousal, social pressure, peer context) to engage the adolescent deficits. Fourth, the principled autonomy-based objection has not been adequately reconciled with the democratic principle that suffrage is a right of membership, not a reward for demonstrated capacity — a tension developed in Section S5.2. Finally, the relationship between civic curriculum reform and actual political knowledge gains in a lower-voting-age regime is untested; the UK's pending reform, announced in July 2025 with legislation introduced in February 2026, may become the first natural experiment in coupling franchise extension directly to statutory citizenship education ([UK Commons Library, 2025](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn01747/); see S1.2 for legislative status).

## Cognitive Decline and the Case for (or against) a Maximum Voting Age

### Clinical Evidence: Dementia Trajectories, Cognitive Domains, and Voting Capacity

The clinical evidence on when cognitive decline impairs the capacity to vote reveals a nuanced picture: voting capacity degrades along a continuum that maps imperfectly onto chronological age, and the specific cognitive domains most critical for voting—understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and choice—decline at different rates and with substantial individual variability.

#### The Doe Standard and Its Operationalization

The clinical benchmark for assessing voting capacity derives from a 2001 federal court decision, the "Doe" standard, which holds that a person can vote if they can understand the nature and effect of voting and can make a choice ([Appelbaum et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263849/)). Appelbaum, Bonnie, and Karlawish operationalized this standard into the Competence Assessment Tool for Voting (CAT-V), adding measures of *appreciation* (grasping the personal significance of a vote) and *reasoning* (the ability to compare choices and their consequences), thereby encompassing all four standard decision-making capacities used in medical consent assessment: understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and choice ([Appelbaum et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263849/)). In their study of 33 patients attending an Alzheimer's disease clinic, the authors found that "patients with very mild to mild Alzheimer's disease generally retained adequate ability to vote, and most persons with severe Alzheimer's disease did not," while "performance was highly variable among persons with moderate Alzheimer's disease" ([Appelbaum et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263849/)). Crucially, they concluded that "the capacity to vote, as embodied in the Doe voting capacity standard, can be measured simply and reliably," and that structured assessment is "particularly likely to be useful for people with moderate Alzheimer's disease, whose performance cannot be predicted from MMSE scores alone" ([Appelbaum et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263849/)).

#### Which Cognitive Domains Decline First—and Which Matter Most for Voting

The Irastorza (2011) study of 68 subjects with dementia and 25 controls found that "the capacity to vote is related to cognitive deterioration and, within that, is more related to understanding and appreciation" than to reasoning or the ability to express a choice ([Irastorza, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21789276/)). Specifically, 53% of dementia patients understood the nature of voting, 44% understood its effect, and 66.2% appreciated its consequences—but performance on comparative and consequential reasoning was markedly poorer ([Irastorza, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21789276/)). This finding was replicated and extended by Tiraboschi et al. (2011), who administered the Italian-adapted CAT-V to 38 subjects with Alzheimer's disease before the 2006 Italian general election. Strikingly, 90% of participants "scored the maximum on the choice measure" despite poor performance on understanding and reasoning items, indicating that "expressing a choice was by far the least affected by the dementing process" ([Tiraboschi et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21785703/)). Only three of 38 subjects scored maximum on all CAT-V items. MMSE and CAT-V scores correlated only moderately (r = 0.59, p < 0.0001), leading the authors to conclude that "voting capacity in AD is only roughly predicted by MMSE scores and may more accurately be measured by a structured questionnaire" such as the CAT-V ([Tiraboschi et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21785703/)). Poptsi et al. (2022) extended this line of research in a Greek sample, administering the CAT-V to older adults with and without cognitive decline, further confirming that structured assessment reveals function that global cognitive screens obscure ([Poptsi et al., 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3425/12/12/1614)).

The clinical implication is that the cognitive demands of voting are relatively modest: a voter must understand what voting means, appreciate that their vote matters to them, reason among options, and communicate a choice—but the threshold for "understanding and appreciation" is lower than that for complex medical or financial decision-making. The ability to *express a choice* is the most resilient capacity, persisting even into moderate stages of dementia when comprehension and reasoning have deteriorated.

#### Dementia Severity Stages and Voting Capacity

The across-studies pattern suggests a rough staging:

| DMMSE Range | Dementia Severity | Voting Capacity |
|---|---|---|
| 24–30 | Normal / borderline | Generally intact |
| 18–23 | Mild | Usually retained; structured assessment advisable |
| 10–17 | Moderate | Highly variable; individualized assessment essential |
| < 10 | Severe | Generally absent |

Appelbaum et al. (2005) confirmed that "the desire to vote was a poor predictor of voting capacity"—motivated but severely impaired patients still lacked capacity, while less motivated but mildly affected patients retained it ([Appelbaum et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263849/)). This finding undercuts the proposition that expressed willingness to participate should substitute for cognitive assessment.

#### Age-Specific Prevalence of Dementia and MCI

The population-level correlates are significant. According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and National Health Statistics data, the percentage of adults with a dementia diagnosis rises sharply with age: approximately 1.7% among those aged 65–74, rising to 13.1% among those 85 and older ([CDC National Health Statistics, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr203.pdf)). Overall dementia prevalence among Americans aged 65+ is estimated at approximately 12% ([Bennett et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8719342/)). Alzheimer's Disease International reports over 55 million people worldwide lived with dementia in 2020, a figure projected to nearly double every 20 years, reaching 78 million by 2030 and 139 million by 2050 ([Alzheimer's Disease International](https://www.alzint.org/about/dementia-facts-figures/dementia-statistics/); [WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dementia)). The incidence of dementia doubles approximately every five years of age up to 75–79, slowing thereafter.

Crucially for the maximum-age-limit debate, the large majority of older voters are *not* demented. Even among those aged 85+, roughly 60–87% do not have a dementia diagnosis. Dementia prevalence, while age-correlated, is not synonymous with old age—a point that directly challenges the logic of a chronological voting ceiling.

#### Normal Cognitive Aging vs. Pathological Decline

Normal (non-pathological) cognitive aging follows a different trajectory from dementia-related decline. Crystallized intelligence—accumulated knowledge, vocabulary, and learned skills—remains stable or even shows modest gains into the seventh and eighth decades, while fluid intelligence—processing speed, working memory, and abstract problem-solving—begins declining from the late twenties and continues through the life course ([PMC review of cognitive aging](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4015335/); [Karger, 2022](https://karger.com/dee/article/12/1/51/822980/Discrepancy-in-Fluid-and-Crystallized-Intelligence)). For the specific cognitive demands of voting—understanding the nature and effect of a ballot, appreciating the stakes for one's life, reasoning among a small number of options, and communicating a choice—crystallized capacities are heavily engaged. A voter relies on accumulated political knowledge, experience with prior elections, and practiced judgment rather than on rapid information processing or novel problem-solving. Normal age-related slowing and working-memory decline therefore do not necessarily impair the specific capacities the Doe standard and CAT-V assess.

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a transitional state between normal aging and dementia, affects approximately 15–20% of adults aged 65+. MCI does not automatically extinguish voting capacity; individuals with amnestic MCI may retain understanding and appreciation while experiencing memory deficits. It is only as MCI progresses to moderate dementia that voting-relevant capacities become substantially and unpredictably compromised—and even then, the evidence shows substantial variability.

#### Community-Dwelling Dementia Patients Who Vote

Research by Karlawish and colleagues has documented that a substantial proportion of community-dwelling persons with dementia exercise their franchise. Karlawish found that "as many as 64% of community-dwelling patients with Alzheimer disease and other forms of dementia may be voting" ([Neurology Today, 2004](https://neurologytoday.aan.com/doi/10.1212/00132985-200205000-00009)). Their broader body of work identified substantial barriers to voting for persons in institutional settings, but community-dwelling dementia patients voted at high rates, often with assistance from family members or caregivers ([Karlawish et al., 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15367557/); [Karlawish et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18198160/)). Whether this prevalence is ethically unproblematic depends on whether the votes cast meet the Doe standard's threshold of understanding and choice—a question that remains largely unaddressed at the population level, since no jurisdiction systematically assesses voting capacity among voters with diagnosed dementia.

#### The Variability Problem and the Bright-Line Challenge

The most consistent finding across all studies is *individual variability*. Appelbaum et al. (2005) found moderate-stage patients ranged from fully capable to clearly incapable ([Appelbaum et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263849/)). Tiraboschi et al. (2011) found that MMSE scores, the most widely used global cognitive screen, predicted CAT-V performance only moderately, meaning that "subjects' performance at any disease stage" was heterogeneous ([Tiraboschi et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21785703/)). The Neurology Today report on Karlawish's work reached a similar conclusion: some 80-year-olds with MCI are sharper than some 50-year-olds with early-onset dementia or other cognitive impairments.

This variability is the central empirical obstacle to a maximum chronological voting age. If voting capacity is distributed continuously across the aging population—if age is a weak proxy for the cognitive capacities that voting requires—then any bright-line age threshold will both over INCLUDE incapacitated individuals (who happen to be younger than the cutoff) and exclude competent ones (who happen to exceed it). The clinical evidence thus points toward individualized capacity assessment as a more accurate mechanism than chronological age, while simultaneously raising the practical question of whether such assessment can be administered fairly and at scale—a question addressed in S3.2.

#### Summary of Clinical Findings

The clinical evidence establishes several propositions relevant to the maximum voting age debate:

1. **Voting capacity is domain-specific.** The capacities required—understanding, appreciation, choice—are less demanding than those for medical consent or financial decision-making, and the ability to express a choice is remarkably resilient even in moderate dementia ([Tiraboschi et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21785703/)).

2. **Dementia severity maps onto voting capacity imperfectly.** Very mild to mild dementia generally preserves capacity; severe dementia generally extinguishes it; moderate dementia is highly variable and requires individualized assessment ([Appelbaum et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263849/)).

3. **Normal aging does not significantly impair voting-specific cognition.** Crystallized capacities, which heavily support political judgment, are preserved into advanced age ([PMC review of cognitive aging](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4015335/)).

4. **Dementia prevalence, while age-correlated, affects a minority of older voters**—roughly 2% among 65–74, rising to 13%+ among 85+ ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr203.pdf)). Most older voters have no dementia diagnosis.

5. **Substantial numbers of community-dwelling dementia patients vote**, with estimates as high as 64% ([Neurology Today, 2004](https://neurologytoday.aan.com/doi/10.1212/00132985-200205000-00009)).

6. **Individual variability undermines chronological proxies.** At any given age, the distribution of voting-relevant capacities is wide, and global cognitive screens like the MMSE are insufficient predictors ([Tiraboschi et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21785703/); [Irastorza, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21789276/)).

These findings do not, by themselves, settle the normative question of whether a maximum voting age is justified. But they do constrain the empirical premise of any such proposal: if the clinical capacities that matter for voting decline gradually, heterogeneously, and with only partial correlation to chronological age, a categorical age threshold is in tension with the clinical evidence. Whether the practical difficulties of individualized assessment—cost, consistency, risk of discriminatory application—outweigh the accuracy gains remains a legal and institutional question addressed in the following section.

### Legal Standards for Voting Competence and the Risk of Discriminatory Assessment

The legal architecture for determining who may and may not vote operates through a patchwork of constitutional provisions, statutory rules, and judicial standards that vary sharply across jurisdictions. What unites nearly all democracies, however, is a foundational asymmetry: no jurisdiction imposes a competence test on young voters, yet many retain mechanisms—however rarely invoked—to strip voting capacity from elderly citizens on cognitive grounds. This section maps that architecture, examines the risk of discriminatory application, and evaluates whether assisted-voting mechanisms offer a preferable alternative to disenfranchisement.

#### The Doe Voting Capacity Standard and Its Operationalization

The most influential legal framework for assessing voting competence in the United States emerged from a 2001 federal court decision establishing the "Doe voting capacity standard," operationalized by Appelbaum and colleagues in a 2005 study published in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* ([Appelbaum et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263849/)). The Doe standard's criteria—understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and choice—and the Competency Assessment Tool for Voting (CAT-V) derived from it are detailed in S3.1, along with the study's clinical findings on voting capacity across dementia stages (very mild to mild Alzheimer's generally preserves capacity; severe Alzheimer's generally extinguishes it; moderate Alzheimer's is highly variable and requires individualized assessment). The Doe standard is notably less demanding than the medical consent capacity framework from which it draws: it requires only understanding and choice, omitting appreciation and reasoning as independent legal requirements (though Appelbaum added them for research purposes).

#### U.S. State Laws on Voting and Mental Capacity

In the United States, the legal ground for removing voting capacity on cognitive bases varies significantly by state. Constitutional provisions, election codes, and guardianship statutes use different wording to exclude categories of persons, and there is no uniform national standard for mental competence to vote ([Doraisamy, 2021](https://digitalcommons.law.uidaho.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1164&context=idaho-law-review)). Some states bar individuals "under guardianship" from voting categorically, while others require a specific judicial finding of incapacity to vote. The ABA Commission on Law and Aging has emphasized that "evaluating a person's capacity to vote outside the voting process itself is inappropriate and should not be done except by a court authorized to do so" ([ABA Commission on Law and Aging, quoted in Aging King County, 2024](https://www.agingkingcounty.org/2024/11/01/the-right-to-vote-and-cognitive-impairment/)). Federal law provides some floor protections: the Voting Rights Act of 1965 prohibits conditioning the right to vote on literacy or interpretation tests, and the Help America Vote Act of 2002 requires accessible voting systems at each polling location ([SS&P Law, 2024](https://ssandplaw.com/blog/voting-rights-for-people-with-cognitive-impairment/)).

#### The UK Approach: Mental Capacity Act 2005 and the Right to Vote

The United Kingdom takes a notably more permissive approach. The UK Electoral Commission states that "a lack of mental capacity is not a legal incapacity to vote," and persons who meet other registration qualifications are eligible for registration regardless of mental capacity ([UK Electoral Commission](https://www.electoralcommission.org.uk/running-electoral-registration-wales/eligibility-register-vote/how-does-mental-capacity-affect-right-register-vote)). Alzheimer's UK confirms that "everyone with dementia has a right to vote" and that "you do not need to have mental capacity to vote" ([Alzheimer's UK](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/blog/can-people-with-dementia-vote)). Dementia UK similarly reports that the Electoral Commission's position is that persons meeting registration qualifications are eligible regardless of mental capacity ([Dementia UK, 2024](https://www.dementiauk.org/news/voting-and-dementia/)). Under the Mental Capacity Act 2005, the functional test for capacity is decision-specific, but in practice this has not been applied to strip voting capacity from persons with dementia. The Act's presumption of capacity, combined with the absence of any statutory mechanism for removing voting rights on cognitive grounds, means that even persons under guardianship or conservatorship retain the franchise in the UK.

#### CRPD Article 29 and International Standards

The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, Article 29, guarantees to persons with disabilities "political rights and the opportunity to enjoy them on an equal basis with others," including the right to vote and be elected. Critically, Article 29(a)(iii) explicitly provides for "assistance in voting by a person of their own choice" where necessary, and Article 29(a)(ii) protects "the right of persons with disabilities to vote by secret ballot" ([UN Enable, CRPD Article 29](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-29-participation-in-political-and-public-life.html)). The CRPD thus frames voting assistance as a right, not a concession, and requires states to facilitate it. The Open Society Foundations has argued that "inclusive democracies require voting rights for people with disabilities" and that CRPD Article 29 "provides unequivocally that all persons with disabilities have the right to political participation" ([Open Society Foundations](https://www.opensocietyfoundations.org/voices/inclusive-democracies-require-voting-rights-people-disabilities)).

#### The Asymmetry: No Competence Test for Young Voters

A central structural feature of all existing suffrage regimes is the asymmetry between minimum and maximum age scrutiny. No democracy requires young voters to demonstrate cognitive competence, political knowledge, or decision-making capacity as a condition of enfranchisement. The U.S. Twenty-Sixth Amendment provides that "the right of citizens of the United States, who are eighteen years of age or older, to vote, shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or any state on account of age" ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/amendments/amendment-xxvi/interpretations/161)). This language, modeled on the Fifteenth Amendment's prohibition on racial discrimination, has been interpreted to prohibit states from imposing special restrictions on 18–20-year-olds, such as heightened residency requirements. Yet the same constitutional order permits—through state guardianship laws—the removal of voting rights from elderly citizens on cognitive grounds that are never applied to the young. If competence is a legitimate suffrage requirement, its selective application only to older voters raises serious equal protection concerns. If competence is not a legitimate requirement, then the existing mechanisms for removing voting capacity from the cognitively impaired are themselves suspect.

#### Discriminatory Application Risks

The risk that competence assessments for voting have been applied discriminatorily is not merely theoretical. Guardianship laws in the United States have been used to restrict voting rights for disabled Americans in ways that disproportionately affect marginalized communities. The American Progress has documented how "guardianship laws that restrict access to the ballot" disenfranchise disabled Americans, noting that "protecting the right to vote for many disabled Americans requires eliminating guardianship laws that restrict access to the ballot" ([American Progress, 2025](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/democracy-denied-for-disabled-americans-guardianship-and-the-right-to-vote/)). The National Disability Rights Network has similarly highlighted that "voting restrictions that disenfranchise some or all people with disabilities who are subject to guardianship" remain a persistent problem ([NDRN, 2023](https://www.ndrn.org/resource/voting-guardianship/)). The Bazelon Center's toolkit for enfranchisement in guardianship notes "discriminatory state laws or practices" that result in individuals losing voting rights through guardianship proceedings ([Bazelon Center, 2022](https://www.bazelon.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Toolkit-for-Enfranchisement-in-Guardianship-2022-02-08.pdf)). These concerns are compounded by evidence from medical consent capacity research showing that even experienced physicians disagree substantially about capacity determinations: one study found only 56% agreement among five experienced physicians regarding the capacity status of Alzheimer's patients, despite 98% agreement on normal comparison subjects ([Marson et al., 1997, discussed in Moye et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5007079/)). If clinical professionals cannot reliably agree on capacity, the risk of discriminatory application—whether by race, class, or political affiliation—in voting contexts is acute.

#### Comparison with Medical Consent and Testamentary Capacity Standards

The four-component model of decisional capacity developed by Grisso and Appelbaum for medical consent—**understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and expression of a choice**—has become the dominant framework in clinical ethics ([Moye et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5007079/)). These components were originally identified through review of case law across jurisdictions and are now generally recognized as the dimensions of capacity that must all be present for a person to be deemed capable of making healthcare decisions. The Doe voting capacity standard draws directly on this framework but is notably less demanding: it requires only understanding and choice, omitting appreciation and reasoning as independent legal requirements (though Appelbaum added them for research purposes).

Testamentary capacity, governed in common law jurisdictions by the 1870 English decision in *Banks v Goodfellow*, requires that a testator: (1) understand the nature and effect of the will, (2) understand the extent of the property being disposed of, (3) be aware of the claims of potential beneficiaries, and (4) not be affected by a disorder of the mind that perverts their sense of right ([Banks v Goodfellow, 1870, discussed in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6762100/)). This standard is more demanding than the Doe voting standard in some respects—it requires knowledge of one's property and the claims of others—but less demanding in others, as it does not require the comparative reasoning about alternatives that the medical consent framework includes. The Mental Capacity Act 2005 in England and Wales has supplemented but not replaced *Banks v Goodfellow* for testamentary purposes, maintaining a functional, decision-specific approach ([Halcyon Doctors](https://halcyondoctors.com/banks-v-goodfellow-and-testamentary-capacity/)).

The transferability of these standards to voting is imperfect. Medical consent capacity is assessed in a clinical setting with a specific decision at stake; testamentary capacity is assessed at the time of will execution. Voting, by contrast, is a periodic act involving a complex, multi-option decision made in a public setting without clinical support. The Doe standard's minimalism—requiring only understanding and choice—may reflect an implicit recognition that voting is a lower-stakes decision than medical consent or estate disposition, or that the dignitary interests at stake in the franchise justify a lower capacity threshold.

#### Assisted Voting as an Alternative to Disenfranchisement

Federal law in the United States already provides multiple mechanisms for assisting cognitively impaired voters. Under the Voting Rights Act, an individual with a cognitive impairment may receive help voting from a person of their choice. The Help America Vote Act requires at least one accessible voting system per polling location, and the Voting Accessibility for the Elderly and Handicapped Act of 1984 requires accessible polling places ([SS&P Law, 2024](https://ssandplaw.com/blog/voting-rights-for-people-with-cognitive-impairment/)). The ABA Commission on Law and Aging, in collaboration with the Penn Memory Center, has produced a guide for assisting cognitively impaired individuals with voting, emphasizing that "a medical diagnosis does not disqualify a person from voting" and that "the ability to express a preference is what is required" ([ABA/Penn Memory Center guide, discussed in Aging King County, 2024](https://www.agingkingcounty.org/2024/11/01/the-right-to-vote-and-cognitive-impairment/)). CRPD Article 29(a)(iii) explicitly guarantees "assistance in voting by a person of their own choice" as a right, not a concession ([UN Enable](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-29-participation-in-political-and-public-life.html)).

The tension between assistance and ballot secrecy is real but manageable. The ACE Project has noted that for some voters with disabilities, "allowing them to be assisted in marking the ballot paper is the only way they can exercise their" franchise ([ACE Project](https://aceproject.org/today/forum/secrecy-of-the-ballot-for-voters-with-disabilities)). The CRPD resolves this tension by simultaneously guaranteeing the right to secret ballot and the right to assistance, suggesting that assistance need not compromise secrecy if the assistant is chosen by the voter and is not an election official or person in a position of authority over the voter. Mail-in and curbside voting, available in many U.S. jurisdictions, provide additional options that allow cognitively impaired voters to cast ballots with assistance in a private setting ([SS&P Law, 2024](https://ssandplaw.com/blog/voting-rights-for-people-with-cognitive-impairment/)).

#### No Age-Capped Franchise Exists in Any Democracy

No democratic jurisdiction currently imposes a maximum voting age or an age-capped franchise. The only jurisdiction with a maximum voting age is the Vatican City State, where the franchise for papal conclaves is limited to cardinals under 80—but this is a theocratic electoral college, not a democratic suffrage system ([Wikipedia, "Voting age"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age)). In Argentina, Brazil, and Ecuador, voting is voluntary for 16- and 17-year-olds but compulsory for those over 18 "until an upper age limit"—but this upper limit (typically 70 or 75) applies to the *obligation* to vote, not the *right* to vote ([Parliament of Australia, 2025](https://www.aph.gov.au/About_Parliament/Parliamentary_departments/Parliamentary_Library/Research/Policy_Briefs/2025-26/LoweringVotingAge)). The absence of any democratic age cap means that proposals for maximum age limits (discussed in S3.3) remain purely theoretical, with no existing institutional model to evaluate.

#### Risks of Converting a Categorical Age Floor into an Individualized Capacity Test

The prospect of replacing a categorical minimum age with an individualized capacity test—applied to all ages—raises several concerns. First, the reliability of capacity assessments is questionable even in clinical settings, as noted above regarding physician disagreement about capacity determinations. Physicians also recognized incapacity in only 42% of medical inpatients who lacked decisional capacity as determined by formal assessment ([Sessums et al., 2011, discussed in Moye et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5007079/)). If such error rates prevail in clinical contexts, applying capacity tests to all voters—young and old alike—would introduce enormous potential for arbitrary exclusion.

Second, the administrative burden would be staggering. The U.S. electorate exceeds 160 million voters; even a brief screening assessment for each would require resources far beyond any existing electoral infrastructure. Third, the potential for discriminatory application—by race, class, political affiliation, or geography—would be substantial, given the evidence that guardianship-based voting restrictions have already been applied in discriminatory patterns ([American Progress, 2025](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/democracy-denied-for-disabled-americans-guardianship-and-the-right-to-vote/); [NDRN, 2023](https://www.ndrn.org/resource/voting-guardianship/)). Fourth, the Twenty-Sixth Amendment's prohibition on denying the right to vote "on account of age" to citizens 18 and older would likely be interpreted to bar age-based capacity tests for that population, creating a constitutional asymmetry: a capacity test could be applied to 16- and 17-year-olds but not to those 18+, unless the test were genuinely age-neutral—a condition difficult to satisfy given the age-correlated nature of cognitive decline.

The existing legal architecture, imperfect as it is, reflects a pragmatic compromise: categorical age floors for youth (with no competence test), categorical franchise preservation for adults (with removal only through judicial guardianship proceedings), and assisted-voting mechanisms for those who need help. The UK's approach—treating mental capacity as irrelevant to voting eligibility—represents the most permissive end of the spectrum, while U.S. state guardianship laws represent the most restrictive. The CRPD's framework of assistance as a right offers a middle path that preserves the franchise for cognitively impaired voters without resorting to disenfranchisement, though its implementation remains uneven across jurisdictions.

### Proposals for Maximum Age Limits, Age-Weighted Voting, and the Philosophical Debate

The question of whether democracies should impose a *maximum* voting age—or, more ambitiously, weight votes differently by age—has generated a sophisticated philosophical literature. Three distinct institutional proposals must be disentangled: (a) a **hard maximum age** that disenfranchises citizens above a threshold; (b) **age-weighted voting** that reduces or increases the electoral weight of certain age cohorts while retaining their formal franchise; and (c) **age-based competence testing** that ties voting rights to demonstrated cognitive capacity rather than chronological age alone. Each proposal faces different normative and practical challenges, and the scholarly debate has moved well beyond the foundational treatment by Poama and Volacu (2023) into a multi-voice exchange that sharpens both the empirical and conceptual issues at stake.

#### The Equal-Influence Challenge: Poama & Volacu (2023)

Poama and Volacu's "Too old to vote? A democratic analysis of age-weighted voting" provides the most systematic framework for evaluating maximum-age proposals on their own democratic merits. They examine whether the principle of **equal opportunity of political influence**—which they regard as the interpretation of political equality most likely to ground age-based disenfranchisement—can justify either blanket disenfranchisement of older citizens or partial reduction of their voting weight through age-weighted schemes. Their conclusion is decisively negative. Blanket disenfranchisement fails on equal-influence grounds alone: older citizens do not actually possess disproportionate influence in every election or political context, so a categorical cut-off cannot be justified by the very principle invoked to defend it. Partial disenfranchisement via age-weighted voting fails on equal-influence grounds *in conjunction* with general electoral-policy considerations. Specifically, they identify **epistemic limitations** in determining the correct size of age-relative electoral weights—there is no reliable method for calibrating how much a 70-year-old's vote should count relative to a 30-year-old's—and **robustness problems**: under some realistic circumstances, equal influence might actually require *increasing* older citizens' voting weight rather than decreasing it, because lower turnout among the young or geographic concentration of elderly voters could already counteract their disproportionate influence ([Poama & Volacu 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14748851211062604)).

This last point is significant. The claim that older citizens wield disproportionate electoral influence—whether through higher turnout, greater political donations, or geographic clustering—is an empirical generalization that may hold in some elections but not others. Poama and Volacu show that an age-weighting scheme calibrated to correct imbalance *in one context* may exacerbate it *in another*, making the proposal's justification inherently unstable across electoral cycles. They also note that non-democratic justifications for disenfranchisement—such as intergenerational justice arguments derived from social utility—are explicitly excluded from their analysis, meaning that even if their conclusions defeat the *democratic* case for age limits, a consequentialist case might still stand ([Poama & Volacu 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14748851211062604)). Geoffrey Downs (2017) drew the explicit policy conclusion from this consequentialist register: "give the vote to people at 16 for both elections and referenda and take it away from them at retirement age," linking minimum and maximum age reform into a single intergenerational justice framework ([Downs 2017](https://www.independent.co.uk/voices/brexit-vote-retirement-age-young-people-democracy-a7900341.html), cited in [Poama & Volacu 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14748851211062604)).

#### The Consequentialist-Egalitarian Divide: Van Parijs (1998) and Gosseries (2022)

The foundational consequentialist argument for reducing elderly voting power comes from **Philippe Van Parijs** (1998), whose "The Disfranchisement of the Elderly, and Other Attempts to Secure Intergenerational Justice" contends that there are "simply too many senile voters and their number is growing," and that "the vote should not be a privilege in perpetuity." Van Parijs's argument rests on intergenerational justice: because younger citizens will bear the long-term consequences of political decisions (especially regarding fiscal policy, climate, and public debt), fairness requires that their interests be weighted more heavily than those of citizens who will live under those decisions for fewer years. This is a purely instrumental or outcome-oriented justification—it treats democracy as valuable for producing just distributive outcomes rather than as an intrinsically egalitarian procedure ([Van Parijs 1998](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1088-4963.1998.tb00072.x), cited in [Bengtson & Albertsen 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w)). As **Astra Taylor** (2019) argued in a widely discussed *New York Times* op-ed, the empirical premise of disproportionate influence is stark: people 65 and older are more than three times as likely to make political donations as those under 30, and the American electorate is the oldest it has been since at least 1970, "graying at a rapid clip, with the well-off living longer than ever before" ([Taylor 2019](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2019/10/18/opinion/old-age-president-2020.html), cited in [Poama & Volacu 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14748851211062604)).

**Axel Gosseries** (2022) critically examines this consequentialist lineage in "Should Old Age Votes Be Granted Less Weight?" He rehearses two possible arguments for age-adjusted disfavour of older voters: the **"future residence" argument** (essentially Van Parijs's exposure-time claim—that younger citizens will be affected by decisions for longer) and the **"differential lifetime" argument** (that fairness between long-lived and short-lived persons requires adjusting voting weights to equalize total lifetime political influence). Gosseries concludes that *neither* argument is collectively conclusive. The future-residence argument conflates affectedness with exposure duration in ways that generate paradoxes (e.g., a policy affecting only retirees would give the young no say under the all-affected principle, yet Van Parijs's age-weighting would still reduce the elderly vote's weight in such decisions). The differential-lifetime argument faces epistemic difficulties analogous to those Poama and Volacu identify: there is no administrable method for calibrating lifetime voting weights, and the argument's premises about what constitutes a "fair" distribution of influence over a lifetime remain contested ([Gosseries 2022](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/server/api/core/bitstreams/1554f1a2-c187-44b4-b0a1-c034855b8b56/content)). Gosseries also notes that the reasons for disenfranchising the young are competence-related, whereas the reasons for reducing the elderly's voting weight are *not* competence-based—a distinction that renders the two types of age limits normatively asymmetrical.

#### The Boundary-Problem Framework: Bengtson & Albertsen (2023)

Bengtson and Albertsen ("The Voting Rights of Senior Citizens: Should All Votes Count the Same?") relocate the debate to the **boundary problem** in democratic theory: who should be included in democratic decision-making? They test three prominent solutions against the question of whether senior citizens should receive lower voting weight:

1. **The all-affected principle** (Goodin 2007; Arrhenius 2005): a decision may affect people to varying degrees, and this variation should be reflected in the strength of their say. On this principle, Bengtson and Albertsen argue there *is* reason to grant most senior citizens a lower voting weight than most non-senior citizens in most decisions, because the temporal reach of decisions is shorter for the elderly.

2. **The all-subjected principle** (López-Guerra 2005; Beckman 2009): those subject to a decision should have a say in it. Here too, because younger citizens will be subject to decisions for longer, a plausible weighted interpretation supports greater voting power for the young.

3. **The relational egalitarian principle** (Bengtson 2022; Kolodny 2014; Viehoff 2014; Wilson 2019): democracy is valuable because it constitutes equal relations among citizens, not merely because it tracks affectedness or subjection. Under this principle, the implications are **context-dependent**: whether senior citizens should have lower weight "depends on how people relate to each other in society." In some relational configurations—for instance, where older citizens dominate political office-holding and party structures—reducing their voting weight might advance relational equality. In others—where elderly citizens are politically marginalized or dependent on younger family members—*increasing* their weight might better serve relational egalitarianism ([Bengtson & Albertsen 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w)).

This tripartite framework is important because it shows that whether a maximum age or age-weighting is democratically justified does not hinge on a single principle but on which theory of democratic inclusion one endorses. The all-affected and all-subjected principles generate a *prima facie* case for reducing older voters' weight, but relational egalitarianism may cut in either direction—or oppose age-weighting altogether if it reveals hierarchies of status that a formal age threshold would entrench.

#### Angell (2024) and Miglino (2025): Sharpening the Debate

Kim Angell (2024, "Should We Increase Young People's Voting Power?") offers the most sustained argument for youth-leaning age-weighting. He contends that **exposure time** to a decision's effects is typically a good proxy for the degree of affectedness, such that democracies have reason to *gradually reduce* members' voting power as they age. He builds this case on both the all-affected and all-subjected principles, arguing that a weighting-sensitive interpretation of each principle—where influence is proportional to affectedness or subjection—supports age-weighted voting in ordinary parliamentary elections. Angell's argument is explicitly empirical and contingent: he does not claim that age-weighting is always required, but that there is at least a *presumption* in its favour over strict "one person, one vote" ([Angell 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-024-10443-2)).

Miglino (2025, "Preference Intensity, Age-Weighted Votes, and the Democratic Empowerment of Young Citizens") mounts a powerful critique. He argues that Angell's argument covertly relies on a **conception of political inclusion proportional to preference intensity**—the idea that those with stronger preferences about the alternatives should have greater say. This understanding, Miglino demonstrates, has two damaging implications. First, it is **exclusionary toward low-preference-intensity persons**—such as ascetics or the politically disengaged—who would effectively be disenfranchised despite having a legitimate interest in political inclusion. Second, it creates **perverse incentives to adopt expensive tastes**: if voting weight scales with preference intensity, citizens have strategic reasons to cultivate costly preferences to increase their enfranchisement, a problem familiar from Dworkin's (1981) critique of welfarist theories of equality. Miglino formalizes this as a logical disproof: if the preference-intensity premise is rejected (as it should be, given its implications), and the temporal/exposure premise alone is insufficient to ground the conclusion, then Angell's case for age-weighted voting under both the all-affected and all-subjected principles collapses ([Miglino 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-025-10495-y)).

This exchange sharpens the debate beyond Poama and Volacu's framework by identifying the *hidden normative commitments* within seemingly all-affected/all-subjected arguments for age-weighting. The dispute is not merely about empirical facts (who votes more, who lives longer) but about whether preference intensity can serve as a legitimate metric of democratic inclusion—a question with implications far beyond age.

#### Relational Egalitarianism Applied to Age Limits

The relational egalitarian framework—developed by Kolodny (2014), Viehoff (2014), Wilson (2019), and Bengtson (2022)—offers an alternative to both the all-affected and all-subjected principles, though its full introduction to democratic-boundary questions appears in S3.3's treatment of Bengtson & Albertsen above and its applications to competence and inclusivity are developed in S5.1 and S5.2 respectively. Applied specifically to age limits, relational egalitarianism complicates both the case for maximum ages and the case for age-weighting. A hard maximum age—disenfranchising citizens at, say, 70—expresses a judgment that older persons are no longer full political equals, imposing a **dignitary harm** analogous to that historically inflicted through race- and gender-based disenfranchisement. The same concern applies, albeit less dramatically, to age-weighting: if a 70-year-old's vote counts for half of a 35-year-old's, the scheme expresses graded citizenship. Relational egalitarians would likely find this incompatible with the equal status democracy requires—though Bengtson and Albertsen's analysis shows the picture is more nuanced, since relational egalitarianism might sometimes support *increasing* older voters' weight if their political subordination in other domains (economic dependency, social marginalization) is the more pressing threat to equal relations ([Bengtson & Albertsen 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w); [Viehoff 2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/papa.12036)).

#### The Brexit Case Study

Brexit serves as the most concrete empirical test of age-based policy bias and the stakes of gerontocratic influence. In the 2016 EU referendum, **73% of 18–24-year-olds voted Remain, while only 40% of those aged 65+ did so**—a gap that has been extensively documented as one of the strongest age divides in modern electoral history ([Statista 2016](https://www.statista.com/statistics/520954/brexit-votes-by-age/)). More significantly, demographic analysis showed that the Leave majority was projected to **evaporate through cohort replacement alone**—as younger, more pro-Remain voters reached voting age and older, more pro-Leave voters passed away—without any individual changing their vote ([Bengtson & Albertsen 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w), citing post-Brexit polling analysis).

This case crystallizes the practical stakes of the theoretical debate. If older voters' disproportionate influence (in turnout, donations, and office-holding age) produced a policy outcome that younger generations will bear for decades, then the intergenerational justice argument for age-weighting gains concrete force. Conversely, the Brexit case also illustrates why a hard maximum age is a blunt instrument: the relevant variable is not chronological age per se but turnout propensity, policy prioritization, and the structural weight of older cohorts in a gerontocratic party system. A maximum age of 70 would not address the disproportionate influence of voters in their 50s and 60s; it would exclude many cognitively intact older citizens who voted precisely as younger cohorts might have done had they turned out at comparable rates.

#### Jerzak and the Broader Landscape

**Ethan Jerzak** (forthcoming in *The Philosophical Quarterly*) contributes a further treatment titled "Age-Weighted Democracy," which challenges the slogan "one person, one vote" as an unreflective democratic dogma and argues, against its sufficiency, that concentrating greater political influence on the parts of life in which citizens are actually engaged—roughly, the younger and middle years—may better serve democratic ends. Jerzak's approach connects age-weighting to a broader dissatisfaction with purely formal accounts of political equality, though the precise contours of his argument await publication ([Jerzak, forthcoming, *Philosophical Quarterly*](https://philarchive.org/archive/JERADW)).

The debate also intersects with **Demeny voting**—parents casting proxy votes on behalf of minor children—which addresses intergenerational underrepresentation through an entirely different mechanism: not by reducing the elderly vote but by adding youthful representation through parental proxy (analyzed in S4.3). While Demeny voting raises its own concerns (parents may not represent children's interests; it entrenches family-based power; it violates strict "one person, one vote"), it illustrates that the age-weighting proposals examined here are not the only available response to gerontocratic bias. The contrast is instructive: Demeny voting increases the *total* number of votes to enhance young people's influence, while age-weighting decreases the *relative* weight of older votes—two structurally different strategies for addressing the same perceived problem of intergenerational inequity.

#### Competence Testing as a Distinct Path

A third proposal—age-based competence testing—warrants distinction from both hard caps and weight adjustment. No democracy currently imposes an upper age limit, but some U.S. states have laws linking voting rights to mental capacity determinations, typically through guardianship proceedings (covered in detail in S3.2). The concern that drives age-based testing—cognitive decline in the elderly—applies *individually* and variably, not categorically. Some 80-year-olds are sharper than some 50-year-olds, and normal age-related cognitive changes do not uniformly impair the specific capacities (understanding, appreciation, reasoning, communicating a choice) that voting requires. A maximum age is thus a *proxy* for a condition (cognitive incapacity) that should be assessed individually—precisely the logic that competence-testing proposals would follow. The **Brennan Center for Justice** has argued for age limits on *office-holding* (noting that about half of people over 80 suffer from dementia or cognitive impairment) but explicitly frames this as a problem of representative capacity, not democratic membership—thereby highlighting the asymmetry between competence concerns applied to office-holding age ceilings and suffrage age ceilings ([Brennan Center 2024](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/age-limits-would-strengthen-congress)). Converting a categorical age floor into an individualized capacity test risks discriminatory application along race, class, and political-affiliation lines—a risk magnified if the test is applied only to elderly voters and not to young ones. The asymmetry remains: no democracy requires a competence test for 16-year-olds, so imposing one solely on citizens above a certain age raises fundamental equal-protection concerns (see S3.2 for the legal architecture and S5.1 for the epistocratic challenge).

#### Synthesis and Unresolved Questions

The philosophical literature reveals a genuine **tension** rather than a resolution. The all-affected and all-subjected principles, on weighted interpretations, generate a *prima facie* case for reducing older voters' voting power, while relational egalitarianism is context-dependent and may cut in either direction. The consequentialist argument from Van Parijs is powerful but treats democracy as merely instrumental, a premise many democratic theorists reject. Angell's extension sharpens the all-affected/all-subjected case but, as Miglino demonstrates, depends on a preference-intensity premise that is independently problematic. Gosseries's critical survey finds neither the future-residence nor the differential-lifetime argument collectively conclusive. (For the full analyses, see the Poama & Volacu, Bengtson & Albertsen, and Angell & Miglino subsections above.)

One circumstance under which equal influence might require *increasing* older voters' weight rather than decreasing it merits emphasis. Poama and Volacu identify it (discussed in the Equal-Influence Challenge subsection above): if older citizens' turnout advantage is already counteracted by geographic concentration or party fragmentation, a scheme calibrated to equalize influence would give them *more* weight, not less. A second arises under relational egalitarianism: if older citizens are politically subordinated through economic dependency, social exclusion, or underrepresentation in party structures, equal relations may require amplifying rather than diminishing their voice ([Bengtson & Albertsen 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w)).

The unresolved empirical question is whether the disproportionate influence of older citizens is a *structural feature* of aging democracies—persistent across electoral cycles and policy domains—or a *contingent outcome* of specific demographic and institutional configurations that could be addressed through non-age-based reforms (lowering the minimum age, proportional representation, youth quotas, deliberative assemblies). The Brexit case suggests both: age-based policy bias was real and consequential, but it was also partially self-correcting through cohort replacement, and its most extreme consequences might have been mitigated by lowering the franchise age rather than raising a maximum. Whether the philosophical case for age limits or age-weighting survives these empirical and institutional qualifications remains, as of 2025–2026, genuinely open.

## Intergenerational Equity and Demographic Representation

### Empirical Evidence: Youth Underrepresentation, Age–Turnout Gradients, and Policy Bias

#### The Age–Turnout Gradient

A robust empirical pattern across advanced democracies is that voter turnout rises steeply with age. In OECD countries, citizens under 25 are approximately 20 percentage points less likely to vote than those aged 55 and above, a gap that has widened over recent decades as youth participation has fallen faster than that of older cohorts ([OECD, Society at a Glance 2024](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/society-at-a-glance-2024_918d8db3-en/full-report/voting_4cc36656.html)). In the United States, while youth turnout (ages 18–29) reached roughly 50% in the 2024 presidential cycle—a notable increase from prior cycles—the rate remained well below that of voters aged 65 and older, which exceeded 70% ([Tufts CIRCLE, 2025](https://circle.tufts.edu/latest-research/new-data-nearly-half-youth-voted-2024)). Similar gradients are documented in European democracies, where first-time voters consistently turn out at lower rates than older first-time voters did at the same age, a pattern sometimes termed the "vicious cycle of political alienation" ([Stockemer & Sundström, 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/59688/1/9780472902842.pdf)). The age–turnout gradient is not merely a lifecycle effect (voters acquiring habits with age); it also reflects cohort-specific differences in political socialization, with younger cohorts in many democracies displaying lower baseline civic engagement than their predecessors at equivalent ages ([Stockemer, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/government-and-opposition/article/age-inequalities-in-political-representation-a-review-article/E1FB531E2E6BD93582792E1D92AB18ED)).

This gradient has direct consequences for political representation. As Stockemer's review synthesizes, "parties usually concentrate on higher-turnout age groups in their quest for a cost-effective" electoral strategy, meaning that the policy preferences of older, higher-turnout voters receive disproportionate attention from parties and candidates ([Stockemer, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/government-and-opposition/article/age-inequalities-in-political-representation-a-review-article/E1FB531E2E6BD93582792E1D92AB18ED)). The result is a systemic skew: the median voter in the European Union is approximately 44.4 years old, while in the United States it is approximately 52—both substantially above the median age of the general population ([Mulkeen, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)). Political leadership mirrors this pattern: in the U.S., the average age of Senators is 65.3 and of Representatives is 57.9 ([Mulkeen, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)).

#### Electorate–Population Age Gaps

The disparity between the age profile of electorates and the age profile of the general population is a structural feature of aging democracies. Because older citizens both live longer and vote at higher rates, electorates skew older than populations. In the United States, the share of eligible voters aged 65 and older rose from 21% in 2016 to 23% in 2020 and was projected to reach approximately 28% by later in the decade ([Center for American Progress, 2020](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/americas-electoral-future-3/)). In Germany, projections indicate that by 2060 approximately two-thirds of all voters will be 50 years or older, with only about one-third younger than 50 ([Buchmeier, 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/FCCA7EAC66F472FF42179B178FD611EC/S1537592723000981a.pdf/the-aging-democracy-demographic-effects-political-legitimacy-and-the-quest-for-generational-pluralism.pdf)). Japan offers the most acute case: the elderly share of eligible voters (age 20 and over) was projected to rise from 21% in 1991 to approximately 33% by 2025 ([Asian Survey, 2022](https://online.ucpress.edu/as/article/62/4/695/185898/Intergenerational-Politics-in-an-Aging-SocietyThe)). These gaps between population and electorate age profiles mean that the deliberative median of the voting body is chronologically distant from the demographic median of the citizenry—a fact that becomes consequential when policy preferences diverge by age.

#### Policy Domains with Measurable Cohort-Skew

Several concrete policy domains exhibit measurable age-based cohort-skew attributable to the age–turnout gradient:

**Climate policy.** A cross-national study published in *Population and Environment* found that a 1% higher share of elderly population is associated with a 2% increase in the NDC predicted per capita emissions in 2030, a 0.28°C rise in the NDC warming index, and approximately five fewer climate change laws (quality- and lifetime-adjusted), using instrumental variables to address endogeneity ([Aklin et al., 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11111-023-00425-4)). The authors interpret this as evidence that "the increasing political influence of the older population as a consequence of aging hinders climate policy ambition." These findings are consistent with survey evidence that older cohorts express lower support for climate mitigation measures, fossil fuel taxes, and public spending on environmental purposes, even though older voters are not necessarily more likely to deny climate change outright ([Aklin et al., 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11111-023-00425-4)). The *Juliana v. United States* litigation—in which youth plaintiffs asserted that the federal government's climate inaction violated their constitutional rights—has become a landmark legal articulation of intergenerational standing, with the U.S. Supreme Court ultimately denying the youth plaintiffs' standing but not rejecting the underlying premise of intergenerational climate harm ([Harvard Law Review, 2021](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-134/juliana-v-united-states/); [Climate Case Chart](https://www.climatecasechart.com/collections/juliana-v-united-states_f8231e)).

**Public debt and fiscal policy.** The U.S. Congressional Budget Office's 2025 long-term budget outlook projects that federal debt held by the public will reach 156% of GDP by 2055, driven substantially by rising Medicare and Social Security outlays tied to an aging population. The deficit is projected to average 6.3% of GDP over the 30-year period, more than one and a half times the average of the past 50 years ([CBO, 2025](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61270)). Mulkeen (2025) documents specific cases of intergenerational fiscal transfer: the United States borrowed over $8 trillion to fund military operations in Iraq and Afghanistan, imposing repayment obligations on younger and future generations who had no voice in the borrowing decisions ([Mulkeen, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)). The pattern of financing current consumption through debt that shifts burdens temporally—rather than through progressive taxation on current beneficiaries—illustrates how distorted suffrage weights can produce concrete intergenerational transfers.

**Pension indexation and social spending.** Older voters' policy preferences are associated with higher spending on pensions and healthcare. In aging democracies, political agendas "often prioritize policies that benefit older populations, such as pensions and healthcare, while neglecting issues like climate action, youth employment, and long-term sustainability" ([Mulkeen, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)). The OECD projects that by 2070, the old-age dependency ratio could rise to 65–70% in many countries, intensifying the fiscal pressure to prioritize social support systems for retirees over investment in education, green infrastructure, or other long-term-oriented spending that disproportionately benefits younger cohorts ([Mulkeen, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)).

#### Demographic Projections Through 2050

Demographic projections amplify the concern that the age–turnout gradient will intensify rather than abate. By 2050, the global population aged 60 and over is projected to reach approximately 2.1 billion, significantly increasing old-age dependency ratios worldwide ([Mulkeen, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)). In the European Union, Eurostat projects that the median age will increase by 6.6 years between 2025 and 2100, with the proportion of people aged 65 and older rising substantially as a share of the total population ([Eurostat, Population Projections](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Population_projections_in_the_EU)). The Center for American Progress "States of Change" project, using census-based demographic simulations, models how generational turnover and aging will reshape the American electorate through 2060, finding that older cohorts will comprise a growing share of the eligible electorate under even conservative assumptions about generational political change ([Center for American Progress, 2020](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/americas-electoral-future-3/)). These projections suggest that the structural bias in suffrage weights—where older, higher-turnout voters dominate electoral outcomes—is likely to deepen over the coming three decades absent institutional countermeasures.

#### Global Share of Adolescents Eligible to Vote

The vast majority of the world's adolescents are currently excluded from universal suffrage. Most countries retain a legal voting age of 18, and several contemporary reforms have lowered the age to 16 in specific national or subnational electoral contexts (see §S1.2 for the global census of voting ages and the historical trajectory from 21 to 18 to 16) ([FRfG Policy Paper, 2024](https://intergenerationaljustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/PP_Voting-Rights.pdf)). The FRfG policy paper notes that "young people make up a considerable portion of the global population" yet are typically barred from the ballot box despite their affectedness by long-duration policy decisions on climate, debt, and education. The UNICEF Innocenti synthesis on children's voting further underscores that adolescents and children represent a substantial and growing demographic cohort whose interests are structurally underrepresented in current suffrage arrangements.

#### The Family Solidarity Counter-Hypothesis

One proposed mechanism by which the interests of non-voting youth might be represented is "family solidarity": the hypothesis that parents and grandparents vote in ways that internalize the interests of their minor children and grandchildren. Empirical assessments of this claim yield mixed results. Research on intergenerational family solidarity finds that family relationships are "the locus of both intergenerational consensus and conflict," and that value differences between generations exist within households, not merely between them ([Encyclopedia.com, Intergenerational Relations](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/intergenerational-relations)). Studies of voting behavior find significant parent–child divergence in political preferences, particularly on issues like climate policy, where cross-national survey evidence documents an inverted U-shaped relationship between age and climate concern, with younger cohorts expressing higher levels of climate anxiety than their parents and grandparents ([Tranter & Booth, 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11111-023-00425-4)). While extended self-interest models suggest that older adults may support childcare and education policies under highly familialized welfare regimes, the same research finds that "a highly familialized welfare model tends to have a negative impact on fostering family solidarity," suggesting that reliance on family-based representation may weaken rather than strengthen intergenerational voice ([MDPI, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/17/15/6771)). The family solidarity hypothesis thus provides at best a partial and contingent mitigation of youth underrepresentation, not a structural remedy.

#### Philosophical Grounding: Lecce (2009)

The empirical patterns described above find a philosophical anchor in Steven Lecce's argument that disenfranchising the young constitutes a form of social injustice. Lecce (2009) frames youth exclusion not merely as a pragmatic polling issue but as a structural wrong: young people are subject to laws and policies they have no role in selecting representatives to shape, violating the democratic principle that those affected by collective decisions should have a voice in making them ([Lecce, 2009, cited in Bengtson & Albertsen, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w); [cited in ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/402857606_Should_Democracy_Grow_up_Children_and_Voting_Rights)). This philosophical framing does not itself resolve the empirical questions—whether lower voting ages produce measurably different policy outcomes, or whether 16-year-olds have sufficient cognitive competence—but it establishes that the empirical patterns of youth underrepresentation carry normative weight that cannot be dismissed as merely descriptive.

#### Emerging Legislative Responses

The empirical evidence of structural intergenerational bias has prompted concrete legislative responses. Senator Elizabeth Warren reintroduced the Youth Voting Rights Act in 2023, which would among other provisions support preregistration for 16- and 17-year-olds who would become eligible to vote by the time of the subsequent general election, and would ensure campus polling place availability ([U.S. Congress, S.2985, 118th Congress](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/2985); [Warren, 2023](https://www.warren.senate.gov/news/press-releases/senator-warren-reintroduces-youth-voting-rights-act-to-empower-young-people-expand-access-to-ballot-box/)). The UK's pending reform to lower the voting age to 16 nationwide would enfranchise approximately 1.7 million young people; the bill's legislative status and timeline are detailed in §S1.2. The UK reform remains a forward-looking natural experiment without observable implementation effects. The EIONET/ETC-ST 2025 report on intergenerational justice and youth participation in Europe has documented how youth participation mechanisms—youth climate councils, local conferences of youth, youth participatory budgets—are being tested across Europe but remain largely advisory rather than decisional, underscoring the gap between youth voice and youth voting power ([EIONET/ETC-ST, 2025](https://www.eionet.europa.eu/etcs/etc-st/products/etc-st-report-1-2025-intergenerational-justice-and-youth-participation/@@download/file/ETC%20ST%20Report%20Intergenerational%20justice%20and%20youth%20participation.pdf)).

The empirical picture is thus multi-dimensional: a persistent and widening age–turnout gradient; an electorate–population age gap that is projected to deepen through 2050 and beyond; measurable cohort-skew in climate, fiscal, and social spending policy domains; and a global default of exclusion for the large adolescent demographic. Whether these patterns constitute an injustice requiring institutional redress—and what form that redress should take—is the question the normative analysis in S4.2 takes up.

### Normative Debate: Gerontocracy, Democratic Legitimacy, and Non-Age-Based Reforms

The empirical evidence surveyed in S4.1 establishes a descriptive fact: in most advanced democracies, older citizens vote at higher rates, hold office at disproportionate rates, and constitute a growing share of the electorate. The normative question is whether this demographic reality constitutes a *democratic deficit* — a structural bias that is not merely unfortunate but *unjust*. This subsection examines the theoretical debate surrounding "gerontocracy," assesses whether the label is warranted, and evaluates non-age-based reform proposals that might address intergenerational bias without imposing age-based suffrage restrictions.

#### The Gerontocracy Diagnosis: Moyn's "Oldigarchy" Framing

The most prominent recent articulation of the gerontocracy thesis comes from Samuel Moyn's *Gerontocracy in America: How the Old Are Hoarding Power and Wealth — and What to Do About It* (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2025). Moyn argues that the United States has drifted into what he terms an "oldigarchy" — a system in which older Americans dominate not only the ballot box but also legislatures, corporate boards, courts, and advocacy organizations, with the AARP functioning as "the best-funded advocacy group in world history" with no youth counterpart. As summarized in the *Harvard Gazette* excerpt, Moyn contends that "an aging society is more set on preservation than on renovation," and that elder political dominance actively blocks younger generations' access to housing, employment, and climate action. The median age of primary voters in 2024 was approximately 65; the median donor age runs as high as 70. Moyn's prescription includes age caps for congressional office and, more radically, reweighting the votes of younger citizens ([Harvard Gazette, Aug. 2026](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/08/why-we-need-to-break-grip-of-oldigarchy-gerontocracy-in-america/); [Yale University Press book page](https://campuspress.yale.edu/samuelmoyn/gerontocracy-in-america-how-the-old-are-hoarding-power-and-wealth-and-how-to-stop-it/)).

Pocasangre and Wahl's *The Age Divide: How America's Electoral System Reinforces the Gerontocracy* (Protect Democracy / New America, July 2025) provides complementary data showing that the U.S. electoral system's architecture — off-cycle elections, low-salence local contests, and primary systems with overwhelmingly older electorates — amplifies elder influence beyond raw turnout differentials ([Protect Democracy report](https://protectdemocracy.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Pocasangre-and-Wahl-The-Age-Divide.pdf); [New America, Aug. 2025](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/a-blueprint-for-healthier-political-parties/)).

Charles McClean's book project *Silver Democracy: Youth Representation in an Aging Japan* (University of California, San Diego) extends the analysis to Japan, where the term "silver democracy" originated as a descriptor of welfare-state politics shaped by an aging electorate. McClean's central finding complicates the gerontocracy thesis: using an original dataset of over 200,000 municipal candidates across more than 20,000 elections (1999–2024), he shows that youth underrepresentation stems primarily from *institutional barriers* — higher minimum age requirements and candidate-centric electoral systems — rather than voter preferences. Survey experiments reveal that "both younger and older voters prefer younger candidates." Moreover, regression discontinuity evidence from close elections shows that young mayors significantly increase child welfare spending (particularly daycare infrastructure) without cutting elderly welfare, suggesting that greater youth representation does not necessarily exacerbate intergenerational conflict but can produce *more* investment in future generations ([McClean project page](https://www.charlesmcclean.com/book-project); [NPR, June 2023](https://www.npr.org/2023/06/28/1184894573/the-growing-concern-of-japans-silver-democracy)).

#### The Rebuttal: Is It Oligarchy, Not Gerontocracy?

The *Boston Review*'s review of Moyn's book (Summer 2026 issue) offers the most developed critique of the gerontocracy framing. The reviewer argues that Moyn's book makes three separate arguments — that older people have amassed disproportionate power, that this is *prima facie* problematic, and that age-based remedies can address it — and that only the first holds. The reviewer contends that "we don't live in a gerontocracy but an oligarchy, and a focus on age can only distract from that more existential threat." Several specific rebuttals emerge:

1. **Housing and jobs are not zero-sum.** If older people vacated their homes, they would occupy different homes; if they retired en masse, automation and AI-driven layoffs mean jobs would not automatically be "freed up" for younger workers. The image of a fixed pie is misleading.
2. **Older voters are not reliably conservative.** In the 2024 presidential election, the 65+ population was "significantly more liberal" than the 50–64 cohort Moyn belongs to. And if older people have one obviously shared interest — the solvency of Social Security — the system remains unreformed, suggesting older voters do not monolithically pursue their own interests.
3. **Educational spending effects are unclear.** The conventional wisdom that older voters oppose school funding "feels true" but lacks rigorous empirical support; proportionally older districts may not systematically underfund education.

The reviewer thus concludes that "the great virtue of Moyn's important new book is that it takes these issues seriously, grounding the debate in data and offering concrete prescriptions" — but that the prescriptive project "is flawed" because the true problem is wealth inequality, not age per se ([Boston Review, Summer 2026](https://www.bostonreview.net/articles/the-myth-of-gerontocracy/)).

#### The Egalitarian Response

The University of Bath IPR blog (October 2025) offered what it titled "An egalitarian answer to gerontocracy," proposing that relational egalitarian principles — which require that citizens relate as equals in a shared democratic community — can diagnose the structural bias problem without endorsing age-based disenfranchisement or age-weighted voting. The blog argues that relational egalitarianism reframes the issue: the problem is not that old people vote, but that the *structure* of political influence (turnout gradients, candidate age thresholds, fundraising networks) systematically degrades the relational standing of younger citizens as equal participants. This reframing points toward institutional reforms — lowering candidate age requirements, proportional representation, deliberative assemblies — rather than suffrage restrictions ([Bath IPR blog, Oct. 2025](https://blogs.bath.ac.uk/iprblog/2025/10/15/an-egalitarian-answer-to-gerontocracy/)).

#### The Normative Case for Intergenerational Injustice

Beyond Moyn's popular treatment, several political theorists have built systematic normative cases that the current age structure of electorates produces *unjust* outcomes:

- **Mulkeen (2025)** argues that "democratic systems are failing younger and future generations" through "harmful short-termism" — where political systems prioritize immediate gains over the well-being of younger and future citizens, with climate policy as the paradigmatic case. Mulkeen proposes age-weighted referendums on policies posing irreversible intergenerational risk and independent Intergenerational Commissions to review such policies, justifying these on both democratic and justice-based grounds ([Mulkeen 2025, *International Journal of Philosophical Studies*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)).
- **Stockemer (2025)** reviews the growing literature on age inequalities in political representation, synthesizing evidence that "people in political decision-making across the globe tend to be much older than the average voter" and that this "risks favouring policies geared towards the interests of older cohorts" while potentially alienating youth from voting. The review identifies multiple mechanisms — candidate selection, resource advantages, and agenda-setting bias — through which the age-turnout gradient translates into policy distortion ([Stockemer 2025, *Government and Opposition*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/government-and-opposition/article/age-inequalities-in-political-representation-a-review-article/E1FB531E2E6BD93582792E1D92AB18ED)).

The strongest version of the normativist case holds that the problem is not merely that elderly voters *happen* to outvote younger ones, but that the *structural design* of democratic institutions — single-member districts, candidate age minimums, campaign finance systems, and the absence of future-generations representation — systematically amplifies elder voices and silences youth, especially on long-horizon issues like climate, debt, and pension policy. The result is what Simon Caney calls "harmful short-termism," where democratic temporality privileges the living over the unborn ([Mulkeen 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)).

#### Italy, Germany, and the Comparative Pattern

Japan and Italy represent the most acute cases of "silver democracy" in comparative perspective. Japan, with the world's oldest population, has seen its pension and healthcare spending consume an ever-larger share of the national budget while youth employment and child welfare remain under-prioritized. McClean's research, however, suggests the problem is institutional rather than demographic: when young candidates win office, they deliver measurable increases in child welfare spending, implying that the barrier is not voter preference but candidate recruitment and party gatekeeping ([McClean project page](https://www.charlesmcclean.com/book-project)).

In Italy, the combination of a rapidly aging electorate, generous pension entitlements (indexed to wages rather than prices), and chronically high youth unemployment has produced what scholars have described as a "grey democracy" in which policy outputs systematically favor pensioners over younger workers. Germany faces analogous pressures: the ifo Institute has documented how German pension reform proposals meet with little public approval even as respondents "underestimate the unequal age distribution of eligible voters," which is characterized by a high proportion of older citizens ([ifo Institute, Jan. 2026](https://www.ifo.de/en/press-release/2026-01-23/reform-options-for-an-economically-sustainable-pension-in-germany-meet-with-little-approval)). In the United States, the structural pattern is replicated: the median age of the electorate exceeds 52, with primary electorates skewing to 65+, while policy outputs on housing, climate, and education funding systematically favor older homeowners' interests ([Pocasangre & Wahl 2025](https://protectdemocracy.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Pocasangre-and-Wahl-The-Age-Divide.pdf)).

#### Is the Bias a Democratic Deficit or a Turnout Artifact?

A critical question is whether gerontocracy is a *sui generis* democratic failure or simply an artifact of differential turnout that could be remedied by increasing youth participation. This distinction matters because it determines whether the appropriate remedy is age-based suffrage reform or non-age-based institutional change.

The gerontocracy-as-artifact view holds that if turnout were equalized across age groups — whether through lowering the voting age, preregistration, or civic mobilization — the age bias in policy would disappear. Descriptive age-based disparities in representation would persist (since older citizens would still run for office at higher rates), but the *electoral* mechanism driving policy bias would be neutralized. This view is supported by the evidence that when institutional barriers are lowered and young candidates are fielded, voters do not punish young candidates and policy outputs do shift toward youth interests ([McClean project page](https://www.charlesmcclean.com/book-project)).

The gerontocracy-as-deficit view, by contrast, argues that even with equal turnout, older citizens' structural advantages — greater financial resources for political donations, longer experience in political networks, incumbency advantages, and the "family solidarity" effect where parents vote for policies that benefit their adult children (who are often middle-aged themselves) — would still produce policy skew. On this view, the problem is not merely voting but the broader ecology of political influence, within which age confers compounding advantages that equal youth turnout alone cannot fully counter.

#### Non-Age-Based Reform Proposals

If the gerontocracy critique is at least partially valid, but age-based suffrage restrictions are rejected (as S1 and S5 argue they should be), several non-age-based institutional reforms have been proposed:

**Youth Quotas in Parliaments.** Juliana Bidadanure (2015) provides the most systematic theoretical defense, arguing that youth quotas are justified on *instrumental* grounds of intergenerational justice. Bidadanure acknowledges the standard objection to age quotas — that "everyone's turn will come" (Gosseries) — but argues this diachronic perspective is insufficient because *policy outcomes* with long time horizons (climate, debt, pensions) cannot be reversed when each age group's "turn" arrives. Her two-fold argument proceeds from (1) *substantive representation*: young parliamentarians enhance the quality of deliberation by introducing age-related concerns (affordable housing, education, unemployment, long-term ecological issues) that might otherwise be excluded from "the party packaging of political ideas"; and (2) *symbolic representation*: a visible youth presence in parliaments promotes "a community of political equals" and can stimulate youth political participation, countering alienation. Bidadanure draws on the Swiss 1990 nuclear-energy referendum (supported by 64% of 18–29-year-olds but rejected overall) to illustrate how youth preferences on long-horizon issues can be systematically outvoted ([Bidadanure 2015, *Intergenerational Justice Review* 1(1): 40–46](https://d-nb.info/1187186074/34)).

**Proportional Representation.** PR systems tend to produce younger and more representative parliaments than majoritarian systems, because party lists can be crafted to include younger candidates without requiring them to win plurality districts that privilege incumbents and established networks. Stockemer's (2025) review suggests that electoral-system design — more than any other institutional variable — shapes the age composition of parliaments ([Stockemer 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/government-and-opposition/article/age-inequalities-in-political-representation-a-review-article/E1FB531E2E6BD93582792E1D92AB18ED)). McClean's research on Japan confirms that(candidate-centered, single-member systems systematically disadvantage younger candidates by emphasizing personal wealth, experience, and name recognition ([McClean project page](https://www.charlesmcclean.com/book-project)).

**Deliberative Assemblies.** Citizens' assemblies selected by stratified random sortition offer a mechanism for injecting intergenerational perspectives into.policy-making without altering suffrage rules. Three landmark cases illustrate the model:

- The **UK Climate Assembly** (2020), convened by Parliament, brought together 108 randomly selected citizens (stratified by age, gender, ethnicity, geography, and climate attitudes) to deliberate on pathways to net-zero. The assembly's recommendations fed directly into parliamentary debates, demonstrating that ordinary citizens — including those underrepresented in regular electoral politics — can produce informed, long-horizon policy recommendations ([Climate Assembly UK; Elstub et al. 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1462901125002035)).
- The **Irish Constitutional Convention** (2012–2014) and its successor, the **Irish Citizens' Assembly** (2016–2018), deliberated on issues including marriage equality, the voting age, and the electoral system. The Citizens' Assembly's recommendation to repeal the abortion referendum ultimately precipitated the 2018 referendum, illustrating the agenda-setting power of deliberative bodies. The Convention explicitly considered the question of lowering the voting age to 16 and voted against it, demonstrating that citizens' assemblies can interject independent deliberative judgment into suffrage debates ([Irish Citizens' Assembly, COP-DEMOS database](https://cop-demos.jrc.ec.europa.eu/citizen-engagement-organisations/irish-citizens-assembly); [Frontiers in Political Science, 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2020.591983/full)).
- The **Belgian G1000** (2011) was a citizen-initiated summit of approximately 1,000 randomly selected Belgians who deliberated on social security, wealth distribution, and immigration during a period of governmental paralysis. The G1000 demonstrated that citizens may bypass representative institutions to produce consensus recommendations on exactly the intergenerational issues — social security and distribution — that gerontocracy theorists identify as most distorted ([G1000.org](https://www.g1000.org/en/cases/g1000-citizens-summit); [Binnema et al. 2022, *International Journal of Public Administration*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01900692.2020.1863425)).

**Candidate Age Requirements and Party Gatekeeping.** McClean's research suggests that lowering minimum age requirements for public office is a powerful, underutilized reform. Japan's case shows that when young candidates overcome institutional barriers and win office, they produce measurable policy shifts toward child welfare and demographic sustainability — without cutting elderly welfare, suggesting that youth representation does not necessarily trade off against elder interests ([McClean project page](https://www.charlesmcclean.com/book-project)).

#### Assessment: Descriptive Fact, Contested Normativity

The structural claim — that older citizens possess disproportionate electoral influence — is well-established empirically. The normative claim — that this constitutes an injustice requiring institutional remedy — remains contestable. The strongest version of the injustice argument holds that when democratic decisions impose *irreversible* harms on future citizens who cannot consent (climate change, unsustainable debt, biodiversity loss), the present-tense franchise systematically fails to protect those with the most at stake — a deficit that no amount of youth turnout equalization fully resolves, because the problem extends beyond voting to include lobbying, donation, and candidate pipeline effects ([Mulkeen 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13698230.2025.2541158)).

The strongest rebuttal holds that the gerontocracy frame risks misdiagnosing a wealth-inequality problem as an age-conflict problem, that older voters do not act as a monolithic bloc, and that the practical remedies proposed — age weighting, age caps on office, mandated age quotas — risk their own democratic legitimacy costs by introducing age-based exclusions that the broader normative framework (S1.1, S5.2) rejects. On this view, the better response is to lower institutional barriers to *entry* for young candidates and voters — through candidate age reductions, PR, preregistration, and civic education — rather than to impose age-based restrictions on *influence*.

The unresolved tension is this: if the problem is structural rather than merely turnout-based, then reforms that only equalize youth participation may be insufficient; yet reforms that weight by age or cap by age contradict the democratic principle of equal political dignity that the gerontocracy critique itself invokes. Deliberative assemblies and youth quotas offer a potential middle path — addressing the representational deficit without altering the suffrage franchise — but they remain supplementary rather than substitutive, lacking the binding authority to correct policy bias systematically. The question of whether any institution short of suffrage reform can adequately address structurally embedded intergenerational injustice remains open.

### Alternative Models: Demeny Voting, Proxy Voting, and Voting by Registration

The debate over age limits for voting tends to frame the choice as binary: either raise the minimum age, lower it, or eliminate it altogether. But a family of alternative models complicates this framing. These models propose mechanisms — proxy suffrage, registration-based enfranchisement, weighted plural votes — that attempt to resolve the tension between youth inclusion and the "one person, one vote" (OPOV) principle without simply moving a chronological boundary. This section examines three such alternatives: Demeny voting (parental proxy), the FRfG "voting by registration" model, and historical plural voting as a cautionary precedent.

#### Demeny Voting: Concept, History, and Proposals

Demeny voting, also called parental voting or family voting, allows custodial parents or guardians to cast proxy ballots on behalf of their minor children until they reach the standard voting age. The concept is named after demographer Paul [Demeny, who in 1986](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demeny_voting) argued that children "should not be left disenfranchised for some 18 years: let custodial parents exercise the children's voting rights until they come of age." Demeny's motivation was partly demographic — making "the political system more responsive to the young generation's interests" — and partly a response to the low-fertility policies of aging societies.

The idea predates Demeny. It was [regularly discussed in France in the 1920s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demeny_voting) and nearly adopted by the National Assembly, and was first debated in Germany in the 1910s. In Belgium, plural voting based on family status and property existed from 1894 to 1919, granting up to three supplementary votes to family heads, property owners, and diploma holders — a system abolished after general strikes in 1902 and 1913 ([Wikipedia: Plural voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural_voting)). More recently, the [German parliament (*Bundestag*) voted on introducing *Kinderwahlrecht* (children's voting rights) in 2003 and 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demeny_voting), but both proposals were defeated. Japan has discussed Demeny voting as a potential answer to its aging population; a [2011 conference at Hitotsubashi University's Centre for Intergenerational Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demeny_voting) surveyed voter attitudes and found that a considerable percentage of respondents would cast their children's votes differently from their own — a finding that undercuts the assumption that parental proxy faithfully transmits children's preferences.

The most concrete political proposal came from Hungary. In 2011, the ruling [Fidesz party inserted a Demeny voting provision into a new constitution](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2011/apr/17/hungary-mothers-get-extra-votes), with senior official József Szájer arguing that "some 20% of society are children" who deserved representation. Concerns that Roma families would gain disproportionate political power led Fidesz to restrict the initial proposal to a single proxy vote per mother, and the provision was ultimately moved from the constitutional text to ordinary legislation. [Fidesz MEP György Schöpflin expressed skepticism](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2011/apr/17/hungary-mothers-get-extra-votes), stating: "I don't think parents would be able to conceptualise their children's interest distinct from how they already vote." University of Chicago law professor Tom Ginsburg criticized the proposal as [harkening back to "19th-century use of property qualifications to restrict the franchise"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2011/apr/17/hungary-mothers-get-extra-votes) and undermining the political rights of those without families.

Pieter Vanhuysse of the European Centre for Social Welfare Policy and Research [argued in 2013](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demeny_voting) that in Austria — where policies are relatively pro-elderly — "the time is ripe for at least opening a clear-headed and empirically informed democratic debate about the radical idea of giving each parent one half extra vote." Belgian parliamentary discussions of proxy or family voting have recurred in the 2020s, reflecting broader European interest in mechanisms that would weight electoral influence toward younger and future generations without directly lowering the voting age.

#### Economic Modeling: Overlapping-Generations Evidence

[Bonatti and Lorenzetti (2023)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecosys/v47y2023i4s0939362523000900.html) model the long-run economic implications of Demeny voting using an overlapping-generations (OLG) framework with endogenous fertility. Their analysis, published in *Economic Systems* 47(1):101151, examines the effects on population growth, capital accumulation, output, and consumption per worker. The OLG model demonstrates that Demeny voting would shift intergenerational fiscal redistribution by giving parents — who bear the costs of child-rearing — additional electoral weight linked to their children's interests. Specifically, the model shows that the introduction of Demeny voting would increase investment in children and would tend to expand the political base supporting education, childcare, and family policy spending, at the expense of transfers skewed toward elderly constituencies. Capital accumulation effects depend on the relative weight of young-family versus elderly voting blocs: when Demeny voting is implemented, the income redistribution operated by government tax and transfer policies shifts toward programs benefiting younger cohorts.

A complementary model by [Grechyna (2024)](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/312063/1/cesifo1_wp11553.pdf) examines fertility and welfare under Demeny voting in a probabilistic voting framework, finding that since Demeny voting raises investment in children, it tends to produce higher fertility rates — potentially creating a virtuous cycle where political incentives align with demographic sustainability. These theoretical models, however, assume perfectly aligned parent-child political preferences or at least rational parental proxy behavior, an assumption that empirical evidence on intra-household political heterogeneity calls into question.

#### The Philosophical and Legal Debate

The most sustained legal-theoretical treatment of Demeny voting is [Kleinfeld and Sachs (2025)](https://scholarship.law.nd.edu/ndlr/vol100/iss3/5/), "Give Parents the Vote" (*Notre Dame Law Review* 100:1201). They argue that children constitute 23% of all citizens, "have distinct interests," and "already count for electoral districting" but lack proportional influence at the polls. Their proposal would allow voting parents to cast proxy ballots on behalf of minor children and would also apply to court-appointed guardians of those mentally incapacitated. Crucially, they frame it as reform achievable entirely at the state level — "a reform that any state can adopt, both for its own elections and for its representation in Congress and the Electoral College."

The proposal drew immediate scholarly responses in the same journal issue. [Fishkin (2025)](https://scholarship.law.nd.edu/ndlr/vol100/iss3/6/), "It Takes a Village . . . But Let the Teenagers Vote," argues that Kleinfeld and Sachs misconstrue the nature of voting. Voting, Fishkin contends, is "a collective act by which a political community makes collective choices" — unlike medical or financial decisions for children, voting involves obligations to the community as a whole, not merely the advancement of individual interests. Fishkin cites empirical evidence that high school students possess voting competence equivalent to young adults and argues that direct teenage suffrage is preferable to parental proxy. [Stephanopoulos (2025)](https://scholarship.law.nd.edu/ndlr/vol100/iss4/), "Give Young Adults The Vote," supplements this by modeling the median voter under a proxy-voting regime and concluding it would closely resemble the median voter if children could vote directly — suggesting that proxy mechanisms achieve empirically similar outcomes to straightforward age reduction, while introducing potential abuse and bureaucratic complexity.

[Könczöl (2023)](https://journals.openedition.org/revus/9753), writing in the *Revue européenne des sciences sociales* (Revus 21/51), provides a systematic critical assessment of parental proxy voting, examining the logical relations between arguments for and against. His analysis engages the core tension: proxy voting implicitly concedes that children have interests worthy of representation while denying them agency in articulating those interests. The proxy model also raises a distinctive OPOV problem — it grants parents two or more votes, depending on the number of children, in a manner functionally analogous to the plural voting systems abandoned in the twentieth century.

The FRfG policy paper acknowledges these objections. Its [section on proxy voting](https://intergenerationaljustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/PP_Voting-Rights.pdf) presents Demeny voting and other parental proxy systems as a response to the intergenerational representation gap — but the paper ultimately distances itself from the proxy model, noting that proxy suffrage "is not any stranger than when adults delegate political authority to their elected representative" (citing Stefan Olsson) yet risks entrenching family-based political power in ways that may not serve children's autonomous interests. Jane Rutherford (1998) proposed five criteria for proxy selection — shared stake, personal familiarity, child access, accountability, and emotional bond — but [John Wall (2014)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demeny_voting) went further, proposing "proxy-claim suffrage," in which a child could actively claim their right to vote from a proxy-holder rather than passively waiting for age-based qualification. This twist attempts to reconcile proxy representation with child agency, but it raises practical difficulties: who adjudicates competing proxy claims, and at what age does a child's override of parental proxy take precedence?

#### "Voting by Registration": The FRfG Model

The Foundation for the Rights of Future Generations (FRfG) proposes an alternative that dispensing with both chronological floors and proxy mechanisms: a system of "voting by registration," in which [any citizen regardless of age can register to vote when they feel ready to do so](https://intergenerationaljustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/PP_Voting-Rights.pdf). This model abolishes the categorical age floor entirely while retaining exclusion only on demonstrated incapacity — meaning cognitive inability to make an electoral choice, assessed individually, would be the sole basis for denial of registration, not age.

Institutionally, the FRfG model envisions several design features: easily accessible and youth-friendly voter registration processes, potentially in partnership with educational institutions; legislative protections against voter exploitation, harassment, or discrimination; voter education materials geared toward younger voters; polling stations staffed by personnel trained to assist first-time voters; and citizenship curriculum requirements to ensure informed participation. The model also recommends youth advisory councils or forums as a complement to electoral participation and ongoing research into how youth voting affects political priorities and turnout.

The voting-by-registration model aims to avoid the dependency risks inherent in proxy systems — parents cannot manipulate or override a child's self-registered vote — while preserving the legitimacy of the franchise through demonstrated readiness rather than an arbitrary chronological threshold. Whether this model is administratively feasible at scale remains untested; no jurisdiction has implemented a registration-without-age-floor system. The closest real-world analogue is flexible voter registration systems that allow preregistration before the voting age (as in some U.S. states), but those still operate within a categorical age floor.

#### Historical Plural Voting as Precedent and Warning

Historical plural voting provides both a precedent and a cautionary tale for age-weighted or proxy models. In the [United Kingdom, university constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural_voting) allowed graduates to vote in both a university constituency and their home constituency. Property owners could vote in constituencies where their property was located in addition to their residential constituency. As Shaw-Lefevre noted in 1892, some individuals held five votes, and [one clergyman obtained the ability to vote in forty different places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural_voting). These practices were abolished for parliamentary elections by the [Representation of the People Act 1948](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural_voting), first applied in the 1950 general election, and for local government elections by the 1969 Act.

Belgium's plural voting system (1894–1919) granted up to three supplementary votes based on education, property, and family-head status — a system [abolished after general strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural_voting) in 1902 and 1913. The trajectory is revealing: each instance of plural voting was dismantled under OPOV logic, on the grounds that weighted votes based on ascribed characteristics — property, education, family status — were incompatible with equal political dignity. Demeny voting, by assigning proxy votes based on parental status, enters territory analogous to these abandoned systems. Whether the analogy holds depends on whether parental proxy is understood as a form of dependent representation (legitimate when the proxy stands in for a person who cannot act for themselves) or as a form of weighted plural voting (illegitimate because it grants some citizens more votes than others on the basis of family composition). The distinction turns on whether one regards children as incapable of political agency in principle or as merely lacking the chronological label that the state attaches to competence.

[Mulligan (2018)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural_voting) has proposed a twenty-first-century version of plural voting using a mathematical algorithm to determine voter ability and assign additional votes accordingly — a proposal that critics characterize as epistocratic and that has not been adopted anywhere.

#### Comparative Assessment: Legitimacy, Feasibility, and OPOV

The three alternative models can be compared across three dimensions: democratic legitimacy, practical feasibility, and compatibility with OPOV.

**Democratic legitimacy.** Demeny voting derives its legitimacy from the affected-interests principle: children are affected by political decisions and deserve representation, and parents are the natural agents for that interest. But the model faces Fishkin's objection that voting is a collective rather than individual interest-representation act, and the FRfG concerns that proxy systems entrench family-based power. Voting-by-registration derives legitimacy from self-determination: each citizen's readiness to vote is individually established, avoiding both the chronological paternalism of age floors and the agency-denial of proxy systems. Historical plural voting was delegitimized because it assigned political weight based on statuses — property, education, family composition — that democratic societies came to regard as irrelevant to political equality. The risk for Demeny voting is that it repeats this move by making parental status a basis for additional votes, even if the justification is children's welfare rather than parental privilege.

**Practical feasibility.** Demeny voting requires mechanisms for registering children's proxies, allocating fractional or whole additional votes, verifying custodial authority, and determining when proxy registration terminates (at the standard voting age). No modern democracy has implemented the system at scale, though the Hungarian proposal reached the legislative stage. Voting-by-registration requires individual readiness assessment without an age proxy, raising the question of who evaluates "readiness" and whether the process could be manipulated. Age-weighted voting of the kind discussed by [Poama and Volacu (2023)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.162.11.2094) requires a principled basis for assigning vote weights by age — a demand that exposes the epistemic difficulty of calibrating political competence or affectedness by cohort.

**Compatibility with OPOV.** Strictly construed, OPOV prohibits any citizen from casting more than one vote. Demeny voting clearly violates this by giving parents proxy votes. Voting-by-registration is compatible with OPOV, as each registered voter — regardless of age — casts exactly one vote. Historical plural voting was abolished precisely because it violated OPOV in the same way that Demeny voting would: some voters had more ballots than others on the basis of ascribed characteristics. The正当 counter-argument holds that proxy votes for children are not truly "parental" votes but rather dependent representation of the child's own single vote — the parent exercises the child's franchise, not an additional one of their own. Whether this conceptual distinction withstands scrutiny depends on whether one accepts that a proxy's choices can meaningfully be attributed to the principal in a way that preserves OPOV's underlying egalitarian principle.

The evidence suggests that while proxy and registration models offer creative responses to the intergenerational representation gap — and OLG modeling confirms they could shift fiscal redistribution toward younger cohorts — each faces significant democratic-legitimacy challenges that age-based reforms (lowering the minimum) do not. The alternatives occupy a terrain between inclusivity and OPOV where the most plausible justification for deviation from strict OPOV is that certain citizens cannot act for themselves and require dependent representation. This justification, however, applies far more coherently to addressing cognitive decline (where individualized capacity assessment, not chronological proxy, is the relevant mechanism) than to enfranchising children, for whom direct suffrage at 16 or even lower is empirically viable, as the Austrian and Belgian evidence indicates.

## Inclusivity vs. Informed Decision-Making: Balancing the Values

### The Competence Principle and the Epistocratic Challenge

The epistocratic challenge to universal suffrage holds that if democratic legitimacy depends on competent decision-making, and many voters — young and old alike — lack sufficient political knowledge, then competence-based restrictions on the franchise, including age limits, may be justified. This section reconstructs the strongest version of that challenge, examines its empirical foundations, and presents the principal democratic rebuttals. The analysis is framed as a set of open questions rather than a settled conclusion.

#### Brennan's Epistocracy and the Jury Argument

Jason Brennan's *Against Democracy* (2016) provides the most influential contemporary defense of epistocracy — a system in which "votes are allocated to those who possess measurable knowledge or competence" ([Brennan, *Against Democracy*, Princeton University Press, 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Against_Democracy)). Brennan categorizes citizens into three types: "Hobbits" (politically indifferent and ignorant), "Hooligans" (biased and irrational), and "Vulcans" (disinterested, evidence-driven reasoners). He argues that most citizens fall into the first two categories and that their political participation produces systematically worse outcomes than a more restricted franchise would yield.

Brennan's most philosophically ambitious argument is the **jury argument**, which he first developed in "The Right to a Competent Electorate" (2011, *Philosophical Quarterly* 61:700–724) and extended in *Against Democracy*. The argument proceeds by analogy. Juries make morally momentous decisions that are involuntarily imposed on defendants; most people intuitively accept that defendants have a right to a competent jury. Brennan identifies four structural features shared by jury trials and democratic politics: (1) both involve morally momentous decisions, (2) both can significantly affect life prospects, (3) both claim sole jurisdiction over the decision, and (4) both impose outcomes involuntarily through force or threats of force. Given this symmetry, Brennan argues that consistency demands we recognize a right to a competent electorate just as we recognize a right to a competent jury. If suffrage should be restricted to the competent in the jury context, it should be so restricted in the electoral context as well ([Kogelmann & Carroll 2024, *Inquiry*](https://www.briankogelmann.com/uploads/5/5/8/6/55864067/there_is_no_right_to_a_competent_electorate.pdf)).

Brennan's epistocratic proposals include **restricted suffrage** (citizens must pass an examination to gain the right to vote) and **plural voting** (citizens receive additional votes for passing knowledge tests). While Brennan does not frame his argument specifically around age limits, the logic of his competence principle applies *pari passu* to any group whose political knowledge or decision-making capacity is systematically deficient — including, potentially, both adolescents and the cognitively impaired elderly. The age-neutrality of the competence criterion is a double-edged feature: it avoids the arbitrariness of age-based line-drawing, but it also implies that if competence is the real criterion, age limits are merely a proxy that may be both over- and under-inclusive.

#### The Rational Ignorance Problem

The epistocratic challenge draws substantial force from the **rational ignorance** problem, first formalized by Anthony Downs in *An Economic Theory of Democracy* (1957). Downs observed that because an individual vote has a near-zero probability of affecting an election outcome, the expected benefit of acquiring political knowledge is vanishingly small, while the cost of doing so is substantial. Rational citizens therefore remain ignorant of politics — not because they are unintelligent, but because the incentive structure of mass elections makes political ignorance a utility-maximizing strategy ([Downs 1957](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_ignorance)). Brennan extends this insight, arguing that rational ignorance is not a marginal phenomenon but a structural feature of democracy: "the majority of voters pay no attention to the details of the election or the issues at stake" ([Brennan 2016: 158](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Against_Democracy)).

If rational ignorance is widespread, the epistocratic argument runs, then universal suffrage empowers large numbers of citizens who lack the knowledge to vote competently, and the resulting policy outcomes are systematically worse than they would be under a more informed electorate. This argument applies across age groups: there is no *a priori* reason to think that 18-year-olds are less rationally ignorant than 40-year-olds, or that 70-year-olds are less susceptible to cognitive bias than 50-year-olds. The competence principle, if taken seriously, would seem to require an individualized assessment rather than an age-based proxy.

#### Empirical Evidence on Political Knowledge Across Age Groups

The empirical foundation for the competence-based challenge rests on decades of survey research documenting low levels of political knowledge among mass electorates. The foundational baseline is Delli Carpini and Keeter's *What Americans Know About Politics and Why It Matters* (1996), which found that large portions of the American public could not answer basic factual questions about political institutions, current events, or the positions of candidates. Their work established that political knowledge is unevenly distributed across the population, with significant gaps by education, gender, and age ([Delli Carpini & Keeter 1996](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/pdf/10.1146/annurev.polisci.4.1.217)).

The relationship between age and political knowledge, however, is not straightforward. Research consistently finds that **older voters tend to have higher levels of factual political knowledge** than younger voters, a pattern attributable to greater life experience, longer exposure to political information, and cohort effects. However, this knowledge advantage does not necessarily translate into better decision-making: older voters may also exhibit stronger partisan biases, greater resistance to new information, and — in cases of cognitive decline — diminished capacity to process political information. Younger voters, while lower in factual knowledge, may be more adaptable and less locked into partisan heuristics. The empirical picture is thus mixed: age correlates with political knowledge, but the correlation is imperfect, non-linear, and does not map cleanly onto decision-making quality in a way that would justify a bright-line age threshold.

The critical question for the epistocratic argument is not whether political knowledge varies by age — it does — but whether the variation is large enough, and the threshold clear enough, to sustain a categorical exclusion. If, as Delli Carpini and Keeter's data suggest, political knowledge is broadly deficient across *all* age groups, then age-based restrictions address only a fraction of the competence problem while imposing the costs of exclusion on a large and heterogeneous population.

#### López-Guerra's Framework: Competence, Capacity, and the Allocation/Operation Distinction

Claudio López-Guerra's *Democracy and Disenfranchisement: The Morality of Electoral Exclusions* (2014, Oxford University Press) provides the most systematic philosophical analysis of competence-based electoral exclusions, including age limits. López-Guerra challenges what he calls the "Conventional Suffrage Doctrine" — the view that minors, the mentally impaired, non-citizen residents, and convicted criminals should be excluded, while ignorant people and expatriates should be included. His "Critical Suffrage Doctrine" inverts this conventional wisdom: some of those typically excluded (e.g., children) should be included, and some typically included (e.g., ignorant voters) should be excluded ([López-Guerra 2014, reviewed in *Contemporary Political Theory* 16:137–140](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/cpt.2015.53)).

López-Guerra's framework introduces a crucial distinction between **franchise capacity** (who deserves to be enfranchised) and **electoral process fairness** (whose vote would not damage electoral integrity). He argues that children who possess "the moral and rational faculty to value the position of being a voter and thus to be hurt by exclusion" should be enfranchised on capacity grounds, while acknowledging that operational considerations — such as susceptibility to coercion — may justify temporary exclusion. Drawing on developmental psychology, López-Guerra contends that children as young as ten have acquired the "natural brain capacity" that qualifies them to vote, and that age is always an imperfect proxy for capacity. If age is to be used, he argues, over-inclusiveness is preferable to under-inclusiveness ([López-Guerra 2014, reviewed in *Contemporary Political Theory*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/cpt.2015.53)).

For the competence question more broadly, López-Guerra's framework implies that the burden of proof falls on those who would exclude: the question is not "who is competent enough to vote?" but "who lacks the capacity to such a degree that exclusion is justified?" This framing is significant because it shifts the epistocratic argument from a general claim about voter ignorance to a specific claim about *incapacity* — a much higher threshold that most age-based exclusions cannot meet.

#### Relational Egalitarianism and the Competence Question

The relational egalitarian framework, developed by Niko Kolodny (2014), Daniel Viehoff (2014), and Andreas Bengtson (2022), offers a distinctive lens on the competence question. Relational egalitarianism holds that justice requires that people **relate as equals** — that social and political institutions must not create or perpetuate relations of hierarchy, domination, or inferiority ([Bengtson 2022](https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/ergo/article/id/7305/); [Kolodny 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0032321719889870)).

Applied to the competence question, relational egalitarianism generates a tension. On the one hand, competence-based exclusion — whether by age or by knowledge test — creates a relation of political superiority and inferiority that is difficult to reconcile with equal political dignity. If some citizens are deemed competent to vote and others are not, the excluded group is marked as politically inferior, which is precisely the kind of status hierarchy that relational egalitarianism condemns. On the other hand, relational egalitarians also care about **equal political power**, and if competence is unequally distributed, formal equality of voting power may mask substantive inequality in political influence. Bengtson (2022) argues that relational egalitarians must grapple with whether differential voting weights — which could compensate for competence differences — are compatible with relating as equals, or whether they too create objectionable hierarchies ([Bengtson 2022, *Ergo*](https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/ergo/article/id/7305/)).

The relational egalitarian framework thus does not straightforwardly resolve the competence question. It provides strong reasons to resist competence-based exclusion as a *stigma* of political inferiority, but it also raises the question of whether *equal* voting power in the face of *unequal* competence is genuinely egalitarian. This tension remains unresolved in the literature and constitutes a key site for future research.

#### Kogelmann's Rebuttal: The Cost of Enforcing Competence Rights

Brian Kogelmann and Jeffrey Carroll's "There Is No Right to a Competent Electorate" (2024, *Inquiry*) takes Brennan's jury argument head-on and rejects its conclusion. Their argument proceeds in two steps. First, they accept the right to a competent jury. Second, they deny that the analogy to electoral competence holds, on the grounds that the **enforcement costs** and **harm prevention** differ dramatically between the two domains.

The right to a competent jury is cheap to enforce — jury selection is a bounded, individualized process — and the harm it prevents (wrongful conviction or acquittal) is severe and concentrated on a single defendant. The right to a competent electorate, by contrast, would require a mass-scale competence assessment apparatus of enormous cost and complexity, and the harm it prevents (suboptimal policy outcomes) is diffuse, probabilistic, and contested. Kogelmann and Carroll argue that before affirming a right, we must examine "how much it costs to enforce [it] and the significance of the harm enforcement prevents." On this cost-benefit analysis, the right to a competent jury is cheap and prevents grave harm; the right to a competent electorate is expensive and prevents comparatively less severe harm. The asymmetry breaks the analogy ([Kogelmann & Carroll 2024, *Inquiry*](https://www.briankogelmann.com/uploads/5/5/8/6/55864067/there_is_no_right_to_a_competent_electorate.pdf)).

Applied to age limits, Kogelmann's argument implies that even if there is a theoretical case for competence-based exclusion, the institutional machinery required to implement it — individualized testing, appeals processes, periodic reassessment — would be so costly and so susceptible to manipulation that it is practically unworkable. Age limits, on this view, are not a solution to the competence problem but a crude and potentially discriminatory shortcut.

#### Democratic Rebuttals

The democratic response to the epistocratic challenge rests on four interconnected arguments:

**First**, the "no right to a competent electorate" argument (Kogelmann 2024) holds that the enforcement costs of competence testing are prohibitive and that the harm prevented is insufficient to justify the institutional apparatus required. If competence is the criterion, then the state would need to administer, monitor, and adjudicate competence assessments for millions of citizens — a system that would inevitably be biased, manipulable, and democratically illegitimate.

**Second**, competence tests for suffrage are **biased and unadministrable**. As Brennan himself acknowledges, the "demographic objection" notes that political knowledge is unevenly distributed across demographic groups by race, class, and education. A competence test would disproportionately exclude marginalized citizens, reproducing existing inequalities under a veneer of epistemic neutrality. No democracy has ever administered a suffrage test that was not, in practice, discriminatory in its effects.

**Third**, the "one person one vote" (OPOV) principle serves as a **safeguard against elite capture**. The historical trajectory of suffrage expansion — from property qualifications to racial exclusion to gender exclusion — demonstrates that every restriction on the franchise has been used to entrench the power of dominant groups. Competence tests, even if designed in good faith, would be vulnerable to the same dynamic: those who design the tests would define "competence" in ways that favor their own supporters and disadvantage their opponents. OPOV, for all its epistemic imperfections, is a bulwark against this form of elite capture.

**Fourth**, political knowledge is **not uniformly age-correlated** in a way that would justify age-based restrictions. While older voters tend to have higher levels of factual political knowledge, the relationship is non-linear, cohort-dependent, and does not map cleanly onto decision-making quality. Younger voters may lack factual knowledge but possess other decision-making virtues — adaptability, openness to new information, longer time horizons — that are politically relevant. The empirical evidence does not support a bright-line age threshold as a proxy for competence.

#### The Unresolved Tension

The epistocratic challenge raises a genuine and unresolved tension in democratic theory. If democracy's legitimacy depends on competent decision-making, and competence is unevenly distributed, then universal suffrage appears to rest on a fiction — the fiction that all citizens are equally capable of political judgment. Yet the alternatives — competence tests, age limits, epistocratic weighting — introduce their own pathologies: bias, administrative complexity, elite capture, and the stigma of political inferiority.

López-Guerra's framework offers a partial resolution by distinguishing between **capacity** (who deserves the franchise) and **operational fairness** (whose vote would damage electoral integrity). On this view, the competence question is not a single question but two: a question about who has the moral and rational faculty to be a voter, and a question about whether their participation would undermine the electoral process. Age-based exclusion may fail the first test (many minors have the capacity) while operational concerns (coercion, dependency) may justify temporary exclusion without permanent disenfranchisement. But this framework, too, leaves open the question of how to assess capacity without individualized testing — and whether individualized testing is compatible with democratic equality.

The relational egalitarian framework deepens the puzzle. If relating as equals requires both equal political power and the absence of status hierarchies, then competence-based exclusion is problematic on both grounds: it creates a hierarchy of competence and it distributes political power unequally. But if competence is genuinely and substantially unequal, then formal equality of voting power may itself be a form of substantive inequality — a "leveling down" that treats unequal citizens as equal in ways that perpetuate injustice. Whether relational egalitarianism can resolve this dilemma — or whether it merely restates it — remains one of the most important open questions in the philosophical literature on suffrage.

### The Inclusivity Principle: Suffrage as Equal Dignity and the Slippery Slope

The inclusivity principle holds that universal suffrage is not merely a procedural mechanism for aggregating preferences but an expression of equal political dignity. On this view, excluding any group of citizens from the franchise on competence grounds imposes a stigma of political inferiority that is incompatible with the foundational democratic commitment to equal citizenship. This section reconstructs the strongest inclusivity-based arguments against age-based suffrage restrictions — both minimum and maximum — and examines whether the principle can accommodate any competence-based exclusion without collapsing into the epistocratic position it seeks to resist.

#### Suffrage as a Badge of Equal Citizenship

The idea that voting is a "badge of equal citizenship" has deep roots in American constitutional theory. Kenneth Karst argued in his influential 1977 Harvard Law Review foreword that the Equal Protection Clause embodies a principle of "equal citizenship" under which the franchise functions as a marker of full membership in the political community — a status whose denial carries an expressive harm distinct from any material disadvantage ([Karst, "Equal Citizenship Under the Fourteenth Amendment," 91 Harv. L. Rev. 1 (1977)](https://illinoislawreview.org/wp-content/ilr-content/articles/2003/3/Hutchinson.pdf)). The concept resonates beyond U.S. jurisprudence: the UNICEF Innocenti synthesis of the children's voting debate frames suffrage as a signal that "societies are committed to realizing [the CRC], listening to [children's] views and valuing their aspirations" ([UNICEF Innocenti, "Should Children Vote?"](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)). On this account, the harm of exclusion is not merely that the excluded lose policy influence — it are declared, in effect, to be less than full political persons.

This dignitary argument applies with equal force to both minimum and maximum age limits. If excluding 16-year-olds on the ground of insufficient cognitive maturity brands them as political inferiors, excluding 75-year-olds on the ground of cognitive decline does the same to them. The asymmetry is that no democracy currently imposes a competence test on young voters, so applying one only to elderly voters raises acute equal protection concerns — a point developed further in S3.2.

#### The "Lack of Exit" Argument

Albert Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970) provides a powerful framework for understanding why democratic membership is different from other associations in which competence-based exclusion might be unproblematic. Hirschman's core insight is that when members of an organization perceive decline, they face a choice: exit (withdraw from the relationship) or voice (attempt to repair it through articulation of grievance). Crucially, the availability of exit reduces the incentive to use voice — and vice versa ([Hirschman, *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exit,_Voice,_and_Loyalty)).

Applied to democratic politics, the argument acquires special force because citizens generally cannot exit the state. Emigration is costly, often impractical, and — for many — not a genuine option. Hirschman himself noted that those with "nowhere else to go" do not have their voices marginalized; rather, their voices become amplified, because voice is the only remaining mechanism of influence ([Hirschman (1970), cited in Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exit,_Voice,_and_Loyalty)). The "lack of exit" argument thus strengthens the case for universal suffrage: if citizens cannot leave the political community, excluding them from voice is especially harmful, because it leaves them with no mechanism whatsoever to protect their interests. This logic applies *a fortiori* to young people, who face the greatest barriers to emigration and the longest horizon of exposure to political decisions made without their input.

The argument also cuts against maximum age limits. An elderly citizen who is disenfranchised on cognitive grounds cannot "exit" the polity — but unlike a young person, they also cannot anticipate future enfranchisement. The lack of exit is permanent, making the denial of voice total.

#### Self-Government, Consent, and Democratic Legitimacy

The self-government argument holds that democratic legitimacy derives from the consent of the governed, and that age-based exclusion undermines the claim that government rests on the consent of all who are subject to it. If laws bind citizens who had no say in their making, the legitimacy of those laws is compromised — not merely as a matter of fairness but as a matter of democratic principle. The UNICEF report notes that "only one fifth of adolescents are eligible to vote in most countries (those 18 and 19) and yet they make up 16 per cent of the global population," meaning that a substantial share of the governed are governed without voice ([UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)).

This argument supports lowering the minimum voting age: if 16-year-olds are subject to laws, taxed (indirectly), and conscripted (in some jurisdictions), their exclusion from consent is a democratic deficit. The argument's extension to maximum age limits is more contested. If cognitive decline genuinely prevents meaningful consent, the self-government rationale might seem to permit exclusion. But the inclusivity counter-argument responds that the appropriate remedy is not categorical exclusion but individualized accommodation — assisted voting, advance directives, or capacity assessment — that preserves the franchise for those who can exercise it meaningfully while providing support for those who struggle (see S3.2).

#### The Slippery Slope in Both Directions

The inclusivity principle generates a "slippery slope" argument that operates in two directions simultaneously.

**Downward (minimum age):** If 16-year-olds lack sufficient political knowledge to vote, why not 14-year-olds? If 14-year-olds, why not 12-year-olds? The UNICEF report notes that "requirements on educational attainment and political knowledge do not currently constitute barriers to voting rights for adults in most countries in the world, and therefore should also not hinder access to voting for adolescents" ([UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)). The point is not that knowledge is irrelevant — it is that imposing a knowledge threshold only on young voters, while exempting the adult electorate from any equivalent test, is discriminatory in effect if not in intent. Empirically, studies in Austria and Brazil have found that 16–17-year-olds demonstrate political knowledge and vote quality comparable to older cohorts (see S2.2 for the detailed natural-experiment evidence). If a political knowledge threshold were applied universally, a substantial portion of the adult electorate would fail — a finding that undermines the case for applying it selectively to youth.

**Upward (maximum age):** If elderly voters are excluded for cognitive decline, why not other cognitively impaired groups? Why not apply competence tests to all voters, regardless of age? The inclusivity principle reveals the asymmetry: no democracy requires a competence test for young voters, so imposing one only on elderly voters raises the same equal protection concerns. The slippery slope here is that age-based cognitive exclusion is a proxy for a broader competence-based exclusion that, if applied consistently, would disenfranchise a significant share of the electorate — and would likely be applied discriminatorily along lines of race, class, and political affiliation (see S3.2).

#### Inclusion as Civic Education

A distinctively democratic argument for inclusivity holds that enfranchisement is not merely a right but a practice — that the act of voting cultivates democratic habits, political interest, and civic competence. Excluding young people from the franchise, on this view, does not merely deny them a right; it breeds alienation and disengagement that persist into adulthood. The UNICEF report cites evidence that "expanding adolescent franchise has the potential to instill greater political interest as voting is a habitual behavior, with younger first-time voters more likely to vote again in subsequent elections," and that "a study of the five Latin American countries that have lowered the voting age to 16 showed that enfranchisement had a significant impact on trust in political parliaments and parties" ([UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)). In the United States, a study of preregistration (registering individuals before they are eligible to vote) found that "it promoted higher youth turnout and politicians were more responsive to issues that matter for the young, such as higher education spending" ([UNICEF Innocenti, citing AEA preregistration study](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)).

This argument inverts the epistocratic logic: rather than restricting the franchise to those who have already demonstrated competence, it holds that the franchise itself is a mechanism for developing competence. The civic education argument thus supports lowering the minimum age as a means of cultivating democratic citizenship — and it applies *a fortiori* to maximum age limits, since excluding elderly voters on cognitive grounds would further alienate a group already at risk of political marginalization.

#### Reconciling Inclusivity with Competence-Based Exclusion

The most difficult challenge for the inclusivity principle is whether it can accommodate *any* competence-based exclusion — for example, the severely cognitively impaired — without collapsing into the epistocratic position it opposes. Several considerations are relevant.

First, there is a principled difference between excluding for lack of capacity that is *remediable* (youth, where cognitive development is ongoing and the excluded will eventually gain the franchise) and excluding for *immutable or irreversible* characteristics. The stigma of exclusion is less severe when the excluded can anticipate future inclusion — a point that supports lowering the minimum age but does not directly address maximum age limits.

Second, the inclusivity principle can accommodate competence-based exclusion only if the competence standard is applied universally and non-discriminatorily. As S3.2 details, no democracy currently imposes a competence test on young voters; imposing one only on elderly voters is inherently asymmetrical. The relational egalitarian framework — which holds that justice requires people to "relate as equals" in their political relationships ([Bengtson (2025), "Can Relational Egalitarians Supply Both an Account of Democratic Equality and a Theory of Justice?"](https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/ergo/article/id/7305/); [Kolodny (2014), cited in Bengtson & Albertsen (2023)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w)) — suggests that unequal political power is incompatible with relating as social equals. On this view, any competence-based exclusion that creates a class of political inferiors violates the relational egalitarian requirement, regardless of how "objective" the competence standard appears.

Third, the inclusivity principle can be reconciled with the concern for severely cognitively impaired persons through *accommodation rather than exclusion*. Assisted voting mechanisms, advance directives, and proxy representation (see S4.3 on Demeny voting) can preserve the franchise for those who struggle with the mechanics of voting without imposing a categorical age or capacity threshold. The UK's approach under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 — which holds that everyone with dementia has the right to vote regardless of mental capacity — exemplifies this accommodationist approach (see S3.2).

#### López-Guerra and the Limits of Competence-Based Exclusion

Claudio López-Guerra's *Democracy and Disenfranchisement* (2014) provides the most systematic treatment of competence-based electoral exclusions, including age. His "Critical Suffrage Doctrine" holds that disenfranchisement is justified only when it protects the democratic process itself — not when it merely excludes those deemed incompetent — and places the burden of proof on those who would exclude (see S5.1 for the full framework). This framework opposes age-based exclusion of both young and old, because neither group's participation can be shown to harm democracy, and because the exclusion itself imposes a dignitary harm that outweighs any speculative epistemic benefit. López-Guerra's framework also reinforces the slippery slope concern: if competence is the criterion, the standard must be applied consistently across all potential exclusions, and the administrative and discriminatory risks of doing so render the epistocratic project self-defeating. On this account, the inclusivity principle is not merely a counterweight to epistocracy — it is the default position from which any exclusion must be justified.

#### Empirical Evidence: Youth Engagement and Alienation

The empirical evidence on whether lowering the voting age improves youth political engagement and reduces alienation is suggestive but not yet conclusive. The Austrian case (2007) found that 16–17-year-olds turned out at rates comparable to or higher than 18–21-year-olds, and that their vote quality matched (see S2.2 for detailed findings). The Scottish independence referendum (2014) saw high youth engagement, and the five Latin American countries that lowered the voting age showed significant gains in trust in political institutions ([UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)). However, the evidence base remains limited by the small number of jurisdictions that have implemented the reform, the novelty effects that may inflate first-election turnout, and the difficulty of isolating the effect of the voting age from other institutional and cultural factors.

The UK's pending reform — which would enfranchise approximately 1.7 million young people in UK-wide elections (see S1.2 for legislative status and timeline) — represents the largest-scale natural experiment yet. As of mid-2026, no implementation effects are observable. The UK case will be critical for testing whether the inclusivity and civic-education effects observed in smaller jurisdictions (Austria, Brazil, Scottish devolution) scale to a national electorate — and whether the slippery slope concerns raised by opponents materialize or dissolve in practice.

#### Unresolved Questions

The inclusivity principle raises several questions that the evidence cannot yet resolve:

1. **Is there a principled stopping point?** If the stigma of exclusion is the harm, does it matter whether the excluded group is small (severely cognitively impaired) or large (all under-18s)? The relational egalitarian framework suggests no — any exclusion creates unequal relationships — but the practical implications differ enormously.

2. **Can accommodation fully substitute for inclusion?** If assisted voting, advance directives, and civic education can preserve the franchise for cognitively impaired persons, does the inclusivity principle require these accommodations — or does it require outright inclusion regardless of capacity? The answer has significant implications for the design of electoral institutions.

3. **Does the civic education effect persist?** The evidence that lowering the voting age cultivates democratic habits is promising but largely based on first- and second-election data. Whether these effects persist across the life course — and whether they generalize across political cultures — remains an open empirical question that the UK's pending reform may help answer.

4. **How should the inclusivity principle weigh against the epistocratic concern for decision quality?** Even if exclusion imposes a dignitary harm, it does not follow that the harm always outweighs the epistemic benefits of a more competent electorate. The question is whether there is a non-arbitrary way to weigh these values — or whether the inclusivity principle must be treated as a side constraint that cannot be traded off against epistemic gains.

### Synthesis: Toward a Principled Framework for Age Limits

The four analytical tensions examined in this report — democratic principles, cognitive development and decline, intergenerational equity, and the balance between inclusivity and informed decision-making — do not resolve into a single tidy recommendation. Instead, they produce a partial convergence around lowering the minimum voting age and rejecting a hard maximum, alongside a genuine divergence created by cognitive decline that minimum-age reform alone cannot address. This synthesis maps the landscape of options, identifies the strongest convergence and divergence arguments, and specifies the unresolved questions that future research must address.

#### The Convergence Argument

The strongest case for convergence holds that the available evidence — empirical, normative, and institutional — pushes in the direction of **lowering the minimum voting age to 16 and maintaining no maximum age**. The argument proceeds along each of the four tensions:

1. **Democratic principles.** The affected-interests principle supports enfranchising citizens who are governed and affected by political decisions, including 16-year-olds. The "evolving capacities" framework in the CRC recognizes that cognitive development is gradual, not categorical, and the Committee on the Rights of the Child has explicitly praised countries that lowered the voting age ([UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)). No democratic theory supports a maximum age: Poama & Volacu (2023) demonstrate that even under the principle most sympathetic to age-based restrictions (equal opportunity of political influence), blanket disenfranchisement of older citizens fails on its own terms, and age-weighted voting is "practically unrobust across a relevant range of political contexts" ([Poama & Volacu 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14748851211062604)).

2. **Cognitive development.** The cold/hot cognition distinction shows that the cognitive demands of voting — abstract reasoning, information processing, long-term thinking — are cold-cognitive tasks that reach adult-equivalent levels by approximately age 16 ([Steinberg et al., PMC2892678](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2892678/)). Empirical studies in Austria, Brazil, and Germany consistently find that 16–17-year-olds possess political knowledge and decision-making quality comparable to 18–21-year-olds (Wagner et al. 2012; [UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)). Education requirements and political-knowledge tests are not applied to adult voters; applying them only to youth would be discriminatory ([Hamilton 2012](https://scholarship.law.wm.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2505&context=facpubs); [UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/should-children-vote)).

3. **Intergenerational equity.** The age–turnout gradient systematically disadvantages younger cohorts. Stockemer and Sundström (2025) find that young people are absent from decision-making bodies "across the national, subnational and supra-national levels," creating structural risk of policies favoring older cohorts ([Stockemer 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/government-and-opposition/article/age-inequalities-in-political-representation-a-review-article/E1FB531E2E6BD93582792E1D92AB18ED)). Lowering the minimum age directly addresses this gap; no democracy has ever raised its voting age, and the absence of upward movement signals that the intergenerational-equity axis supports expansion, not contraction. For the maximum-age question, Bengtson and Albertsen (2023) show that under the all-affected and all-subjected principles, there is reason to grant senior citizens lower voting weight — but under relational egalitarianism (their preferred framework), the question is context-dependent, and sometimes favors *greater* senior weight ([Bengtson & Albertsen 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w)).

4. **Inclusivity vs. informed decision-making.** The epistocratic challenge (Brennan 2016) holds that if democracy's legitimacy depends on competent decision-making, competence-based age restrictions may be justified. But Kogelmann and Carroll (2024) rebut that there is no right to a competent electorate — democratic legitimacy derives from consent and equal dignity, not from epistemic performance ([Kogelmann & Carroll 2024](https://www.briankogelmann.com/uploads/5/5/8/6/55864067/there_is_no_right_to_a_competent_electorate.pdf)). The López-Guerra (2014) framework for analyzing competence-based exclusions (including age, mental capacity, and imprisonment) provides the systematic treatment (see S5.1), but its application to age specifically faces the objection that no society applies competence tests to young voters while exempting adults — the asymmetry is discriminatorily selective. If a political-knowledge threshold were applied universally, Delli Carpini and Keeter's (1996) baseline evidence suggests that a substantial fraction of the adult electorate would fail, undermining the case for age-specific exclusion.

On each dimension, the evidence argues against the status quo of 18-and-up, no-maximum-age. The convergence recommends: (a) lowering the minimum to 16, where cognitive-capacity evidence and natural-experiment results align; and (b) maintaining no maximum age, where the dignitary harm of categorical exclusion, the epistemic indeterminacy of age-weighting, and the failure of equal-influence justifications converge.

#### The Divergence Argument

Despite this convergence, a genuine tension remains: **cognitive decline in older adults creates a problem that minimum-age reform alone does not resolve.** The divergence is structural, not merely empirical.

The clinical evidence reviewed in S3.1 shows that normal, non-pathological aging does not significantly impair the cold-cognitive demands of voting. However, dementia prevalence rises sharply with age, and even though many individuals with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease retain decisional capacity on the dimensions most critical for voting (understanding and appreciation), a non-trivial fraction of older voters will, at some point, lack meaningful electoral choice (see S3.1 for prevalence data, capacity findings, and the Doe standard).

This creates a tension between inclusivity and informed decision-making that has no analogue on the youth side. For 16-year-olds, the evidence overwhelmingly supports cognitive capacity, and objections are primarily principled (autonomy, paternalism) rather than empirical. For some elderly voters, the objection is genuinely empirical: dementia can impair the ability to understand candidates, appreciate consequences, or communicate a choice. A principled framework must address both ends of the age spectrum without collapsing into either blanket inclusion or blanket exclusion.

The divergence argument holds that minimum-age reform (lowering to 16) resolves the intergenerational-equity tension but does not resolve the cognitive-decline tension. A coherent framework must therefore address each tension with distinct institutional mechanisms, rather than assuming that a single age threshold can satisfy all criteria.

#### A Principled Framework: Presumptive Inclusion with Individualized Exclusion

The most coherent principle that can simultaneously justify a minimum age floor (however low) and resist a maximum age cap is what the FRfG policy paper calls **"presumptive inclusion with individualized exclusion only for demonstrated incapacity"** ([FRfG Policy Paper](https://intergenerationaljustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/PP_Voting-Rights.pdf)). This principle, which undergirds the "voting by registration" model, operates as follows:

- **Inclusion is the default.** Every citizen is presumed able to vote regardless of age. This eliminates both the minimum-age floor and the maximum-age cap as categorical exclusions. The affected-interests principle and equal political dignity support this presumption.
- **Exclusion requires individualized demonstration of incapacity.** Rather than using chronological age as a proxy for capacity, the system requires a specific, individualized finding that a person cannot understand, appreciate, reason about, or communicate a political choice. The "Doe" standard (2001 federal court) and its operationalization by Appelbaum provide the clinical criteria already developed in U.S. law (detailed in S3.1).
- **Assisted voting precedes exclusion.** Before any individualized exclusion is considered, assisted voting mechanisms — such as help understanding the ballot, physically marking it, or having a trusted person present — must be offered. The UK's position under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 that "everyone with dementia has the right to vote" regardless of mental capacity represents the strongest version of this principle ([Alzheimer's UK](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/blog/can-people-with-dementia-vote); [ABA guidance](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_aging/resources/voting_cognitive_impairments/)).

This framework avoids arbitrary line-drawing because chronological age is never the ground for exclusion: capacity is assessed individually and functionally, as it already is in medical consent and testamentary capacity law. It also avoids the asymmetry that currently characterizes suffrage: no democracy requires a competence test for young voters, so imposing one only on the elderly raises equal-protection concerns. An individualized test applied to *all* ages — young and old alike — would be symmetric, but its administrative feasibility remains the central challenge.

#### Conditions for an Administratively Feasible Competence Test

For an individualized competence test applied across all ages to be both administratively feasible and democratically legitimate, several conditions must be met:

1. **Functional, not epistemic, criteria.** The test must assess *can this person understand and communicate a political choice?* not *does this person know enough about politics?* The former is a functional capacity standard; the latter is a political-knowledge test that would disenfranchise millions of adults and would be discriminatorily administered. The "Doe" standard's functional criteria (detailed in S3.1) provide an appropriate template.

2. **Independent assessment, not partisan assessment.** The assessment must be conducted by a neutral clinical professional, not by election officials or political actors. The risk of discriminatory application — by race, class, or political affiliation — is the most serious objection. Existing guardianship proceedings in U.S. states, where courts (not officials) make capacity determinations, provide a partial model, though they are individual-case proceedings that would not scale to millions of voters.

3. **Due-process protections.** Any finding of incapacity must be appealable, time-limited (subject to periodic review), and accompanied by assisted-voting alternatives. The CRPD Article 29 framework supports supported decision-making rather than substituted decision-making or exclusion.

4. **Scale.** The hardest condition: administering individualized assessments to a national electorate of millions, at regular intervals, is unprecedented. The UK's current approach — universal inclusion with voluntary assisted voting and court-based exclusion only in guardianship proceedings — avoids this scale problem but at the cost of allowing some incapacitated persons to vote. Whether this cost is morally serious enough to justify individualized testing at scale is an open empirical and normative question.

5. **Symmetry.** If young voters are subject to the test, the political-knowledge and capacity objections currently directed at 16-year-olds must be tested too. The evidence that 16-year-olds fare as well as 18–21-year-olds on political knowledge and vote quality (Wagner et al. 2012) suggests they would pass easily — but the test must not be designed to fail only one age group, which would render it discriminatory.

Until these conditions are met, the practicable framework is **presumptive inclusion with categorical age floors, assisted voting, and court-based exclusion only for persons with documented cognitive disability** — the current global norm. The question is whether the minimum floor should be 16 or lower, and whether any additional protections for the cognitively impaired elderly are needed beyond what assisted voting and guardianship proceedings currently provide.

#### The UK's Pending Reform as a Forward-Looking Natural Experiment

The UK's pending reform — announced in July 2025, with the Representation of the People Bill 2024-26 introduced in February 2026, which would enfranchise approximately 1.7 million young people (see S1.2 for legislative status and implementation details) — offers an unprecedented opportunity to observe the effects of minimum-age lowering at national scale in a large, established democracy.

The UK case should be tracked along several dimensions as a natural experiment:

- **Turnout durability.** Will 16–17-year-old turnout match or exceed older first-time voters across multiple election cycles, or will novelty effects decay?
- **Vote quality.** Will UK 16–17-year-olds demonstrate political knowledge and decision quality comparable to older voters, replicating the Austrian and Brazilian findings?
- **Policy responsiveness.** Will parties and policymakers become more responsive to youth concerns (climate, housing, education spending)?
- **Downstream civic engagement.** Will the reform produce durable increases in political trust, volunteering, and continued voting?
- **Civic education effects.** Will the associated citizenship curriculum reforms mediate any capacity-concern effects?
- **Administrative integration.** How will automated voter registration interact with youth enfranchisement?

No implementation effects are yet observable. The UK must be treated as a forward-looking case, not an evaluated one. The principal risk in interpreting future results is selection bias — the first cohort of 16–17-year-olds to vote may be more politically engaged than subsequent cohorts, inflating apparent effects.

#### Unresolved Questions for Future Research

Several empirical and normative questions remain unresolved and should structure the next phase of inquiry:

1. **Long-run effects.** Will the turnout and democratic-trust gains observed in Austria, Brazil, and the Latin American countries persist beyond the first two election cycles? The durability of voting-as-habit has been theorized but not yet confirmed longitudinally across multiple national contexts.

2. **Assessment reliability.** Can individualized voting-capacity assessments be administered reliably, non-discriminatorily, and at scale? No jurisdiction has attempted this for a general electorate. The gap between clinical capacity assessment (small-N, high-reliability) and population-level screening (large-N, feasibility) remains a wide one.

3. **Feasibility of "voting by registration."** The FRfG model — universal suffrage without an age floor, with individuals registering when personally ready — has never been implemented. What safeguards would prevent institutional manipulation (e.g., schools pressuring students to register/not register), and how would individual assessment of "readiness" be conducted without an effectively age-based proxy?

4. **Normative stability across boundary principles.** Bengtson and Albertsen (2023) show that the democratic case for age-based restrictions varies depending on whether one adopts all-affected, all-subjected, or relational egalitarian principles — and these principles can yield opposite conclusions about senior citizens' voting weight ([Bengtson & Albertsen 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-023-10417-w)). Is there a meta-principle for selecting among boundary principles, or is the debate permanently underdetermined?

5. **Non-age-based reforms.** Do youth quotas (Bidadanure 2015), proportional representation, deliberative assemblies (UK Climate Assembly, Irish Constitutional Convention, Belgian G1000), or Demeny voting offer more tractable paths to intergenerational equity than age-based suffrage reform, and can they complement or substitute for it?

6. **Divergence resolution.** If cognitive decline creates a genuine tension that minimum-age reform does not resolve, can the presumptive-inclusion-with-individualized-exclusion framework satisfy both the inclusivity values that argue for lowering the minimum and the competence values that concern some elderly voters? Or does the framework simply relocate the contested boundary from chronological age to functional assessment — substituting one form of line-drawing for another?

The balance across these unresolved questions suggests that the strongest principled position — one that the evidence supports but does not compel — is: lower the minimum to 16 (where cognitive evidence, natural experiments, and democratic principles converge); maintain no maximum age (where the dignitary, epistemic, and institutional arguments converge); and invest in assisted voting and individualized capacity assessment as alternatives to any age-based exclusion of the elderly. Whether this position can be made compatible with the practical realities of electoral administration in aging democracies remains the central open question.
