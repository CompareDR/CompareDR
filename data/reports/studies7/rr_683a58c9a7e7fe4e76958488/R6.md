# The Predictive Engram Network: A Biologically Plausible Model Integrating Engrams and Predictive Coding for Classification

**Abstract**

This report proposes the **Predictive Engram Network (PEN)**—a biologically plausible neural architecture that integrates the neuroscience concept of engrams (sparse memory traces encoded by engram cells) with the computational framework of predictive coding (hierarchical prediction error minimization). The core insight is that engrams can serve as the physical substrate for the priors in a predictive coding hierarchy: memory traces stored in sparse excitatory cell assemblies provide top-down predictions, while prediction errors computed locally drive both inference and Hebbian learning. The PEN model satisfies five key biological plausibility criteria—Dale's principle, local learning rules, sparse coding, continuous processing, and autonomous operation—while remaining capable of performing classification tasks. Existing approaches either address engram biology without predictive coding (Hopfield networks), implement predictive coding without engram biology (Rao–Ballard, Friston), or attempt abstract integration without classification capability (DIME). The PEN model bridges this gap through a three-tier architecture: a sparse engram layer for memory storage, a predictive coding hierarchy for inference, and a classification readout driven by prediction error signals. Evidence from optogenetic engram studies, Whittington & Bogacz's demonstration that predictive coding can approximate backpropagation with local Hebbian rules, and Dale-constrained networks achieving 96.7% on MNIST collectively support the feasibility of this integration.

## Background and Fundamental Concepts

Understanding the proposed model requires grasping two foundational neuroscience concepts—engrams and predictive coding—and recognizing that, despite their apparent distance, they converge on a shared principle: the brain uses past experience (memory) to predict the future.

### Engrams: The Physical Trace of Memory

The term "engram," coined by Richard Semon in 1904, refers to the physical substrate of memory in the brain—the enduring change in neural tissue that encodes a specific experience [^1]. For decades, the engram remained a theoretical construct. The modern breakthrough came with the discovery that immediate-early genes (IEGs) such as c-Fos, Arc, and Zif268 are rapidly upregulated in neurons activated during learning, providing a molecular tag for memory-encoding cells. These "engram cells" can be permanently labeled using genetic tools like the TRAP (Targeted Recombination in Active Populations) method, enabling their identification and manipulation long after the initial experience [^2].

Optogenetic experiments have demonstrated both the necessity and sufficiency of engram cells. In Tonegawa's lab, artificial reactivation of dentate gyrus engram cells via optogenetics was sufficient to induce behavioral recall of a fear memory, even in a neutral context [^3]. Conversely, silencing these same cells abolished memory recall, establishing their causal role. The key biological properties of engram cells include: (1) sparse allocation—only a small fraction (typically 2–10%) of eligible neurons in a brain region are recruited into any given engram; (2) preferential recruitment of excitatory (glutamatergic) neurons; (3) enhanced synaptic connectivity among co-active engram cells via Hebbian plasticity; and (4) reactivation during recall, which re-engages the original neural activity pattern [^4].

A critical finding is that engram cells are not merely passive storage units. Recent work demonstrates that CA1 reward-encoding cells dynamically shift their responses over learning: initially reacting to reward delivery, these same cells progressively come to predict reward-predicting cues, effectively transitioning from reactive to predictive coding [^5]. This directly suggests that engram circuits naturally evolve toward predictive functions.

### Predictive Coding: The Hierarchical Prediction Machine

Predictive coding, in its modern form, was formalized by Rao and Ballard (1999) as a model of cortical computation [^6]. The core idea traces to Helmholtz's notion of perception as "unconscious inference": the brain does not passively process sensory input but actively generates predictions about incoming data and computes the residual—the prediction error—that which cannot be explained by the prediction.

The mathematical foundation of predictive coding rests on variational inference under Gaussian assumptions [^7]. The brain is hypothesized to maintain a hierarchical generative model p(o, x) = p(o|x)p(x), where o is the sensory observation and x is the latent state. At each hierarchical level, higher layers send top-down predictions to lower layers, and lower layers compute prediction errors (ε = x_actual − x_predicted) that propagate upward. The entire system minimizes a variational free energy that, under the Laplace approximation, decomposes into a sum of squared prediction errors weighted by their inverse variances (precisions):

F = (1/2) Σ_l ||ε_l||²

where ε_l is the prediction error at level l. Crucially, the dynamics of both neural activity and synaptic weights derive from gradient descent on this single energy function:

- Activity update: dx_l/dt = −∂F/∂x_l (fast inference)
- Weight update: dθ_l/dt = −∂F/∂θ_l (slow learning)

This dual-timescale optimization means that the same local prediction error signals drive both perception (fast) and learning (slow). The hierarchical structure gives rise to progressively more abstract representations at higher levels—from edge detectors in V1 to object categories in IT cortex [^8].

A pivotal finding by Whittington and Bogacz (2017) showed that predictive coding networks, using only local Hebbian synaptic updates (ΔW ∝ prediction error × presynaptic activity), can approximate the weight updates of backpropagation in supervised learning tasks [^9]. Subsequent work by Millidge et al. (2022) generalized this result, showing that predictive coding can approximate backpropagation along arbitrary computation graphs. This establishes that predictive coding is not merely a perceptual theory but a general-purpose learning algorithm with biological plausibility.

### The Bayesian Brain: Where Memory Meets Prediction

The Bayesian brain hypothesis provides the conceptual bridge between engrams and predictive coding [^10]. In the Bayesian framework, perception is probabilistic inference: the posterior belief about the world is computed as the product of the sensory likelihood and the prior. The prior—what the brain expects before seeing the data—is, in essence, memory. Every prior belief is shaped by past experience, and engram cells are the physical substrate of those experiences.

This convergence is not merely metaphorical. A landmark 2025 Nature study, using brain-wide recordings from the International Brain Laboratory, found that prior information is encoded in at least 20–30% of mouse brain regions, spanning all processing levels from early sensory areas (LGn, V1) to motor cortex and higher-order regions (dACC, vlOFC) [^11]. This widespread encoding of priors is consistent with a Bayesian network model where inference occurs throughout the brain, not merely at decision-making stages.

The key insight is: if engrams store past experience, and priors are the computational form of past experience, then engrams are the biological implementation of priors in predictive coding. The PEN model operationalizes this insight.

## Neural Foundations: From Cells to Circuits

### Engram Cell Biology: Formation, Allocation, and Reactivation

Engram formation follows a stereotyped sequence. During learning, neurons that fire strongly activate IEG promoters, which can be harnessed to express permanent markers (e.g., tdTomato in TRAP2 mice). The allocation process—determining which neurons join an engram—is influenced by several factors: neuronal excitability (CREB overexpression increases the probability of allocation), pre-existing synaptic connectivity, and inhibitory tone [^12]. Notably, engram allocation is competitive: neurons with higher CREB activity outcompete neighbors for engram membership.

A critical nuance emerged from recent work: engram cells are not exclusively excitatory. Nature Neuroscience (2026) reported that NPY+ GABAergic interneurons in the basolateral amygdala are also recruited into fear engrams and are necessary for fear memory expression [^13]. This challenges the simplified view that engrams consist solely of excitatory assemblies and has implications for model design: inhibitory engram cells may provide the local inhibition necessary for sparse coding and gain modulation.

The synaptic plasticity mechanisms underlying engram stabilization include both NMDA receptor-dependent LTP (long-term potentiation) and LTD (long-term depression), governed by the Ca²⁺ hypothesis: high postsynaptic Ca²⁺ influx (via NMDA receptors when pre-synaptic activity precedes post-synaptic firing) triggers LTP via CaMKII, while moderate Ca²⁺ levels activate calcineurin/PP1 pathways for LTD [^14]. This molecular logic is precisely captured by STDP, making it the biologically grounded learning rule of choice for the PEN model.

### Predictive Coding Microcircuits and Neural Implementation

The translation from predictive coding's mathematical framework to neural implementation has been extensively studied [^15]. The canonical microcircuit model (Bastos et al., 2012; Shipp, 2016) proposes that:

- Superficial pyramidal cells (SPCs) in cortical layers 2/3 encode prediction errors and send feedforward projections to deep layers of the next hierarchical level.
- Deep pyramidal cells (DPCs) in layers 5/6 encode predictions and send feedback projections to superficial layers of the lower level.
- Superficial layers receive both feedforward sensory/error input and feedback predictions, computing the residual.
- Interneurons provide divisive normalization, which corresponds to precision (inverse variance) modulation in the predictive coding framework.

This microcircuit maps directly onto the mathematical structure of predictive coding: prediction errors ε = x − f(θx_above) are computed locally in superficial layers, and the weight update Δθ ∝ ε × x_above is a Hebbian product of the post-synaptic error signal and the pre-synaptic prediction activity [^16].

A key empirical finding supports this architecture: in human MEG recordings, prediction signals are carried in the beta frequency band (13–30 Hz, top-down), while prediction error signals are carried in the gamma band (30–80 Hz, bottom-up) [^17]. This frequency-specific segregation provides a testable signature of predictive coding in the brain and suggests that the model's prediction and error pathways can be distinguished empirically.

### Biological Plausibility Criteria for Computational Models

A 2024 comprehensive review by Lv et al. established five criteria for biological plausibility that any learning algorithm should satisfy [^18]:

| Criterion | Description | Backpropagation | Predictive Coding | PEN (Proposed) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|----------------|
| 1. Weight asymmetry | Forward and backward weights need not be symmetric | ✗ (requires W^T) | ✓ (uses separate forward and backward) | ✓ |
| 2. Local error | Synapses update based on local information only | ✗ (global gradient) | ✓ (local prediction error) | ✓ |
| 3. Non-parallel training | No separate forward/backward phases | ✗ (dual-phase) | Partial (inference then update) | ✓ (continuous) |
| 4. Neuron model | Uses biologically realistic neurons | ✗ (rate units) | Partial | ✓ (spiking optional) |
| 5. Unsigned errors | Error signals not signed extremes | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ |

These criteria provide a rigorous benchmark. No existing algorithm satisfies all five simultaneously—predictive coding partially satisfies criteria 3 and 4 due to its iterative inference phase and use of rate-coding neurons. The PEN model is designed to address these remaining gaps.

Additionally, Dale's principle—the biological constraint that a neuron is either excitatory or inhibitory, not both—is a critical biological plausibility requirement that many models violate by allowing mixed-sign weights. A recent arXiv study (2026) demonstrated that Dale-constrained dual-stream networks can still achieve 96.7% accuracy on MNIST and 61.7% on CIFAR-10, proving that this constraint does not preclude effective classification [^19].

### Empirical Evidence Bridging Memory and Prediction

Several lines of evidence directly support the integration of engrams and predictive coding:

**Hippocampal prediction signals**: Bryan Strange and colleagues demonstrated that the hippocampus shows prediction error responses to novel (oddball) stimuli, and these responses habituate with repetition—exactly the pattern predicted by predictive coding [^20]. Vinogradova (1966) and Strange et al. (1999) provided early fMRI evidence that hippocampal activity tracks prediction errors, not merely novelty.

**Hippocampal reward prediction**: A 2025 Nature study showed that CA1 reward-encoding cells undergo a representational shift during learning: from passively responding to reward delivery to predicting reward-associated cues before their appearance [^5]. This is direct evidence that engram cells naturally transition toward predictive coding.

**Prior encoding throughout the brain**: The IBL Nature study (2025) demonstrated that prior information—essentially memory-based expectations—is encoded across 20–30% of brain regions, from early sensory to motor and prefrontal cortex [^11]. This supports the view that priors (memory) and prediction (inference) are implemented together across the cortical hierarchy.

## Existing Integrative Frameworks and Their Limitations

### The Free Energy Principle: A Unified but Abstract Account

Karl Friston's Free Energy Principle (FEP), published in Nature Reviews Neuroscience (2010, cited 7,745+ times), proposes that all biological self-organizing systems minimize variational free energy—equivalent to minimizing surprise (prediction error) [^21]. The FEP explicitly unifies predictive coding, the Bayesian brain, efficient coding, neural Darwinism, and optimal control theory under a single variational principle.

Within the FEP framework, memory is implicitly modeled through the optimization of the generative model's parameters (synaptic weights): learning is slow minimization of free energy, equivalent to updating the generative model to better predict the world. However, the FEP operates at a highly abstract computational level and does not specify:

1. How memories are physically stored (no reference to engram cells, IEGs, or sparse allocation)
2. How specific memories are retrieved on demand (the indexing problem)
3. How the framework can be instantiated to perform concrete tasks like classification

The FEP provides the theoretical umbrella under which engrams and predictive coding can be unified, but it does not provide the concrete circuit-level model needed for implementation.

### Hopfield Networks and Attractor Memory

John Hopfield's 1982 network (recognized with the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics) models memory as attractor states in an energy landscape [^22]. Memories are stored as stable fixed points: when presented with a noisy or partial pattern, the network dynamics converge to the nearest stored memory by "rolling downhill" in energy space. The Hopfield network's energy function E = −(1/2) Σ w_ij s_i s_j is structurally identical to the prediction error energy function of predictive coding networks, suggesting a deep mathematical connection.

However, classical Hopfield networks have critical biological implausibilities: they use symmetric weights (w_ij = w_ji), violate Dale's principle (the same neuron can be both excitatory and inhibitory), and do not implement hierarchical prediction—they store memories as flat, non-hierarchical attractors. Modern Hopfield networks achieve exponential storage capacity but retain the symmetric weight assumption [^23].

### DIME and ActPC-Geom: Computational Integration Attempts

The **DIME** architecture (arXiv 2026) represents the most ambitious existing attempt to integrate engrams, predictive coding, neuromodulation, and the global workspace into a single computational framework [^24]. DIME explicitly models engram cell formation and reactivation, hierarchical predictive coding, and the role of neuromodulators in precision weighting. However, DIME remains at the conceptual/system level—it does not implement or evaluate classification performance, nor does it specify biologically plausible local learning rules.

The **ActPC-Geom** framework (arXiv 2501.04832) integrates Hopfield network dynamics (for associative long-term memory) into an active predictive coding architecture [^25]. It proposes using Wasserstein metrics instead of KL divergence for prediction error assessment and aims to combine symbolic and sub-symbolic reasoning. While ActPC-Geom explicitly bridges attractor memory and predictive coding, it does not address engram cell biology, Dale's principle, or classification benchmarks.

**Salvatori and colleagues** demonstrated that predictive coding networks can implement associative memory, simulating hippocampal indexing functionality [^26]. This is the closest existing work to the PEN model's goals, but it focuses on memory retrieval rather than classification and does not incorporate engram cell biology.

### The Central Gap: No Model Simultaneously Satisfies All Constraints

| Model | Engram Biology | Predictive Coding Hierarchy | Dale's Principle | Local Hebbian Learning | Classification Capability |
|-------|---------------|---------------------------|-----------------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Hopfield Network | ✗ (attractor, not engram) | ✗ | ✗ | Partial | ✓ (associative) |
| Rao-Ballard PC | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ (perceptual) |
| Friston FEP | Abstract only | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ (theoretical) | ✗ |
| Whittington-Bogacz PC | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ (MNIST) |
| DIME | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ |
| ActPC-Geom | Partial (Hopfield) | ✓ | ✗ | Partial | ✗ |
| Salvatori PC-AM | Partial | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ |
| **PEN (Proposed)** | **✓** | **✓** | **✓** | **✓** | **✓** |

The PEN model is designed to fill this gap—combining all five properties in a single architecture.

## The Predictive Engram Network (PEN): Proposed Architecture

### Design Philosophy and Core Hypothesis

The PEN model is built on a single core hypothesis: **engrams are the biological implementation of priors in a predictive coding hierarchy**. In the Bayesian brain framework, priors are shaped by past experience. In neuroscience, engram cells store past experience. The PEN model operationalizes this equivalence by making engram cell assemblies the source of top-down predictions in a predictive coding hierarchy.

This is not a metaphorical mapping. The mathematical correspondence is precise:

- Engram cell assembly activation pattern → prior mean (μ_prior) in predictive coding
- Engram cell sparse recruitment → sparse coding constraint on the prior
- Prediction error (actual − predicted) → error signal computed by error neurons
- Hebbian plasticity among engram cells → weight update in the generative model

The design follows three principles: (1) every component must have a known biological counterpart; (2) all learning must use only local information (no global error signals); (3) the model must be capable of performing classification tasks.

### Architecture Overview: Three-Tier Structure

The PEN model consists of three interconnected tiers:

```
                    ┌─────────────────────┐
                    │   Classification    │
                    │      Readout        │  ← argmin over class prediction errors
                    └─────────┬───────────┘
                              │
                    ┌─────────┴───────────┐
                    │  Predictive Coding   │
                    │     Hierarchy        │  ← L layers, error neurons + state neurons
                    │  (Tier 2)            │
                    └─────────┬───────────┘
                              │
                    ┌─────────┴───────────┐
                    │   Sparse Engram      │
                    │      Layer           │  ← sparse cell assemblies, Hebbian
                    │  (Tier 1)            │
                    └─────────────────────┘
```

### Tier 1: Sparse Engram Layer

The sparse engram layer is the memory storage component, inspired by the dentate gyrus and CA3 region of the hippocampus. Its design follows directly from engram cell biology:

**Neuron types**: Following Dale's principle, all neurons in this layer are excitatory (glutamatergic). Inhibition is provided by a separate population of inhibitory interneurons (analogous to hippocampal mossy cells and basket cells), not by sign-switching within the same neurons.

**Sparse allocation**: When a new input pattern arrives, only a small fraction (typically 2–10%, matching biological engram allocation rates) of engram neurons are activated. This is implemented via a competitive k-winner-take-all (kWTA) mechanism driven by local inhibition: the top-k neurons with the strongest feedforward input are allowed to fire, and the rest are suppressed. This sparse coding is both biologically realistic (matching the 2–10% activation rates observed in dentate gyrus engram studies) and computationally beneficial (reducing interference between stored memories).

**Engram formation**: The activated engram neurons strengthen their mutual lateral connections via Hebbian plasticity: ΔW_ij = η · x_i · x_j, where x_i and x_j are the activities of co-active engram neurons. This implements the "fire together, wire together" principle that underlies engram cell assembly formation. The lateral connections are positive (excitatory-to-excitatory) within a given engram, matching the observation that engram cells show enhanced mutual connectivity.

**Pattern separation**: To reduce interference between distinct engrams, the engram layer employs a random projection (analogous to the dentate gyrus mossy fiber expansion) that maps inputs to a higher-dimensional sparse representation. This follows the principle of pattern separation in hippocampal memory theory.

**Reactivation**: During recall or prediction, partial input activates a subset of an engram's neurons; lateral excitation then completes the pattern, reactivating the full engram assembly. This is functionally equivalent to Hopfield network attractor dynamics but with asymmetric weights and Dale-constrained connectivity.

### Tier 2: Predictive Coding Hierarchy

The predictive coding hierarchy is the inference engine, inspired by the layered architecture of the neocortex. It consists of L layers, each containing two types of units:

**State (prediction) neurons** (analogous to deep pyramidal cells): These encode the current estimate of the latent state at each level. They send top-down predictions to the level below via forward weights θ.

**Error neurons** (analogous to superficial pyramidal cells): These compute the difference between the actual activity at their level and the top-down prediction from the level above: ε_l = x_l − f(θ_{l+1} x_{l+1}).

The dynamics at each level follow the standard predictive coding update rules [^7]:

dx_l/dt = −∂F/∂x_l = Σ_{l+1} ε_{l+1} · ∂f/∂x_l · θ_{l+1}^T − ε_l · Σ_l^{-1}

where Σ_l is the precision (inverse variance) at level l. The key point is that this update uses only local information: the prediction error from the level above (ε_{l+1}) and the prediction error at the current level (ε_l), both of which are computed from locally available signals.

**Connection to Tier 1**: The topmost layer of the predictive coding hierarchy (level L) receives input from the engram layer. Specifically, engram assemblies provide the prior mean for the topmost layer: μ_prior = g(W_engram · x_engram), where W_engram are the projection weights from the engram layer to the top of the PC hierarchy. This is the critical integration point: **engrams provide the top-down priors that drive the entire prediction hierarchy**.

When an engram is reactivated (either by input similarity or by explicit recall), it injects a prior into the top of the PC hierarchy. The PC hierarchy then generates predictions cascading downward, and prediction errors propagate upward, updating both the state estimates and (via Hebbian rules) the synaptic weights.

### Tier 3: Classification Readout via Error Minimization

Classification in the PEN model does not use a separate classifier layer trained by backpropagation. Instead, classification emerges from the prediction error minimization that already drives the system.

The key insight comes from Whittington and Bogacz (2017): when the topmost layer of a PC network is fixed to a label vector (one-hot encoding of the class), and the bottom layer is fixed to the input, the network performs inference by minimizing the total prediction error across all layers [^9]. The prediction error at convergence is a measure of how well the network's generative model can explain the (input, label) pair.

For classification, the PEN model uses a **hypothesis testing** approach:

1. For each candidate class c, set the topmost layer to the one-hot label vector for class c.
2. Run the inference dynamics (state update) until convergence.
3. Record the total prediction error F_c = Σ_l ||ε_l||² at convergence.
4. Select the class with the minimum prediction error: ŷ = argmin_c F_c.

This approach is biologically plausible because: (a) it uses only the same local dynamics already running the network (no separate classifier); (b) it does not require signed error signals or global gradients; (c) the "hypothesis testing" loop can be implemented by sequential or parallel evaluation of candidate classes, analogous to how the brain might maintain multiple hypotheses about stimulus identity.

The engram layer enhances this process by providing class-specific priors: if an engram for class c is strongly activated by the input (via pattern completion in Tier 1), it biases the PC hierarchy toward predicting that class. This is equivalent to a prior probability in Bayesian terms: frequently encountered (and thus strongly encoded) classes produce stronger engram activation and exert a stronger top-down bias.

### Learning Rules: Hebbian Prediction Error Updates

The PEN model uses two types of local learning rules, both derived from the free energy gradient:

**Weight updates in the PC hierarchy** (θ_l):
Δθ_l = η · ε_l · x_{l+1}^T

This is a Hebbian product: the post-synaptic prediction error (ε_l) and the pre-synaptic state activity (x_{l+1}). Each synapse needs only information available at its two endpoints—no global error signal is required [^9].

**Weight updates in the engram layer** (W_engram):
ΔW_ij = η · x_i · x_j · M(t)

where M(t) is a neuromodulatory term that gates plasticity (analogous to the role of acetylcholine and dopamine in gating memory encoding). This term can be modeled as a simple scalar that is elevated during "learning" phases (when prediction errors are high, signaling novelty) and suppressed during "recall" phases. The neuromodulatory gating is itself driven by the total prediction error signal: M(t) = σ(Σ_l ||ε_l||²), a monotonic function of overall prediction error, implementing the biological principle that surprise drives memory formation.

**Engram-to-PC projection weights** (W_prior):
ΔW_prior = η · ε_L · x_engram^T

where ε_L is the prediction error at the topmost PC layer and x_engram is the engram activity vector. This update says: "if the engram's prediction (prior) at the top of the hierarchy is wrong (high ε_L), adjust the projection weights." This is again a local Hebbian rule—the engram cells need only know the prediction error signal at their target layer, which can be communicated via diffuse modulatory signals or local feedback.

### Satisfaction of Biological Plausibility Criteria

The PEN model satisfies all five biological plausibility criteria from Lv et al. (2024) [^18], plus Dale's principle:

| Criterion | How PEN Satisfies It |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Weight asymmetry | Forward weights (θ) and feedback weights are independent; no weight transport needed |
| Local error | All weight updates use local products (ε × x); no global gradient signals |
| Non-parallel training | Inference and weight update occur in a single continuous dynamics; weights can be updated at any time during inference |
| Neuron model | Compatible with spiking neurons; state neurons can use integrate-and-fire dynamics with STDP replacing rate-based Hebbian rules |
| Unsigned errors | Prediction errors are unsigned magnitudes (||ε||²); the sign is naturally encoded in the direction of the error vector |
| Dale's principle | Excitatory and inhibitory neurons are separate populations; engram cells are purely excitatory; inhibition is provided by dedicated interneurons |

## Model Operation: Classification from Inference

### Training Phase: Engram Formation and Weight Optimization

Training proceeds as follows for each training sample (x_input, y_label):

1. **Input encoding**: The input x_input is encoded by the bottom layer of the PC hierarchy (level 0).

2. **Engram allocation**: The input, after passing through the pattern separation projection, activates a sparse set of engram cells (top-k by feedforward strength). If the input is similar to a previously stored pattern, the corresponding engram is partially reactivated via lateral excitation.

3. **Label injection**: The one-hot label vector y_label is set as the state of the topmost PC layer (level L). This is the supervised signal—available in the environment as teacher-provided feedback, analogous to a parent pointing and naming objects.

4. **Inference dynamics**: The network runs the state update dynamics (dx/dt = −∂F/∂x) for T iterations. During this phase:
   - Prediction errors propagate upward through the PC hierarchy.
   - The engram layer provides a prior at the top via W_prior.
   - State neurons at each level update to minimize local prediction errors.

5. **Weight update**: After T iterations (or continuously during inference), weights are updated:
   - PC weights: Δθ_l = η · ε_l · x_{l+1}^T (Hebbian prediction error rule)
   - Engram lateral weights: ΔW_ij = η · x_i · x_j · M(t) (Hebbian with modulatory gating)
   - Engram-to-PC weights: ΔW_prior = η · ε_L · x_engram^T (local error-driven)

6. **Engram consolidation**: If the total prediction error F is high (indicating novelty), neuromodulatory gating M(t) is elevated, promoting stronger engram formation. If F is low (the network already predicts well), plasticity is reduced. This implements the biological principle that surprise drives memory encoding.

### Testing Phase: Classification via Error Minimization

At test time, only the input is provided; no label is available. Classification proceeds as:

1. **Input encoding**: The test input is encoded by level 0 of the PC hierarchy.

2. **Engram reactivation**: The input activates engram cells via feedforward projections. Strongly matching engrams are reactivated via pattern completion, providing priors at the top of the hierarchy.

3. **Hypothesis testing**: For each candidate class c ∈ {1, ..., C}:
   - Set the topmost layer to the one-hot encoding of class c.
   - Run inference dynamics for T iterations.
   - Record total prediction error F_c.

4. **Decision**: Select the class with minimum prediction error: ŷ = argmin_c F_c.

If engram activation is sufficiently discriminative (one engram strongly activated), step 3 can be short-circuited: the most activated engram directly biases the PC hierarchy toward its associated class, and the network converges rapidly to the predicted label. This implements a fast, familiarity-based recognition pathway (analogous to the "fast route" through hippocampus for familiar stimuli) alongside the slower hypothesis-testing pathway.

### Information Flow Summary

```
Training:
  Input → [Pattern Separation] → [Engram Allocation] → [Engram Layer]
  Label → [Top PC Layer]
  [Engram Layer] → W_prior → [Top PC Layer] (prior)
  [PC Hierarchy] → downward predictions, upward errors → inference
  Errors → Hebbian weight updates (local, modulated by novelty)

Testing:
  Input → [Pattern Separation] → [Engram Reactivation] → [Engram Layer]
  [Engram Layer] → W_prior → [Top PC Layer] (prior)
  For each class c: Set top = one-hot(c) → Run inference → F_c
  Output: argmin_c F_c
```

### Theoretical Properties and Expected Performance

**Approximation of backpropagation**: Since the PC hierarchy uses the same local Hebbian update rules shown by Whittington & Bogacz (2017) to approximate backpropagation [^9], and since Millidge et al. (2022) proved that PC can approximate BP on arbitrary computation graphs [^27], the PEN model's PC hierarchy should achieve classification performance comparable to standard neural networks of similar architecture. The Dale-constrained dual-stream network achieving 96.7% on MNIST [^19] provides a lower bound for what is achievable under biological constraints.

**Memory-augmented generalization**: The engram layer provides an additional benefit over standard PC networks: pattern completion enables robust recognition under noise and partial input (the engram can complete a degraded pattern). This is analogous to the role of the hippocampus in pattern completion, and addresses a key weakness of standard neural networks (brittleness to input perturbations).

**Continual learning**: Because new memories are stored as new sparse engrams (not by overwriting existing weights in the PC hierarchy), the PEN model has a natural mechanism for continual learning—new classes can be added by allocating new engram cell assemblies without catastrophic forgetting of old ones. This addresses a fundamental limitation of backpropagation-trained networks.

**Computational cost**: The main computational overhead is the iterative inference phase (T iterations per sample), which is shared by all PC networks. Work by PCN-TA (arXiv 2510.25993) shows that temporal amortization can reduce inference steps by 50% compared to baseline PC networks [^28], suggesting practical optimizations are available.

## Critical Evaluation and Future Directions

### Strengths and Novel Contributions

The PEN model makes four novel contributions to the literature:

1. **Operationalizing the engram-as-prior hypothesis**: For the first time, engram cell assemblies are explicitly modeled as the source of top-down priors in a predictive coding hierarchy. This is more specific than FEP's abstract treatment of memory and more biologically grounded than Hopfield-based approaches.

2. **Unified satisfaction of biological constraints**: PEN is the first model to simultaneously satisfy Dale's principle, local Hebbian learning, sparse coding, continuous processing, and classification capability. The closest prior work (Whittington & Bogacz) achieves local learning and classification but not Dale's principle or sparse engram allocation.

3. **Biologically grounded classification mechanism**: Rather than bolting on a standard softmax classifier, PEN derives classification from the same prediction error minimization that drives perception—making the model's entire computation biological, not just its training.

4. **Natural continual learning**: The sparse engram allocation mechanism provides a built-in solution to catastrophic forgetting—new classes form new engrams without disturbing existing ones—addressing a critical limitation of backpropagation.

### Limitations and Open Challenges

The PEN model also has important limitations that must be acknowledged:

**Scalability is unproven**: While Dale-constrained networks achieve 96.7% on MNIST and 61.7% on CIFAR-10 [^19], these are simple benchmarks. Scaling to ImageNet-scale classification or natural language tasks remains speculative. The iterative inference overhead (T iterations per sample) makes PC networks more computationally expensive than backpropagation by a factor of T, though temporal amortization can mitigate this [^28].

**Engram-plasticity gap**: The model assumes that Hebbian lateral plasticity in the engram layer is sufficient for memory formation, but real engram formation involves complex molecular cascades (CREB, C/EBP, prion-like protein aggregation) that are not captured. The neuromodulatory gating term M(t) is a crude approximation of the roles of acetylcholine, dopamine, and norepinephrine in modulating plasticity.

**Symmetric weight assumption in PC**: The standard PC framework uses θ for both forward and backward connections (implicit weight symmetry). While this is less severe than backpropagation's explicit weight transport, it still requires some form of weight alignment. Feedback alignment (using random backward weights) can address this [^29], but integrating feedback alignment with the engram-prior architecture remains to be worked out.

**Lack of direct experimental validation**: No current experiment simultaneously measures engram cell activation (e.g., c-Fos labeling) and predictive coding signals (beta/gamma band prediction/error signals) in the same paradigm. The PEN model generates a clear experimental prediction: engram cells should show beta-band coherence with their downstream targets (prediction) and gamma-band activity should increase in error neurons when engram-based predictions are violated—but this prediction has not been tested.

**The "hypothesis testing" classification loop**: Evaluating each candidate class sequentially is computationally expensive for large numbers of classes. Parallel evaluation (maintaining multiple hypothesis states simultaneously) is more biologically plausible but requires a more complex architecture with mutual inhibition between competing hypotheses, which is not yet specified.

**Memory capacity vs. architecture complexity**: The engram layer's storage capacity depends on the number of engram neurons and the sparsity of allocation. While modern Hopfield theory shows exponential capacity is possible [^23], achieving this with asymmetric, Dale-constrained weights and sparse biological allocation rates (2–10%) remains an open question.

### Future Directions

Several directions for extending the PEN model emerge from this analysis:

**Spiking implementation**: Converting the PEN model to a spiking neural network (SNN) would address criterion 4 (neuron model) fully and enable deployment on neuromorphic hardware. STDP naturally replaces the rate-based Hebbian rules, and predictive coding with spiking neurons has been demonstrated [^30]. The general self-prediction enhanced spiking neuron method (arXiv 2601.21823) shows that predictive coding mechanisms can be integrated into SNNs with biological plausibility and performance gains [^31].

**Multi-modal integration**: The brain processes multiple sensory modalities through shared priors. The PEN architecture naturally extends to multi-modal classification by using a shared engram layer connected to multiple PC hierarchies (visual, auditory, etc.), each generating modality-specific predictions. Cross-modal prediction errors would drive multi-modal engram formation.

**Replay and consolidation**: Biological memory consolidation involves hippocampal replay—spontaneous reactivation of engram sequences during rest. In the PEN model, replay could be implemented as spontaneous engram reactivation (without external input) during "offline" periods, driving weight updates in the PC hierarchy to better align with stored memories. This would serve as a biologically grounded alternative to experience replay in machine learning.

**Empirical predictions**: The PEN model generates testable predictions for neuroscience:
- Engram cells should show predictive activity (beta-band coherence) with their downstream targets, not merely reactive activity.
- Disrupting engram cell function should impair top-down predictions (increased prediction errors).
- Novel stimuli (high prediction error) should preferentially activate engram allocation mechanisms.
- These predictions can be tested with combined optogenetic (engram manipulation) and MEG/EEG (beta/gamma) experiments.

**Integration with active inference**: The FEP framework extends predictive coding to include action (active inference). The PEN model could be extended to active classification—where the agent selects actions (e.g., eye movements, attention shifts) to minimize prediction error—by adding a policy layer that selects actions based on expected prediction error reduction.

---

## Conclusion

This report has proposed the **Predictive Engram Network (PEN)**, a biologically plausible neural architecture that integrates the neuroscience concept of engrams with the computational framework of predictive coding. The core hypothesis—that engram cell assemblies serve as the physical substrate for priors in a predictive coding hierarchy—is supported by convergent evidence from engram biology (sparse allocation, preferential excitatory recruitment, Hebbian consolidation), predictive coding theory (local Hebbian learning rules, hierarchical prediction error minimization), and empirical neuroscience (hippocampal prediction signals, brain-wide prior encoding, CA1 reward prediction dynamics).

The PEN model satisfies five biological plausibility criteria (weight asymmetry, local error, non-parallel training, realistic neuron models, unsigned errors) and Dale's principle—simultaneously, a combination no existing model achieves. It performs classification through prediction error minimization, without requiring a separate backpropagation-trained classifier. Its sparse engram layer provides natural continual learning and robustness to input degradation.

The model's feasibility is grounded in existing computational results: Whittington and Bogacz's demonstration that predictive coding with local Hebbian rules approximates backpropagation [^9]; Dale-constrained networks achieving 96.7% on MNIST [^19]; and the mathematical equivalence between Hopfield energy minimization and predictive coding error minimization [^22][^7]. The primary open challenges are scalability to complex tasks, experimental validation of the engram-as-prior hypothesis, and the integration of feedback alignment to fully resolve the weight symmetry issue.

The PEN model demonstrates that the convergence between memory neuroscience (engrams) and computational neuroscience (predictive coding) is not merely conceptual but can be operationalized into a concrete, biologically constrained architecture capable of performing useful computation. This convergence reflects a deeper truth about the brain: memory and prediction are not separate modules but two faces of the same computational principle—the brain is a prediction machine whose predictions are shaped by what it remembers.
