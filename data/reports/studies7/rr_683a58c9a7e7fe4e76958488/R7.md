# An Integrated Model of Memory and Prediction: A Hierarchical Spiking Network for Classification

## Foundational Frameworks: The Neurobiology of Engrams and Predictive Coding

To construct a unified model, it is essential first to deconstruct the fundamental principles of engrams and predictive coding as described in contemporary neuroscience. These two concepts, while originating from different lines of inquiry, address complementary aspects of brain function: engrams provide a substrate for memory storage, while predictive coding offers a framework for perception and inference. Understanding their individual properties is the necessary precursor to identifying their points of convergence.

Engrams are defined as physical ensembles of neurons that store a memory trace [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf)]. They represent the tangible biological basis of our experiences and knowledge. The functional integrity of an engram is not merely its existence but its capacity for reactivation. Experimental evidence strongly supports the hypothesis that memory retrieval relies on the reactivation of the specific prior perceptual representations that were active during the initial encoding of the experience [[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12458145/), [10](https://eeb.embo.org/p/Memory-responses-in-visual-cortex-track-recall-success-after-single-trial-encoding)]. This phenomenon, often termed cortical reinstatement, has been observed in early visual cortex, suggesting that even high-level memories involve the temporary return of sensory-specific activity patterns [[9](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/111250)]. The fidelity of this process is critical; the strength of a memory recall is correlated with the degree of overlap between the cells active during encoding and those active during retrieval, rather than simply the total number of cells within the engram ensemble [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf)]. This implies that the *functional state* of the engram, specifically its accessibility for reactivation, is a primary determinant of memory performance. Forgetting, therefore, is not necessarily the permanent loss of the engram itself but a reduction in its reactivation potential [[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. In a mouse object recognition task, animals tested after two weeks showed no behavioral preference for a novel object, indicating memory loss. However, the total number of labeled engram cells remained unchanged compared to animals tested after 24 hours. The crucial difference was a significant decrease in the number of cells that were co-active during both encoding and retrieval, directly linking poor recall to reduced engram reactivation [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf)].

This dynamic nature of engrams is actively regulated by molecular mechanisms. The signaling protein Rac1, for instance, has been identified as a key mediator of engram plasticity and forgetting [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. Pharmacological inhibition of Rac1 following memory encoding prevented the natural decay of memory over time, while its activation accelerated forgetting [[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. This effect was linked to a reduction in engram reactivation, suggesting Rac1 acts as a plasticity-enabling molecule that modulates the accessibility of stored traces [[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. Furthermore, structural changes accompany this functional modulation. Morphological analysis of engram cells in the dentate gyrus revealed a significant reduction in dendritic spine density and spine volume following natural forgetting, indicating that forgetting may involve a physical remodeling of the engram's synaptic connections [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf)]. The accessibility of an engram can also be experimentally manipulated. Optogenetic activation of an engram that had become behaviorally inaccessible ("forgotten") was sufficient to restore memory recall, demonstrating that the original cellular ensemble remains intact but is simply less excitable or accessible under normal conditions [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. Similarly, reminder cues that induce positive prediction errors can facilitate engram reactivation and subsequent memory recovery [[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)].

In stark contrast to the localized and experience-dependent nature of engrams, predictive coding presents a global, hierarchical theory of brain function. It posits that the brain operates as a sophisticated Bayesian inference engine, constantly generating top-down predictions about the causes of its sensory inputs and minimizing the discrepancy between these predictions and the actual bottom-up sensory data [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5371651/)]. This discrepancy is termed the prediction error. The core computational principle is the iterative minimization of this prediction error across multiple levels of a processing hierarchy, ultimately leading to a stable internal model that best explains the world [[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40651-w)]. This architecture is characterized by a canonical microcircuit involving excitatory pyramidal neurons and various classes of inhibitory interneurons [[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3777738/)]. Within this framework, feedforward connections primarily transmit bottom-up prediction errors, while feedback connections carry top-down predictions [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5371651/)]. The theory suggests a specific anatomical locus for prediction error computation, proposing that it occurs within a canonical interneuron circuit that receives input from lower layers and provides inhibitory control to pyramidal cells receiving top-down predictions [[7](https://www.bu.edu/hasselmo/ChapmanHasselmo2023.pdf)].

This hierarchical organization allows for increasingly abstract representations at higher levels. Lower levels might predict simple features like edges and orientations, while higher levels predict more complex objects or contextual scenarios. The process of "explaining away" is a key feature of this architecture, where a strong top-down prediction at a higher level can suppress or eliminate the need for a corresponding prediction at a lower level if the higher-level prediction is sufficient to account for the sensory data [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5371651/)]. This mechanism is central to perceptual inference and classification. Computational models based on predictive coding have demonstrated practical utility in tasks such as visual object recognition, achieving competitive accuracy on benchmarks like handwritten digit recognition (MNIST dataset) and face identification [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5371651/)]. These models formalize the core dynamics using iterative equations that update predictions and compute errors through matrix operations representing synaptic weights [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5371651/)]. While powerful, these models often rely on rate-coded neurons and matrix algebra, which, while computationally effective, sacrifices some of the biological detail present in the underlying neurophysiology. The synthesis of engrams and predictive coding aims to create a model that retains the computational power of the latter while embedding the rich, experience-dependent memory mechanisms of the former.

## Unifying Principles: Prediction Error as the Common Currency of Memory and Inference

The most profound insight derived from synthesizing the literature on engrams and predictive coding is the identification of prediction error as a unifying principle that governs both memory processes and perceptual inference. While engrams provide the content of memory—the specific cellular ensembles—and predictive coding provides the machinery of inference—the mechanism driving both appears to be the same: the brain's response to unexpected events. This convergence transforms the relationship between the two theories from one of parallelism to one of deep mechanistic integration.

In the context of engram dynamics, prediction errors serve as a direct instructional signal for updating the relevance and strength of memory traces. A computational model based on the Rescorla-Wagner rule formalizes this process, explaining forgetting as an adaptive learning phenomenon driven by prediction errors [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. According to this model, an engram's strength, or "engram relevancy," is dynamically updated in response to the discrepancy between expected and experienced outcomes. A positive prediction error—an event that is not predicted—strengthens the engram, reinforcing the association between the cue and the outcome [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf)]. Conversely, a negative prediction error—an expected event that fails to occur—weakens the engram, leading to forgetting [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. This mechanism allows an organism to prioritize relevant information by continuously updating the salience of stored memories based on new experiences. For example, repeated exposure to a training context without the associated objects leads to accelerated forgetting of the object-context memory, a process that requires the activity of the original engram ensemble itself [[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. This suggests that the brain uses the absence of an expected outcome (a sustained negative prediction error) to down-weight the importance of the stored memory trace.

This concept of prediction error-driven plasticity finds a direct parallel in the computational implementations of predictive coding, where the entire inferential process is predicated on minimizing prediction error [[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40651-w)]. In the standard predictive coding loop, top-down predictions are generated by the network, and these are compared against bottom-up sensory evidence. The resulting mismatch is propagated upwards as a prediction error signal, which is then used to update the predictions at all levels of the hierarchy until a stable, self-consistent state is reached [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5371651/)]. The act of successfully retrieving a memory can thus be reframed as a process of prediction. When a person encounters a familiar stimulus, the brain generates a top-down prediction of its features based on a stored categorical representation. If this prediction perfectly matches the incoming sensory data, the prediction error signal will be zero or minimal. This state of zero prediction error signifies successful inference and constitutes the feeling of clear, confident recall. In this view, memory recall is not just the passive replay of a stored pattern but an active inferential process where the brain's generative model produces a perfect match to the sensory input.

Building on this foundation, researchers have developed biologically plausible plasticity rules that explicitly link synaptic change to prediction error or its proxies, such as temporal contiguity. One such rule, "Latent Predictive Learning" (LPL), is a local, layer-wise plasticity rule derived from an objective function that combines predictive and Hebbian components [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. The predictive term in the LPL rule pulls together temporally consecutive inputs, driving neurons to learn slow, predictable features in their environment. This component is crucial for forming stable representations from variable sensory data. Simultaneously, a variance-modulated Hebbian term prevents representational collapse by promoting neuronal diversity, ensuring that the network does not degenerate into a single, overly strong representation [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. The LPL framework naturally reproduces experimentally observed properties of synaptic plasticity, including Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP) and metaplasticity, without being explicitly designed for them [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. It provides a normative explanation for how the brain might learn invariant features independently of data co-variance, a feat difficult to achieve with purely Hebbian rules [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. Another model proposes a single-neuron predictive plasticity rule where a neuron learns a low-rank model of its own synaptic input dynamics to minimize the mismatch between actual and predicted inputs [[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40651-w)]. This rule enables neurons to anticipate and recall sequences of inputs, leading to phenomena like stimulus-evoked sequence replay and spontaneous recall, which mimic neural replay observed in vivo [[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40651-w)]. These models demonstrate that prediction error or predictive mismatches can be the driving force behind synaptic plasticity at the single-neuron level, bridging the gap between macroscopic cognitive phenomena like memory and microscopic cellular processes like long-term potentiation and depression.

This synthesis reveals that engrams and predictive coding are not opposing or independent systems but are deeply intertwined. The stored memory trace (engram) serves as the substrate for a top-down prediction in the predictive coding hierarchy. The sensory evidence serves as the bottom-up prediction error signal. The process of learning a new category or consolidating a memory trace is fundamentally an error-minimization process: the system adjusts its synaptic weights (i.e., strengthens the engram) until the top-down prediction generated from the engram matches the bottom-up sensory evidence, thereby eliminating the prediction error. Forgetting, in turn, can be seen as a failure of this matching process. If a memory is rarely accessed, the predictive pathway may weaken, leading to a large prediction error upon attempted retrieval. This persistent error could either trigger a relearning process or, if sustained, lead to the down-weighting and eventual functional silencing of the engram, consistent with experimental observations [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. Therefore, prediction error becomes the common currency that mediates learning, recall, and forgetting across both frameworks.

## Proposed Model Architecture: A Hierarchical Spiking Network

Based on the unifying principles of prediction error-driven plasticity, a biologically plausible architecture emerges: a multi-layered, recurrent spiking neuronal network that mirrors the hierarchical organization of the cerebral cortex. This model, tentatively named the Hierarchical Predictive Engram Network (HPEN), assigns distinct computational roles to different cortical layers, integrating episodic memory storage, feature abstraction, and high-level generative modeling into a single, cohesive system capable of performing classification tasks. The architecture is designed to be consistent with known neuroanatomy and neurophysiology, incorporating elements like spiking neurons, diverse synaptic conductances, and specific microcircuit motifs.

The HPEN consists of three primary layers, each modeled after a specific cortical region and serving a unique function in the memory and classification pipeline.

**Layer 1: Sensory Episodic Buffer (Modeled on Primary Visual Cortex - V1)**
The lowest layer of the network is responsible for processing raw sensory input and forming transient, high-fidelity representations of individual episodes. This corresponds to the function attributed to early sensory cortices, which are thought to support one-shot episodic memory by reinstating perceptual representations [[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12458145/), [9](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/111250)]. Analogous to V1, this layer receives direct input from a sensory source (e.g., an image or sound waveform). Its neurons translate this analog input into a sparse code of action potentials. Upon the presentation of a novel stimulus, a specific, sparse ensemble of neurons in this layer becomes active, forming a unique pattern of activity that constitutes a nascent engram [[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12458145/)]. The formation of this initial trace relies on local, Hebbian-like plasticity mechanisms, such as spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), which strengthen the connections within the co-active neuronal group [[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40651-w), [2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. This layer acts as a short-term buffer where memories are initially encoded before being consolidated into more abstract, categorical representations in higher layers.

**Layer 2: Intermediate Feature/Category Representation (Modeled on Inferior Temporal Cortex - IT)**
The middle layer is tasked with extracting invariant features from the sensory streams provided by Layer 1 and forming more abstract, categorical representations. This layer is analogous to the primate inferotemporal (IT) cortex, a region known for its role in object recognition and experience-dependent plasticity [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. Neurons in Layer 2 receive two critical streams of information. First, they receive top-down feedback projections from Layer 3, which carry predictions about the category of the current stimulus. Second, they receive bottom-up feedforward projections from Layer 1, carrying the actual sensory prediction error signals. The interplay between these two inputs allows neurons in Layer 2 to refine their selectivity. A neuron that responds to a specific feature (e.g., a particular combination of shapes) will only maintain its activity if the top-down prediction from Layer 3 aligns with the bottom-up sensory evidence from Layer 1. If there is a mismatch, the prediction error signal will drive plasticity, adjusting the weights of the synapses that failed to correctly predict the input. The Latent Predictive Learning (LPL) rule is particularly well-suited for this layer, as its predictive component leverages temporal contiguity to learn disentangled object representations, while its Hebbian component ensures representational stability [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. Over time, as a new class of stimuli is repeatedly encountered, the engrams representing individual instances of that class, initially formed in Layer 1, are progressively recruited and strengthened in Layer 2, allowing the network to build robust category-level representations.

**Layer 3: High-Level Generative Model & Context Provider (Modeled on Prefrontal/Dorsolateral PFC)**
The highest layer of the hierarchy maintains a stable, long-term generative model of the world. This layer is analogous to prefrontal cortical areas, which are implicated in working memory, executive function, and maintaining high-level contextual expectations. Its primary function is to generate the top-down predictions that drive inference in Layer 2. These predictions might encode not just object categories but also semantic relationships, spatial contexts, or anticipated outcomes. Layer 3 operates on a slower timescale than the lower layers, maintaining stable "bump" attractor states that represent these high-level concepts [[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5399521/)]. It receives prediction error signals from Layer 2 and uses them to update its internal generative model. For example, if Layer 2 consistently reports a large error for a prediction generated by Layer 3, Layer 3 might adjust its internal parameters to favor a different hypothesis. This layer effectively provides the "context" for the predictions sent back down to Layer 2, enabling the network to classify stimuli based on a rich, learned understanding of the world rather than just their immediate sensory features.

The connectivity within this architecture is predominantly recurrent and bidirectional. Feedforward connections (from Layer 1 to 2, and Layer 2 to 3) are primarily excitatory and carry prediction error signals. Feedback connections (from Layer 3 to 2, and Layer 2 to 1) are also excitatory but carry the top-down predictions. Crucially, the model incorporates a canonical predictive coding microcircuit involving inhibitory interneurons, which are hypothesized to be the primary carriers of the prediction error signal [[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3777738/), [7](https://www.bu.edu/hasselmo/ChapmanHasselmo2023.pdf)]. This creates a finely tuned balance between excitation and inhibition necessary for stable computation. The overall design is a fully recurrent spiking network, reflecting the reality of cortical connectivity, and its dynamics are governed by the iterative process of prediction and error correction, providing a comprehensive framework for both learning and classification.

## Core Operational Dynamics: From Sensory Encoding to Categorical Classification

The operational dynamics of the Hierarchical Predictive Engram Network (HPEN) are governed by the continuous, iterative process of prediction error minimization, which seamlessly integrates the formation of new memories, the refinement of existing representations, and the execution of classification tasks. This process unfolds across the three layers of the network, transforming a stream of novel sensory inputs into stable, categorized knowledge.

During the **Encoding (Learning) Phase**, the HPEN learns the statistical regularities of its environment in an unsupervised manner. When a novel stimulus is presented to Layer 1, it elicits a specific pattern of activity. Simultaneously, Layer 3, drawing on its high-level generative model, sends a top-down prediction to Layer 2. This prediction might be weak or ambiguous, perhaps corresponding to a general category like "animal" or "vehicle." Layer 2 compares this prediction with the bottom-up sensory evidence arriving from Layer 1. If the prediction does not match the sensory data, a substantial prediction error signal is generated and propagated back up to Layer 3. This error signal acts as a teaching signal, driving synaptic plasticity within Layer 2. Synapses that contribute to the incorrect prediction are weakened, while others that better align with the sensory input are strengthened. This process refines the category representations in Layer 2. Concurrently, the unique sensory trace in Layer 1 is solidified into a more permanent engram, potentially becoming linked to the newly refined category representation in Layer 2. This entire process mirrors the Rescorla-Wagner model, where unexpected outcomes (high prediction error) lead to the strengthening of associations that help predict future events [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. With each subsequent exposure to the same or similar stimuli, the prediction error diminishes as the network's internal model becomes better aligned with the sensory data, leading to the consolidation of a stable categorical engram in Layer 2.

Once a category representation is established, the HPEN can perform **Classification (Inference)** through the mechanism of "explaining away" [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5371651/)]. When a new stimulus is presented, Layer 3 does not send a single, rigid prediction. Instead, it may generate multiple hypotheses or predictions simultaneously, corresponding to different possible categories. Each of these predictions is sent to Layer 2, which evaluates them against the bottom-up sensory evidence from Layer 1. The predictive coding dynamics ensure that the hypothesis which best matches the sensory data will "explain away" the most variance in the lower layers, resulting in the lowest overall prediction error for that specific pathway. The winning hypothesis, the one that minimizes the prediction error across the hierarchy, is selected as the network's classification. This process is equivalent to activating the engram associated with that category. The top-down activation of the category engram in Layer 2 reinstates the corresponding stored pattern of activity, which then propagates back down to Layer 1, priming the network to expect the features associated with that category. Successful classification is thus marked by a state of near-zero prediction error, where the top-down prediction generated from the retrieved engram perfectly accounts for the bottom-up sensory input.

The HPEN model also provides a coherent framework for understanding **Forgetting and Reconsolidation**. If a category is rarely used, the top-down predictions associated with it in Layer 3 may weaken due to lack of reinforcement, a process akin to synaptic depotentiation. When a stimulus from that forgotten category is eventually encountered, the mismatch between the weak prediction from Layer 3 and the strong sensory input from Layer 1 will generate a large prediction error. This strong error signal can trigger a process of reconsolidation, where the previously weakened engram is rapidly re-strengthened to better align with the current sensory evidence, restoring its predictive power. This explains why a brief reminder can restore a seemingly forgotten memory [[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. Conversely, if the prediction consistently fails or if the stimulus is never encountered again, the engram may gradually lose its predictive utility. The persistent inability to generate a correct prediction leads to a reduction in the engram's reactivation potential, mirroring the experimental findings of reduced co-activity between encoding and recall [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)]. This process, mediated by molecular mechanisms like Rac1, represents a form of active forgetting where the memory trace becomes functionally silent, not because it is erased, but because it is no longer deemed relevant by the brain's generative model [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf)]. This dynamic interplay between prediction, error, and plasticity allows the HPEN to maintain a flexible and adaptive representation of the world, capable of learning new categories, recalling old ones, and discarding irrelevant information.

## Biological Plausibility and Computational Implementation

The proposed Hierarchical Predictive Engram Network (HPEN) is designed to be biologically plausible, grounding its computational principles in known neuroanatomical structures, neurophysiological mechanisms, and plausible synaptic plasticity rules. This commitment to biological realism distinguishes it from purely abstract machine learning models and provides a testable hypothesis for how the brain might implement memory and classification.

At the most fundamental level, the HPEN is a spiking neuronal network, composed of excitatory pyramidal cells and inhibitory interneurons, mirroring the basic cellular composition of the neocortex [[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5399521/)]. The network's operation is not based on continuous-valued "activations" but on discrete, time-encoded spikes, which allows for the incorporation of timing-dependent plasticity rules like STDP—a cornerstone of many biologically inspired learning models [[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40651-w), [2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. The connectivity is recurrent, reflecting the dense interlaminar and long-range connections observed in real brains, which are essential for the feedback loops required by predictive coding [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5371651/)]. A critical aspect of its biological plausibility is the inclusion of a canonical microcircuit for predictive coding, which posits that prediction error signals are carried by specific populations of inhibitory interneurons [[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3777738/), [7](https://www.bu.edu/hasselmo/ChapmanHasselmo2023.pdf)]. In the HPEN, this translates to the bottom-up feedforward pathways from Layer 1 to 2 and Layer 2 to 3 carrying prediction error signals, likely implemented through the activity of fast-spiking interneurons that inhibit postsynaptic targets when a prediction mismatch is detected.

The model's learning capabilities are rooted in local, non-linear plasticity rules that do not require global, non-local credit assignment signals. The Latent Predictive Learning (LPL) rule is a prime example of such a mechanism [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. Derived from a principled objective function, LPL operates on a layer-by-layer basis, using only local pre-synaptic activity and post-synaptic prediction error to update synaptic weights. Its predictive component drives the selection of slow, predictable features, while its variance-modulated Hebbian component prevents the network from collapsing into a single representation, a problem that plagues purely predictive or purely Hebbian systems [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. This rule naturally reproduces complex STDP profiles and predicts novel forms of metaplasticity, making it a strong candidate for implementing unsupervised category learning in Layer 2 [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. Similarly, the single-neuron predictive plasticity rule demonstrates how a neuron can learn to anticipate sequential inputs and replay them, a mechanism that could underlie the formation and retrieval of episodic-like traces in Layer 1 [[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40651-w)]. These plasticity rules provide a concrete, computationally tractable implementation of the idea that prediction errors are the primary driver of synaptic change, bridging the gap between cognitive theory and cellular biology.

Furthermore, the HPEN accommodates different forms of memory storage through its hierarchical organization, aligning with a distinction between synaptic and intracellular memory stores [[5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0303264722002064)]. The rapid, labile engrams in Layer 1, dependent on transient changes in synaptic efficacy, correspond to short-term sensory memory. The more stable, abstract engrams in Layer 2, built from invariant features, represent intermediate-term category learning. Finally, the generative model in Layer 3, which may involve longer-term structural changes, could store long-term "fact memories" or probabilistic beliefs about the world [[5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0303264722002064)]. The model also incorporates known biophysical mechanisms for sustaining persistent activity, a key feature for holding category-level predictions in Layer 3. This is achieved through a delicate balance of synaptic conductances with different time constants. Long-duration currents, such as those mediated by NMDA receptors and GABAB receptors (with time constants around 150 ms), are crucial for maintaining a stable "bump" of activity that represents a persistent memory or prediction [[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5399521/)]. In contrast, fast AMPA and GABAa currents (with time constants of ~6 ms) enable precise, rapid signaling. The stability of these bump attractor states critically depends on a "surround inhibition" connectivity topology, where inhibitory neurons preferentially target distant surrounding neurons rather than their nearest neighbors, creating a local valley of low inhibition that allows a small group of excitatory neurons to sustain high firing rates [[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5399521/)]. This detailed neurobiological implementation enhances the model's fidelity and provides a mechanism for the stable maintenance of categorical representations.

## Synthesis and Future Directions

The proposed Hierarchical Predictive Engram Network (HPEN) represents a significant step toward unifying two major paradigms in neuroscience: the study of memory at the cellular level through engrams and the theory of brain function as a whole through predictive coding. By proposing a biologically plausible spiking network architecture grounded in the principle of prediction error-driven plasticity, the HPEN provides a coherent, computationally grounded framework that integrates memory formation, retrieval, and classification into a single, dynamic system. The core insight of this model is that prediction error is the common currency that mediates learning, recall, and forgetting. During learning, prediction errors drive the synaptic plasticity that strengthens engrams and refines category representations. During classification, the process of "explaining away" identifies the stored engram whose top-down prediction best matches the bottom-up sensory evidence, resulting in a state of minimal prediction error. Forgetting is conceptualized as a consequence of the failure to generate a correct prediction, leading to the down-weighting and functional silencing of an engram that is no longer deemed relevant by the brain's generative model. This synthesis offers a powerful explanatory account for how the brain uses stored experiences to inform perception and decision-making, moving beyond a simple dichotomy between memory and processing.

The HPEN model is notable for its strong adherence to biological plausibility. It employs a hierarchical, recurrent spiking network architecture consistent with cortical anatomy. Its computational dynamics are implemented through local, non-linear plasticity rules like Latent Predictive Learning (LPL) and single-neuron predictive plasticity, which derive their learning signals from local pre-synaptic activity and post-synaptic prediction error, avoiding the need for implausible global learning signals [[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40651-w), [2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)]. The model incorporates specific neurophysiological mechanisms, such as the interplay of fast AMPA/GABAa and slow NMDA/GABAB synaptic conductances, to generate stable, localized "bump" attractor states that represent persistent categorical predictions [[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5399521/)]. Furthermore, it accommodates different types of memory storage across its layers, from transient sensory traces in Layer 1 to stable, abstract category engrams in Layer 2 and a high-level generative model in Layer 3, aligning with distinctions between episodic, semantic, and procedural memory systems [[5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0303264722002064), [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12458145/)]. This deep integration of computational principles with known biological constraints makes the HPEN a compelling hypothesis for how the brain achieves its remarkable feats of learning and inference.

Despite its strengths, the HPEN model, as outlined, contains several areas that remain speculative and require further investigation. A primary direction for future work is the explicit and detailed implementation of the canonical predictive coding microcircuit. While the model assumes that inhibitory interneurons carry prediction error signals [[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3777738/), [7](https://www.bu.edu/hasselmo/ChapmanHasselmo2023.pdf)], a more concrete specification of how specific interneuron subtypes (e.g., Martinotti, VIP, SST-positive cells) interact to mediate the flow of these signals between layers is needed for full biological fidelity. Another area requiring deeper exploration is the precise mechanism of engram formation and stabilization at the single-cell level. While the model relies on plasticity rules like LPL [[2](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.17.484712v3.full-text)], the exact translation of these network-level dynamics into downstream molecular cascades, such as those involving the Rac1 protein that regulates dendritic spine density [[3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92860v2/pdf), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537488/)], needs to be articulated. Bridging this gap between network-level prediction error and cellular-level structural plasticity would significantly enhance the model's explanatory power. Finally, extensive simulation is necessary to explore the training dynamics and operational characteristics of the full spiking network. Questions regarding the number of exposures required to convert a sensory engram into a stable categorical engram, the stability of the bump attractor states under noisy conditions, and the precise quantitative performance of the model on classification tasks must be addressed through computational experiments. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for validating the HPEN model and refining our understanding of the intricate relationship between memory and predictive processing in the brain.
