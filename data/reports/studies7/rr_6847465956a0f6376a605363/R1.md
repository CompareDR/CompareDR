# Turo vs. Getaround: Why Two Car-Rental Marketplaces Diverged, 2020–2025

## Executive summary

Turo and Getaround entered 2020 with superficially similar businesses—technology marketplaces connecting vehicle owners with renters—but their economic trajectories diverged sharply. **Turo turned the post-COVID rental-car shortage into scale and sustained operating profitability; Getaround never achieved enough scale to cover its operating costs and ultimately became constrained by liquidity and debt.** Turo's net revenue rose from $149.9 million in 2020 to $958 million in 2024, while gross booking value, or GBV, rose from roughly $336 million in 2020 to $2.5 billion in 2024. Turo reported positive adjusted EBITDA from 2021 onward. Getaround, by comparison, generated only $72.7 million of 2023 revenue and $203.9 million of GBV while still losing $72.0 million on adjusted EBITDA. [[1]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm)

The valuation contrast is even stronger, although the two companies must be measured differently. Turo remained private, so there is **no reliable annual market valuation series**. Its 2019 Series E put it above $1 billion; Forge's private-market database subsequently identifies about **$1.24 billion** as its last primary-round valuation associated with the Series E period. A further $30 million was added to that Series E in February 2020, but neither Turo nor the investors publicly disclosed a new pre- or post-money valuation. Turo then filed for an IPO in January 2022 but never priced it, so the filing created no observable IPO valuation. [[2]](https://www.autorentalnews.com/news/turo-lands-250-million-in-new-funding)

Getaround did receive a hard public valuation mark. Its May 2022 SPAC agreement assumed a **$1.176 billion pro-forma equity value and $900 million enterprise value at $10 per share**, assuming no SPAC redemptions. In reality, holders redeemed 24.325 million SPAC shares before the December closing; Getaround later told the SEC that those redemptions “significantly reduced” its proceeds and left it without enough capital to fund its plan. By July 2024, the New York Stock Exchange suspended Getaround because its average global market capitalization had fallen below the exchange's $15 million minimum; contemporary market data put capitalization at roughly $12 million. Thus, on reasonably comparable equity-value measures, Getaround lost approximately **99% of the announced 2022 SPAC value** in little more than two years. [[3]](https://assets.website-files.com/5c16e90c8f6920b098f834e5/627ed4a42321de25a3bc1929_Getaround%20Investor%20Presentation%20Deck.pdf)

Employment followed the underlying economics. Turo cut about 30% of its workforce when COVID abruptly stopped travel in early 2020, but then rehired aggressively: 429 full-time employees in September 2021, 761 in September 2022, 931 at year-end 2023 and 965 in June 2024. After abandoning the IPO in February 2025, it cut about 150 jobs, or 15%, in April, citing economic uncertainty; this was principally a **growth/cost-discipline reset at a still-profitable company**, rather than an emergency financing event. Getaround cut about 150 employees, or roughly 25%, even before the worst of COVID in January 2020; cut another 10% in February 2023; cut 30% of its North American workforce in February 2024; and laid off nearly all remaining U.S. staff when it stopped U.S. rentals in February 2025. In Getaround's case, layoffs increasingly reflected **cash preservation and solvency risk**, not merely operating efficiency. [[4]](https://www.bizjournals.com/sanfrancisco/news/2020/03/31/turo-hits-rough-road-and-cuts-a-wide-swath-of-jobs.html)

For readers unfamiliar with venture capital, an important distinction runs throughout this report: **funding raised is not valuation**. A company can raise $100 million while being valued at $1 billion; valuation is the negotiated or market value of the whole equity. Likewise, a SPAC's announced valuation is not necessarily the amount of cash a company receives. Public shareholders can redeem their SPAC shares before the merger—as happened dramatically at Getaround. “Adjusted EBITDA” is a management-defined measure of operating profitability before interest, taxes, depreciation and selected adjustments; it is useful for direction but is not equivalent to net income or free cash flow. “GBV” is the total value booked on the marketplace, much of which belongs to vehicle owners and therefore is not the platform's revenue. [[5]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm)

## Turo: scale, profitability and a valuation that remained private

Turo's decisive event was not a financing round but the **2020–21 change in marketplace economics**. In Q2 2020 booked days fell 38% year over year. Management cut costs and headcount and paused much marketing, while introducing the Turo Risk Score in April 2020 to vary guest fees according to risk. Hosts also increased vehicle prices. As travel returned, consumers preferred road trips, while conventional rental-car supply was unusually scarce. Those forces meant that Turo entered the recovery with higher revenue per booking and a leaner expense base. Its contribution margin moved from **–19.1% in 2019 to +11.5% in 2020**, even though the pandemic had reduced transaction volume. [[21]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm)

The result was unusually strong operating leverage in 2021. Revenue more than tripled to $469 million as both booked days and GBV per day increased. Turo's own filing explicitly attributed the improvement to vaccine availability, lifting travel restrictions, greater willingness to travel and a shortage of conventional rental cars. Adjusted EBITDA swung from a $42.1 million loss in 2020 to an $81.1 million profit in 2021. That is the economic event most likely to have raised Turo's private fair value, even though no transaction publicly repriced the whole company. [[22]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm)

The 2022 IPO filing therefore came at an economically logical moment: Turo had moved from pandemic losses to fast growth and positive adjusted EBITDA. But it came into a deteriorating U.S. IPO environment. The Federal Reserve began raising its main policy interest rate in March 2022 and ultimately took the target range from essentially zero to **5.25%–5.50% by July 2023**. Higher interest rates hurt high-growth-company valuations because investors can earn more on safer assets and therefore discount distant future profits more aggressively. They also increase vehicle-financing costs for professional hosts. Turo could afford to wait because its operations were generating earnings; it repeatedly amended its S-1 rather than accept an unattractive market price. [[24]](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20220316a1.htm)

This distinction matters for valuation. **The absence of an IPO was not evidence that Turo's value had fallen to zero or even below its 2020 mark; it simply removed public price discovery.** Stronger revenue, GBV and profitability would normally raise value, while slowing growth and higher discount rates lower the multiple investors are willing to pay for each dollar of revenue or EBITDA. Because no priced equity financing or IPO occurred, assigning a precise Turo value for 2021–25 would be false precision. Forge reports secondary-market activity, including a 2023 secondary transaction, but such trades can involve small blocks, different share classes and illiquidity discounts, and therefore are not automatically equivalent to an enterprise-wide post-money valuation. [[25]](https://forgeglobal.com/turo_ipo/)

The August 2024 IAC transaction is another potential source of confusion. Turo's SEC filing describes it as a **net exercise of an existing warrant**, resulting in 4,488,716 Series E-2 preferred shares. A warrant exercise changes ownership and dilution; it is not automatically a new VC round, and a net exercise need not inject the amount of cash suggested by multiplying shares by an external share-price estimate. For that reason, databases that label the event as a new 2024 “funding round” should not be used to infer a fresh $1.9 billion-type valuation without additional documentation. [[26]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000162828024036158/turoinc-sx1a11.htm)

Turo also expanded internationally, including the acquisition of France's OuiCar, with exchangeable-securities arrangements and a small amount of French government-guaranteed debt inherited with the business. These were strategically relevant but small compared with Turo's marketplace scale and were not major balance-sheet restructurings of the kind later seen at Getaround. [[27]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522304828/d145731ds1a.htm)

Employment traces the same cycle more clearly than valuation does. After the emergency COVID reduction, Turo hired rapidly as transaction volume and customer-support needs recovered. Its filings specifically tie higher operations-and-support staffing to higher travel demand and show increased engineering, product and marketing investment as the company scaled. [[28]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm)

## Getaround: the SPAC valuation collided with cash burn and debt

Getaround's trajectory started from a weaker position. Its roughly 25% workforce reduction in January 2020 preceded the main U.S. pandemic shock and reflected a push toward efficiency during a period in which venture investors—including SoftBank-related portfolios broadly—were increasingly emphasizing paths to profitability. Then COVID hit, driving Getaround's March–April demand down around 75%. The subsequent **$140 million financing in October 2020 was therefore economically a liquidity reinforcement**. Because its valuation was not publicly disclosed, it is not possible to establish whether that round was an up round or down round from primary evidence. [[31]](https://techcrunch.com/2020/01/07/getaround-is-latest-softbank-portfolio-company-to-announce-layoffs/)

Operationally, Getaround did recover, but at a dramatically smaller scale than Turo. The company's May 2022 investor presentation showed GBV rising from **$105 million in 2019 to $136 million in 2020 and $167 million in 2021**. Yet its losses remained larger than its revenue: net losses were approximately $165 million in 2020 and $120 million in 2021. [[10]](https://assets.website-files.com/5c16e90c8f6920b098f834e5/627ed4a42321de25a3bc1929_Getaround%20Investor%20Presentation%20Deck.pdf)

The 2022 SPAC transaction is central to understanding everything that followed. A SPAC is a listed cash shell that merges with a private company, allowing the private company to become publicly traded. Getaround's presentation assumed $10 per share, 117.6 million pro-forma shares, **$1.176 billion equity value**, $276 million of pro-forma net cash, and therefore **$900 million enterprise value**. In ordinary terms, investors were being asked to value Getaround's operating business at roughly $900 million after adjusting for expected cash. [[32]](https://assets.website-files.com/5c16e90c8f6920b098f834e5/627ed4a42321de25a3bc1929_Getaround%20Investor%20Presentation%20Deck.pdf)

Those numbers rested on aggressive projections. The May 2022 presentation projected approximately **$234 million of 2023 net revenue and $493 million of 2023 GBV**, with adjusted EBITDA expected to turn positive around 2024. Actual 2023 results were only **$72.7 million of revenue and $203.9 million of GBV**, roughly 69% and 59% below those respective projections. This gap between the SPAC story and subsequent reality was a powerful reason for investors to slash the multiple they were willing to pay. [[33]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000119312522147020/d289503dex992.htm)

More damagingly, the SPAC's nominal $1.2 billion valuation did **not** mean Getaround received anything close to $1.2 billion. Public SPAC investors had the right to redeem their shares for cash before the merger; holders of 24.325 million shares did so. The closing announcement described about $228 million of “gross proceeds,” but this included the issuance of **$175 million of convertible debt** and conversion of roughly $37.5 million of bridge notes. Getaround's later 10-K explicitly states that the redemptions significantly reduced transaction proceeds and left the company insufficiently capitalized to fund operations for a year. [[34]](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20221209005329/en/Getaround-Announces-Closing-of-Business-Combination-with-InterPrivate-II-Acquisition-Corp)

The $175 million Mudrick notes were themselves burdensome. They were senior and secured, with interest payable at roughly **8% in cash or 9.5% if paid in kind**, and had an initial conversion rate corresponding to about $11.50 per share. “Paid in kind,” or PIK, means the company can conserve cash by adding the interest to the debt balance—but that makes the amount ultimately owed larger. “Senior secured” means the creditor gets a claim on company assets ahead of ordinary shareholders. Thus, financing that kept Getaround alive could simultaneously **reduce the residual value attributable to common stock**. [[35]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024057835/getr-20240331.htm)

The operating picture did improve in some respects in 2023. Revenue rose 22% to $72.7 million and GBV rose 16% to $203.9 million. However, the HyreCar acquisition contributed $19.3 million of the revenue increase and $36.9 million of incremental GBV, while total trips actually fell from 941,000 to 938,000. In other words, the headline growth did not demonstrate strong organic transaction growth. [[36]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm)

Its cost structure remained the deeper problem. In 2023, direct cost of revenue was only about **9% of revenue**, implying attractive accounting gross margin. But operations/support expense was roughly **90% of revenue**, general and administrative expense around **70%**, technology/product 22%, and sales/marketing 26%. Those percentages overlap because they are separate operating-cost categories, and together they explain why a marketplace with strong gross margin could still lose $72 million on adjusted EBITDA on just $73 million of revenue. Scale was too small to absorb the overhead. [[37]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm)

The company responded as distressed businesses normally do: shrink staff, reduce marketing and product spending, seek new credit and give lenders stronger protections. Adjusted EBITDA loss improved from **$89.7 million in 2022 to $72.0 million in 2023**, which Getaround attributed in part to cost reduction. But cash and restricted cash plunged from $67.9 million to $15.6 million during 2023, leaving little runway. The company also dealt with late-filing covenant notices and increasingly senior financing. [[38]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm)

By February 2024, Getaround was cutting **30% of North American staff** explicitly to extend cash runway and accelerate the path toward profitability. Later in 2024, Mudrick committed another $20 million while the parties contemplated converting some existing debt into shares at an adjusted **$0.25 per-share** price. Then came the hardest valuation evidence. On July 9, 2024, the NYSE announced immediate suspension and delisting proceedings because Getaround's average global market capitalization over 30 trading days had fallen below **$15 million**. That is a market judgment, not an accounting adjustment: public investors collectively valued the common equity at a tiny fraction of the 2022 SPAC figure. [[41]](https://ir.theice.com/press/news-details/2024/NYSE-to-Commence-Delisting-Proceedings-Against-Getaround-Inc.-GETR/default.aspx)

Importantly, Getaround's unit economics had not completely collapsed. In Q3 2024 it reported a 90% service gross margin, 48% trip contribution margin, and an adjusted EBITDA loss of $9.3 million versus $11.3 million a year earlier. But revenue was only $22.4 million for the quarter, and the company said suspension of New York State operations was a significant drag. **Good marginal economics on each trip cannot rescue a company if there are too few trips, too much corporate overhead, a large debt stack and too little cash.** [[42]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024127263/getr-ex99_1.htm)

That is precisely what the February 2025 U.S. shutdown demonstrated. Getaround said that despite progress on profitability and restructuring, its continuing lack of liquidity made U.S. operations unviable; it stopped accepting new U.S. rentals after February 11 and laid off nearly all U.S. workers. The causal chain was therefore unusually direct: **persistent losses → cash depletion → expensive senior financing → lower equity value/dilution → layoffs to reduce burn → inability to raise enough fresh capital → U.S. wind-down.** [[18]](https://getaround.com/blog/winding-down-our-carsharing-service-in-the-u-s/)

## Market context and why it affected the two companies differently

**COVID first destroyed demand, then created an unusually favorable rental-car pricing environment.** Turo's filing shows its booked days falling 38% year over year in Q2 2020. As travel returned in 2021, conventional rental supply was scarce. U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data illustrate the magnitude: by June 2021, consumer prices for car and truck rentals were **87.7% higher than a year earlier**, while the used-car market was also exceptionally tight—the used-car CPI jumped 10% in April 2021 alone. Turo explicitly cites conventional rental-car supply shortages as a factor behind its 2021 acceleration. [[43]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm)

For an asset-light marketplace, that was unusually favorable. Traditional rental companies must buy or lease large fleets; Turo primarily obtains supply from hosts who already own cars or operate their own small fleets. When conventional fleets were scarce and rental prices high, Turo could add distributed supply without financing hundreds of thousands of corporate-owned vehicles. Getaround also had an asset-light marketplace component, so it benefited from the same macro trend, but its much smaller transaction base and larger overhead relative to revenue prevented comparable operating leverage. [[44]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm)

The tailwind then normalized. Turo's revenue growth slowed from **213% in 2021 to 59% in 2022, 18% in 2023 and 9% in 2024** as the extraordinary shortage environment faded. That does not mean the business deteriorated—revenue and GBV continued growing—but a company growing 9% is normally valued at a lower revenue multiple than the same company growing 50–200%, all else equal. This helps explain why Turo could become more profitable while simultaneously finding the public-market valuation environment less attractive. [[45]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522156266/d145731ds1a.htm)

**Interest rates created a second valuation shock.** The Federal Reserve began raising rates in March 2022, reached 5.25%–5.50% by July 2023 and held that level well into 2024. In plain language, when safe government bonds pay much more, investors are less willing to pay extraordinary prices today for profits a startup might earn many years in the future. Debt is also more expensive. This hurt Getaround twice—through lower equity multiples and through its need for outside borrowing—while Turo's positive EBITDA gave it the option simply to postpone its IPO. [[46]](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20220316a1.htm)

Getaround's SPAC timing was consequently unfortunate. The transaction was announced in May 2022 just as the U.S. monetary tightening cycle and post-2021 technology-valuation reset were accelerating. SPAC investors exercised their redemption rights at high levels, reducing the cash actually delivered. The company's subsequent failure to come close to its 2023 SPAC projections compounded the repricing. Turo avoided that same public-market mechanism because its S-1 never became effective and no shares were sold. [[47]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000119312522147020/d289503dex991.htm)

**Insurance and claims became another industry headwind.** U.S. consumer motor-vehicle-insurance prices increased **20.3% during 2023** and another **11.3% during 2024**. Those CPI figures are not the exact commercial insurance rates Turo or Getaround paid, so they should be treated as a broad cost signal rather than a direct expense proxy. Turo's filings nevertheless identify physical-damage claim frequency/severity and insurance/protection costs as material drivers of cost of revenue. Higher repair costs and insurance pricing therefore put pressure on marketplace economics even when booking demand remained healthy. [[48]](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/consumer-price-index-2023-in-review.htm)

Regulation adds another structural cost. Peer-to-peer car sharing is regulated state by state in the U.S., with rules involving insurance, taxes, vehicle eligibility and airport access. Both companies warn that changing regulatory requirements can increase costs or restrict availability. Getaround's 2024 results provide a concrete example: suspension of New York State operations was a material contributor to lower revenue. Public disclosures available for this research do not establish a single simple regulatory cause for that suspension, so it should not be attributed to insurance regulation alone. [[49]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000119312522150304/d327368d8k.htm)

Viewed against one another, the two companies provide an unusually clean marketplace case study. Turo reached enough scale that fixed technology, support, safety and corporate costs could be spread across billions of dollars of GBV. Getaround demonstrated that **high gross margin is not the same as a viable company**: its 2023 direct cost of revenue was only 9% of revenue, but support, G&A, technology and marketing expenses overwhelmed gross profit. Once Getaround had to finance those losses with high-priority debt rather than cheap venture equity, the value increasingly migrated from common shareholders toward creditors. [[50]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm)

In simplified valuation terms:

**Turo:** COVID cost cuts + risk-based pricing → post-COVID rental scarcity → rapid GBV/revenue growth → positive EBITDA → capacity to wait for a better IPO market → continued private value, but no observable 2021–25 market valuation.

**Getaround:** smaller scale + persistent losses → ambitious SPAC valuation/projections → redemptions reduce cash → projections missed → cash burn → increasingly expensive/senior debt → dilution and equity repricing → repeated layoffs → NYSE delisting → U.S. shutdown.

The essential difference was therefore **not merely which company raised more money or went public first**. Turo produced enough operating cash economics to retain strategic choice. Getaround went public before it had reached sustainable scale and then became dependent on external capital precisely when that capital became much more expensive. [[51]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000162828024008965/turoinc-sx1a9.htm)

## Data gaps, uncertainties and assumptions

**Turo valuation is the largest gap.** Turo publicly stated that the 2019 IAC Series E took it above a $1 billion valuation; Forge's database subsequently gives approximately $1.24 billion as the last-known primary-round valuation around the 2020 Series E period. The February 2020 $30 million Series E extension did not disclose a new pre-money or post-money figure. Because the 2022–25 IPO process never reached pricing, there is no defensible public valuation mark for those years. Any chart showing a precise annual Turo valuation curve would therefore be partly invented. [[2]](https://www.autorentalnews.com/news/turo-lands-250-million-in-new-funding)

Private secondary transactions are also not treated as equivalent to a priced VC round. Forge records secondary activity, including an August 2023 transaction, but a secondary transaction generally transfers existing shares between investors rather than putting new capital into the company. Unless the transaction's share class, price, volume and contemporaneous fully diluted share count are all known, extrapolating it into a whole-company valuation can be misleading. [[52]](https://forgeglobal.com/turo_ipo/)

The **Getaround $1.176 billion figure is an implied transaction valuation, not an amount paid in cash**. It came from the May 2022 SPAC presentation at $10 per share and assumed no redemptions. Actual redemptions were very large. Conversely, the roughly $12 million July 2024 figure is a contemporaneous public-market capitalization. Comparing them is appropriate for showing the collapse in shareholder value, but they arise from different valuation mechanisms. [[53]](https://assets.website-files.com/5c16e90c8f6920b098f834e5/627ed4a42321de25a3bc1929_Getaround%20Investor%20Presentation%20Deck.pdf)

Employee numbers are also uneven. Turo's 2021–24 SEC figures are comparable full-time-employee counts. The early-2020 Turo and Getaround figures and the 2025 Turo figure rely partly on press reports and arithmetic around announced layoff percentages. Getaround's 2023 layoff coverage cited LinkedIn headcount, while SEC filings reported full-time employees; those populations are not interchangeable. Its February 2024 cut applied specifically to North America rather than its entire global workforce. For that reason, no false annual global headcount series has been constructed for Getaround. [[54]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm)

Financial measures are not perfectly identical between the companies. Each defines GBV and adjusted EBITDA in its own filings; both exclude or adjust items that GAAP net income does not. In particular, Turo's 2022 $154.7 million GAAP net income included a substantial warrant fair-value effect, while Getaround's GAAP losses were likewise affected by fair-value movements in convertible instruments and warrants. The report therefore emphasizes revenue, GBV, contribution economics, operating trends and adjusted EBITDA together rather than using any single headline profit number. [[55]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000162828024008965/turoinc-sx1a9.htm)

Finally, 2024–25 disclosure quality is asymmetric. Turo supplied 2024 operating figures through company communications after its last public S-1 amendments, while Getaround's deteriorating listing status and eventual U.S. wind-down made full-year comparability less clean. Where a precise full-year figure could not be verified from the cited primary or high-quality secondary material, it has been omitted rather than estimated. The central conclusion does not depend on filling those gaps: by the end of the period, **Turo was a roughly $1 billion-revenue, EBITDA-positive private marketplace; Getaround had fallen from a $1.176 billion SPAC headline valuation to a sub-$15 million NYSE capitalization threshold and then exited the U.S. market for lack of liquidity.** [[56]](https://turo.com/blog/news/celebrating-13-years-of-drive/)
