## Introduction — The P2P Car Rental Industry and Key Financial Concepts

### What peer-to-peer car rental is and how Turo and Getaround operate

**Peer-to-peer (P2P) car rental** is a business model in which private car owners list their own vehicles on an online platform, and renters book those cars by the day or week — much like Airbnb connects travelers with people who have a spare room. The platform itself owns no cars; it simply matches supply (car owners, called "hosts") with demand (renters, called "guests") and takes a commission on each transaction. This is the fundamental difference from traditional rental companies such as **Hertz** and **Enterprise**, which own and maintain large fleets of vehicles themselves, employ staff at physical rental counters, and bear the costs of vehicle depreciation, insurance, and upkeep. In the P2P model, those costs fall on the individual car owner; the platform provides the software, payment processing, and (usually) a layer of insurance, and in return keeps a percentage of each rental's value.

Both companies profiled in this report are frequently described as the ["Airbnb for cars"](https://www.nerdwallet.com/travel/learn/airbnb-for-cars-best-app-rent-peoples-cars) because they apply the same marketplace logic — underutilized private assets monetized through a digital platform — to the car rental industry.

---

#### Turo Inc.

**Turo** was founded in 2009 in Boston, Massachusetts, under the name **RelayRides** by Shelby Clark. The company [rebranded to Turo in November 2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company)) to reflect a strategic shift away from short-term, hour-by-hour rentals toward longer-duration bookings. Turo is headquartered in San Francisco, California, and as of 2025 operates in five countries: the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, France, and Australia. The company's CEO is **Andre Haddad**, who has led the company through its growth phase and its ultimately withdrawn attempt to go public.

Turo's largest shareholder is **IAC Inc.** (InterActiveCorp), a New York-based media and internet holding company. IAC [invested $250 million in Turo in July 2019](https://www.cnbc.com/2019/07/17/iac-invests-250-million-in-car-sharing-company-turo.html), making it the company's largest shareholder with a stake of approximately [31%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company)) (some sources cite up to 32%). IAC also has rights to expand its ownership over time. Turo has raised a total of approximately [$557.9 million](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/) in venture capital funding across multiple rounds.

Turo's commission structure is tiered: the platform takes [between 10% and 40% of each rental's value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company)), depending on the level of insurance protection the host selects. Hosts who choose minimal coverage keep a larger share (up to 90% of the trip price), while those who opt for comprehensive coverage receive a smaller share but greater protection against damage or liability. Renters may also pay additional trip fees and young-driver surcharges.

As of March 2024, Turo's platform hosted [360,000 active vehicle listings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company)) and more than [140,000 active hosts](https://turo.com/blog/news/celebrating-13-years-of-drive/), with approximately 1,000 employees. The company does not own or maintain any vehicles — every car on the platform belongs to an individual owner or a small commercial fleet operator.

---

#### Getaround Inc.

**Getaround** was founded on September 9, 2009, in San Francisco, California, by **Sam Zaid**, Jessica Scorpio, and Elliot Kroo. The company [launched publicly on May 24, 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround), at the TechCrunch Disrupt conference, where it won the competition. Like Turo, Getaround is headquartered in San Francisco and operates a P2P marketplace connecting car owners with renters.

At its peak, Getaround was significantly larger in geographic reach than Turo. As of May 2022, the company's platform had [66,000 active cars and trucks](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000119312522147020/d289503dex991.htm) across more than 950 cities in 8 countries, with 1.6 million guests. A 2020 company blog post described Getaround as having [5 million users in 300 cities worldwide](https://getaround.com/blog/getarounds-next-chapter/). However, following the cessation of US operations on February 11, 2025, Getaround's footprint contracted to [eight European markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround) — France, Germany, Spain, Austria, Belgium, the United Kingdom, Norway, and the Netherlands — operated through its Drivy subsidiary (acquired in 2019 for $300 million).

Getaround's commission structure was notably more expensive for hosts than Turo's. The platform charged vehicle owners a [40% commission on every trip](https://www.leli.rentals/review/getaround) — the highest rate among major P2P car rental platforms — plus mandatory installation of a proprietary "Getaround Connect" device (approximately $99 upfront) that enabled keyless entry. Renters paid additional guest fees of 15–30% on top of the host's listed price, meaning the combined platform cut could reach 55–70% of the total transaction value.

Getaround raised approximately $600 million in total funding (some sources, including Sacra, report up to $750 million when including a 2018 SoftBank round). Key funding events included a [$300 million round from SoftBank in August 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround) and a [$140 million Series E in October 2020](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/14/getaround-raises-a-140-million-series-e-amid-rebound-in-short-distance-travel/) led by SoftBank Vision Fund. The company also made two acquisitions in 2019 — Drivy ($300 million) and Nabobil ($12 million) — that expanded its European presence but consumed significant capital.

Sam Zaid served as Getaround's CEO from founding through early 2024. In February 2024, the board appointed Eduardo Iniguez as CEO, and **AJ Lee** — who had joined Getaround as VP of Gig Marketplace in May 2023 — was promoted to Chief Operating Officer and later served as [interim CEO](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/GETR/getaround-reports-full-year-2023-jpbc5nn2cqi8.html) during the company's 2024 collapse.

#### The central question

Both companies pioneered the same business model in the same city, founded in the same year. Yet by 2025, Turo remained a private company valued in the billions of dollars, with nearly $1 billion in annual revenue and a profitable track record, while Getaround had seen its public-market valuation collapse from $1.2 billion at its December 2022 stock market debut to effectively zero, culminating in the shutdown of its US operations and a planned dissolution of the entire company. The sections that follow trace exactly how and why this divergence occurred, year by year, from 2020 through 2025.

### Key economic and venture-capital concepts readers need to know

This section defines every financial term used later in the report, in plain language, so readers without a background in US economics or venture capital can follow the valuation stories of Turo and Getaround.

---

**Valuation** is an estimate of what a company is "worth" at a given moment. For a **public company** — one whose shares trade on a stock exchange like the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) — this worth is captured by **market capitalization** (often called "market cap"), which is simply the current share price multiplied by the total number of shares outstanding. For example, if a company has 10 million shares trading at $5 each, its market cap is $50 million. Market cap changes every trading day, sometimes every minute, as the share price moves.

For a **private company** — one whose shares are not publicly traded, like Turo — there is no daily share price. Instead, valuation is set only when the company raises money from investors in a **funding round**, or when existing shares are sold on a secondary market. Between those events, the last agreed-upon valuation simply carries forward. This is why Turo's private valuation could remain at roughly $1.24 billion for years even as its business grew: no new funding round had priced it higher. ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/))

---

**Funding rounds** are the primary way startups raise money. In a funding round, a company sells a portion of its ownership (equity) to investors — typically venture capital firms, hedge funds, or wealthy individuals — in exchange for cash to fund operations and growth. Rounds are labeled sequentially: **Seed** (the earliest, smallest round), then **Series A, B, C, D, E**, and so on. Each letter represents a new fundraising event, usually at a higher valuation than the last, reflecting the company's growth and reduced risk. ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/personal-finance/102015/series-b-c-funding-what-it-all-means-and-how-it-works.asp))

**Series E** is the fifth major funding round. By the time a company reaches Series E, it is typically well-established, generating significant revenue, and may be preparing for an IPO or a major expansion. Turo's Series E, raised in July 2019, brought in $250 million from IAC (its largest shareholder) and valued the company at over $1 billion. Getaround's Series E, raised in October 2020, brought in $140 million led by SoftBank Vision Fund. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/07/17/peer-to-peer-car-sharing-marketplace-turo-raises-250m-at-over-1b-valuation-from-iac/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/14/getaround-raises-a-140-million-series-e-amid-rebound-in-short-distance-travel/))

**Post-money valuation** is the company's estimated value *after* the new investor money has been added. If a company was valued at $80 million before a round (the "pre-money" valuation) and raises $20 million, its post-money valuation is $100 million. The investors who put in that $20 million now own 20% of the company ($20M ÷ $100M). This matters because each funding round **dilutes** — reduces — the ownership percentage of existing shareholders, including the founders.

---

**Unicorn** is the nickname given to a private company valued at $1 billion or more. The term signals rarity and success in the startup world. Turo became a unicorn in 2019 when its Series E from IAC set a post-money valuation above $1 billion. Getaround was reported to have a $1.7 billion valuation in 2019, though this figure was never confirmed by a priced funding round at that level. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/07/17/peer-to-peer-car-sharing-marketplace-turo-raises-250m-at-over-1b-valuation-from-iac/); [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/carsharing-startup-getaround-reportedly-raising-200m-at-1-7b-valuation/))

---

**IPO (Initial Public Offering)** is the process by which a private company first sells its shares to the public on a stock exchange. In a traditional IPO, the company files a detailed registration statement (Form S-1) with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), disclosing its financials, business model, risks, and management. After SEC review and approval, the company's shares begin trading on an exchange like the NYSE. Turo filed its S-1 on January 10, 2022, and was approved to list on the NYSE under the ticker "TURO," but it never completed the offering — it kept filing updated amendments (S-1/A) while waiting for better market conditions, and ultimately withdrew its filing on February 13, 2025. ([Turo S-1 filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/))

---

**SPAC (Special Purpose Acquisition Company)** is an alternative path to going public. A SPAC is a "blank-check" shell company that raises money from public investors through its own IPO first, with no actual business operations. It then has a limited time (typically two years) to find and merge with a private company, effectively taking that private company public without the private company going through the traditional S-1 IPO process. The merger is faster and involves less upfront disclosure, but the resulting public company often faces intense market scrutiny immediately. ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/b/blankcheckcompany.asp))

Getaround chose a SPAC merger in 2022, combining with **InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp** to list on the NYSE under the ticker GETR. The deal was announced in May 2022 with an implied equity valuation of approximately $1.2 billion and completed on December 8, 2022. On its first day of trading (December 9, 2022), Getaround's shares dropped as much as 65%. This crash reflected both company-specific concerns and a broader collapse in SPAC performance: the **CNBC SPAC Post Deal Index**, which tracks SPACs after they complete their mergers, had fallen over 60% in the year before Getaround's debut. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/06/27/spacs-wipe-out-half-of-their-value-as-investors-lose-appetite-for-risky-growth-stocks.html))

The key difference: a traditional IPO (Turo's path) involves extensive SEC review, a roadshow to market the shares to institutional investors, and a gradual pricing process. A SPAC merger (Getaround's path) is faster but can leave the company exposed to immediate market forces without the cushion of a carefully managed debut. When the SPAC market collapsed in 2022, companies that went public via SPAC — including Getaround — suffered disproportionately.

---

**Gross Booking Value (GBV)** is the total dollar value of all bookings made on a platform before any fees or commissions are deducted. For a car rental marketplace, GBV represents the full amount renters pay for all trips. **Net revenue** (often just called "revenue" in financial statements) is the platform's cut — the commission or fee it retains after paying the car owner their share. For example, if Getaround facilitated $65.1 million in total bookings (GBV) in Q3 2024, its net revenue was a fraction of that, reflecting the platform's take rate. Both Turo and Getaround report GBV as a measure of marketplace activity, but their actual revenue — the money they keep — is much smaller. ([Getaround Q3 2024 results](https://www.otcmarkets.com/stock/GETR/news/Getaround-Reports-Third-Quarter-2024-Financial-Results?id=459143))

---

**Net income** (or **net loss**) is the bottom-line profit or loss after *all* expenses — including cost of revenue, marketing, salaries, rent, interest, taxes, and depreciation — have been subtracted from total revenue. A company with positive net income is "profitable" on a **GAAP** (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) basis, meaning it follows the standard accounting rules that all US public companies must use. A net loss means the company spent more than it earned. Turo reported net income of $154.7 million in 2022 (its most profitable year) but only ~$14.7 million in 2023. Getaround posted net losses throughout its public life, including -$46.8 million in 2022 and -$15.5 million in Q3 2024. ([Turo S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm); [StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/GETR/getaround-reports-third-quarter-2024-financial-wvedc64aodrd.html))

---

**Adjusted EBITDA** is a profitability metric that starts with net income and then adds back certain expenses that are considered non-cash, non-recurring, or not reflective of core operations. EBITDA stands for "Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization." The "adjusted" version goes further, also adding back items like stock-based compensation, one-time legal settlements, and restructuring costs. The goal is to show what the company's underlying business operations earn, stripped of accounting noise. However, because companies choose which items to add back, adjusted EBITDA is not standardized and can be more flattering than GAAP net income. Both Turo and Getaround report adjusted EBITDA alongside GAAP figures. For example, Getaround's Q3 2024 net loss was -$15.5 million, but its adjusted EBITDA loss was a smaller figure, reflecting the add-backs. Adjusted EBITDA is a **non-GAAP** metric, meaning it is not calculated according to standard accounting rules. ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/adjusted-ebitda.asp); [Getaround 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm))

A company can be "profitable" on an adjusted EBITDA basis while still posting a GAAP net loss — a situation that applied to Getaround for much of its public life. This distinction matters because investors and lenders often look at adjusted EBITDA to assess whether a company's core business is viable, even if one-time costs or non-cash expenses push the official bottom line into the red.

---

**Delisting** is the removal of a company's stock from a major stock exchange like the NYSE. Exchanges set **continued listing standards** that companies must meet to remain listed. The NYSE requires, among other things, that a company maintain a minimum average 30-day market capitalization of $15 million, a minimum stock price (generally above $1), at least 400 stockholders, and timely filing of financial reports. If a company falls below these thresholds, the exchange sends a **noncompliance notice** and gives the company a grace period (typically six months) to cure the deficiency. If it fails, the exchange initiates delisting proceedings. ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/09/stock-delist.asp))

Getaround received a continued-listing standard notice from the NYSE in February 2023 because its stock price had fallen below $1 (trading at ~$0.64) and its market cap had fallen below $50 million. In July 2024, the NYSE suspended trading and commenced delisting proceedings against Getaround's common stock for failing to meet the $15 million minimum market capitalization requirement. The stock was transferred to **OTC Markets** (specifically OTCQB, under the same ticker GETR), where it trades with far less liquidity and transparency. After the move to OTC, Getaround's market cap collapsed to approximately $20,000. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/02/01/car-sharing-platform-getaround-gets-delisting-warning-from-nyse/); [ICE/NYSE](https://ir.theice.com/press/news-details/2024/NYSE-to-Commence-Delisting-Proceedings-Against-Getaround-Inc.-GETR/default.aspx); [Simply Wall St](https://simplywall.st/stocks/us/transportation/otc-getr/getaround))

---

**Runway** refers to how long a company can continue operating before it runs out of cash, assuming current spending and revenue levels stay the same. It is typically expressed in months. For example, if a company has $30 million in cash and is spending $3 million per month more than it earns (its "burn rate"), it has 10 months of runway. Startups that are not yet profitable constantly monitor runway because running out of cash means bankruptcy or a forced fire sale. When Getaround laid off 30% of its North American workforce in February 2024, the company explicitly stated the goal was to "extend the cash runway" — in other words, to reduce monthly spending so the remaining cash would last longer. ([Corporate Finance Institute](https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/valuation/cash-runway-explained/); [BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240207550685/en/Getaround-Announces-Restructuring-Plan-to-Accelerate-the-Path-to-Profitability))

---

**Liquidity** has two related meanings in this report. First, for a company, liquidity refers to how easily it can meet its short-term financial obligations — paying bills, making payroll, servicing debt — using the cash and assets it has on hand. A company with high liquidity can pay its bills without difficulty; a company with low liquidity may struggle to meet payroll or be forced to raise emergency funding at unfavorable terms. When Getaround announced the shutdown of its US operations in February 2025, it cited an "ongoing lack of liquidity" — meaning it simply did not have enough cash to continue operating in the US market. ([NetSuite](https://www.netsuite.com/portal/resource/articles/accounting/liquidity.shtml); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/))

Second, for a stock, liquidity refers to how easily shares can be bought or sold without significantly affecting the price. Stocks on major exchanges like the NYSE are highly liquid — there are always buyers and sellers. Stocks on OTC markets, like Getaround after its delisting, are much less liquid, meaning it can be hard to find a buyer, and the price may be very low as a result.

---

**Why these concepts matter for the Turo-Getaround comparison:** The fundamental structural difference between the two companies' valuation trajectories comes down to private versus public status. Turo remained private throughout the period covered by this report. Its valuation only moved when new funding rounds or secondary-market transactions priced it — and because it was growing revenue and achieving profitability, those valuations trended upward (from ~$1.24 billion in 2020 to a range of ~$1.7–$2.7 billion by 2024). Getaround went public via SPAC in December 2022, and from that moment its valuation was set by the daily market — which reflected its small revenue ($62.3 million in 2022), persistent losses, and inability to achieve profitability. The market cap collapsed from $1.2 billion at the SPAC announcement to ~$24 million by mid-2024 to ~$20,000 after the OTC transfer — a decline of over 99.99%. Turo's patience as a private company preserved its valuation; Getaround's public exposure accelerated its collapse.

## Turo — Economic Events and Valuation Trajectory, 2020–2025

### 2020 — COVID shock: layoffs, revenue stalls, Germany exit, Series E extension

When the COVID-19 pandemic swept across the globe in early 2020, Turo — the San Francisco-based peer-to-peer (P2P) car rental marketplace — faced an immediate crisis. Unlike traditional rental companies such as Hertz or Enterprise, which own their fleets, Turo simply connects owners and renters. This meant that when travel collapsed, Turo's revenue stream — which depends entirely on people renting cars — dried up almost overnight.

**Layoffs and workforce reduction.** In April 2020, as shelter-in-place orders brought travel to a near standstill, Turo laid off 108 employees, representing approximately 30% of its workforce, across all departments including its San Francisco and Phoenix offices ([Layoffs.fyi](https://layoffs.fyi/2020/04/21/turo-laid-off-108-employees/)). The cuts were part of a broader wave of pandemic-driven layoffs across the travel sector: competitors Getaround and Zipcar also reduced staff during the same period. The layoffs reflected a sudden and severe drop in consumer demand — with people confined to their homes, there was simply no market for short-term car rentals.

**2020 financial results: revenue stall and net loss.** According to Turo's S-1 registration statement filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) in January 2022, the company generated net revenue of $149.9 million in 2020 — a mere 6% increase from $141.7 million in 2019 ([Turo S-1, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm); [TechCrunch, Jan. 11, 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/01/11/turos-s-1-filing-shows-that-unicorns-can-slingshot-out-of-the-pandemic/)). For a venture-backed company preparing for an IPO, this was an alarmingly thin growth rate. More critically, Turo posted a net loss of $97.1 million in 2020, essentially unchanged from the $98.6 million loss in 2019. The loss was driven in part by high fixed costs — particularly insurance and host protection program costs, which consumed 54% of net revenue in the first nine months of 2020, compared to 33% in the same period of 2021 as the business recovered ([TechCrunch, Jan. 11, 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/01/11/turos-s-1-filing-shows-that-unicorns-can-slingshot-out-of-the-pandemic/)). Adjusted EBITDA — a profitability metric that excludes certain non-cash and one-time costs — was negative $38.1 million for the full year, though the company briefly reached positive adjusted EBITDA in Q3 2020 as cost-cutting took effect ([Turo S-1/A, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000162828024008965/turoinc-sx1a9.htm)).

**Germany P2P market exit.** On April 9, 2020, Turo announced it would cease all private peer-to-peer listings in Germany, cancel future bookings, and shift to a model allowing only commercial hosts — dedicated small rental companies — to operate on the platform ([Wikipedia: Turo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company)); [CNET, Apr. 10, 2020](https://www.cnet.com/roadshow/car-apps/turo-germany-p2p-business-dead-coronavirus-covid19/)). Turo had entered the German market in early 2018 after acquiring Daimler's Croove car-sharing platform ([Wikipedia: Turo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company))). The closure was attributed to the sudden drop in travel and mobility during the pandemic, which made the peer-to-peer model — where private individuals rent out their personal cars — unworkable in Germany. CEO Andre Haddad stated the company remained "fully committed" to its P2P marketplaces in the U.S., Canada, and U.K. ([CNET, Apr. 10, 2020](https://www.cnet.com/roadshow/car-apps/turo-germany-p2p-business-dead-coronavirus-covid19/)). The financial impact of the Germany exit was not separately disclosed, but the move reduced Turo's international footprint at a time when cross-border travel had effectively stopped.

**The IAC Series E and valuation.** Despite the losses, Turo's private-market valuation held steady — consistent with how private-company valuations function (private companies are only valued when new funding rounds or secondary-market transactions price them; see S1.2 for the full explanation). Turo's most recent priced funding round was a $250 million Series E led by IAC Inc. (the internet media company that owns brands like Match.com and OkCupid) in July 2019. This round valued Turo at over $1 billion, making it a unicorn ([TechCrunch, Jul. 17, 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/07/17/peer-to-peer-car-sharing-marketplace-turo-raises-250m-at-over-1b-valuation-from-iac/)). IAC became Turo's largest shareholder, with rights to expand its ownership over time ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/startups/turo-raises-another-30m-to-series-e/)). In February 2020, just weeks before the pandemic's full impact hit, Turo added a $30 million extension to the Series E, bringing total funding raised to approximately $500 million ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/startups/turo-raises-another-30m-to-series-e/)). This $1 billion-plus valuation remained the most recent private valuation mark throughout 2020 and beyond, because no new funding round occurred during the pandemic year.

**Pandemic-era trust and safety: the Turo Risk Score.** In April 2020, as the pandemic began, Turo introduced the Turo Risk Score — a system that evaluates 50 data points from each driver to assess risk and determine fees ([Turo S-1, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm); [Claims Journal, Jan. 3, 2025](https://www.claimsjournal.com/news/national/2025/01/03/328217.htm)). This was a pandemic-era trust-and-safety feature designed to help the platform manage risk during a period of heightened uncertainty, when rental patterns were shifting and new types of renters were using the platform.

**The paradoxical recovery.** While 2020 was devastating on the surface, the pandemic ultimately created a powerful tailwind for P2P car sharing. As the initial shock subsided, several forces converged to benefit Turo. First, people avoided public transit and ride-sharing services due to health concerns, increasing demand for personal vehicle access. Second, traditional rental companies like Hertz and Enterprise — facing their own financial crises — sold off large portions of their fleets, creating a severe shortage of rental cars. Third, as travel restrictions eased and vaccines became available in 2021, pent-up demand for travel surged. Turo's S-1 filing explicitly cited a "rental car supply shortage" as a compounding factor in its revenue growth ([TechCrunch, Jan. 11, 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/01/11/turos-s-1-filing-shows-that-unicorns-can-slingshot-out-of-the-pandemic/)). By mid-2021, Turo hosts were reporting bookings two to three times per week, with some hosts expanding their fleets to meet demand ([CNBC, Aug. 10, 2021](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/08/10/an-auto-rental-shortage-sparked-a-boom-in-peer-to-peer-car-sharing-.html)). CEO Andre Haddad noted that "with traditional rental car companies having limited inventory and charging sky-high rates for the cars they do have, our hosts have been able to capitalize on this moment in time" ([CNBC, Aug. 10, 2021](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/08/10/an-auto-rental-shortage-sparked-a-boom-in-peer-to-peer-car-sharing-.html)). The company's revenue would go on to surge 213% in 2021 to $469 million, and Turo achieved its first operating profit — but that recovery story belongs to the next chapter.

### 2021–2022 — Explosive rebound: 213% then 59% revenue growth, first profits, IPO filing, international expansion

After the pandemic-driven shock of 2020 — when Turo laid off 30% of its workforce and posted a $97.1 million net loss — the company staged one of the most dramatic recoveries in the peer-to-peer car rental industry. The rebound was powered by a confluence of factors: pandemic travel restrictions eased, consumers sought alternatives to public transit, and traditional rental companies like Hertz and Enterprise had sold off large portions of their fleets during the downturn, creating a supply shortage that pushed renters toward P2P platforms ([TechCrunch, March 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/24/turo-just-dropped-its-2022-financials-as-its-ipo-hunt-continues/)).

**2021: The 213% surge and first operating profit.** In 2021, Turo's revenue surged 213% to $469 million, up from $149.9 million in 2020. The company achieved its first operating profit of $46.6 million, a striking turnaround from the $97.1 million net loss posted the year before. However, Turo still recorded a GAAP net loss of $40.4 million in 2021, meaning the company was not yet profitable on a fully-loaded accounting basis — but the trajectory was unmistakably positive ([TechCrunch, March 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/); [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)). The 213% growth rate was not sustainable; it reflected the mathematical effect of recovering from a depressed 2020 baseline. By 2022, growth had decelerated to 59%, and by 2023 it would slow further to 18% — a pattern typical of companies emerging from a crisis-driven trough.

**New York State re-entry.** A significant regulatory milestone accompanied the recovery. Turo had been barred from New York State since 2014, when the state's Department of Financial Services fined the company $200,000 for insurance-related violations. In January 2021, Turo was permitted to partially reopen its platform in New York, marking its first return to the state in seven years ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company))). Full re-entry followed in June 2022, after New York passed Senate Bill 6715, a peer-to-peer car-sharing law that established a legal framework for platforms like Turo to operate in the state ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company))). Regaining access to New York — the largest car rental market in the United States — removed a major growth constraint and contributed to the 2022 revenue acceleration.

**2022: Peak profitability and the IPO filing.** In 2022, Turo's revenue grew another 59% to $746.6 million. The company's net income reached $154.7 million — its most profitable year on record. However, this headline figure requires context. Turo's operating income (profit from core business operations before non-operating items like interest and taxes) was a more modest $33.8 million in 2022, down from $46.6 million in 2021. The roughly $121 million gap between operating income and net income indicates significant non-operating items — likely including interest income on the company's cash reserves and potentially a one-time tax benefit related to the company's transition from accumulated losses to profitability. Turo's adjusted EBITDA (a profitability metric that excludes non-operating items, stock-based compensation, and other adjustments) was $79.7 million in 2022, essentially flat compared to $81.1 million in 2021 — confirming that core profitability was stable even as the GAAP net income figure was inflated by below-the-line items ([TechCrunch, March 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/24/turo-just-dropped-its-2022-financials-as-its-ipo-hunt-continues/)).

On January 11, 2022, Turo filed its S-1 registration statement with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), becoming the first major company to file for an IPO that year. Turo planned to list on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker symbol TURO, with Wall Street banks Morgan Stanley and J.P. Morgan underwriting the offering. Renaissance Capital, an IPO research firm, estimated the deal could raise up to $300 million. At the time of filing, Turo reported over 85,000 car owners renting out approximately 160,000 vehicles across the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom, with more than 1.3 million users having booked over 7.4 million rental days in the first nine months of 2021 ([Fortune, January 2022](https://fortune.com/2022/01/11/turo-car-sharing-ipo-filing-nyse/)).

**International expansion.** Turo used its recovery to accelerate international growth. In June 2022, the company acquired OuiCar, a leading French peer-to-peer car-sharing marketplace, for an undisclosed sum. The acquisition gave Turo an established presence in France and access to OuiCar's host and renter base ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company)); [Bloomberg, June 2022](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-06-01/turo-acquires-car-sharing-service-ouicar-to-expand-in-europe)). In October 2022, Turo announced its expansion into Australia, which went live on November 29, 2022 — the company's second international expansion of the year and its first in the Asia-Pacific region ([TechCrunch, October 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/10/18/turo-is-expanding-car-sharing-to-australia/); [BusinessWire, October 2022](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20221018005502/en/Car-Sharing-Marketplace-Turo-Launches-Down-Under)). These expansions increased costs — sales and marketing spending grew from $52.7 million in 2021 to $111.3 million in 2022 — but also broadened Turo's addressable market and revenue base ([TechCrunch, March 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/24/turo-just-dropped-its-2022-financials-as-its-ipo-hunt-continues/)).

**Why the IPO was not completed.** Despite filing the S-1 in January 2022, Turo never completed its IPO. The timing was unfortunate: 2021 had been a record year for IPOs, with 416 companies going public and raising nearly $156 billion, but the market turned sharply in 2022 as the Federal Reserve raised interest rates to combat inflation and tech stock valuations collapsed ([Fortune, January 2022](https://fortune.com/2022/01/11/turo-car-sharing-ipo-filing-nyse/)). Rather than proceed with an offering at depressed valuations, Turo chose to wait. The company continued filing updated S-1/A amendments — quarterly updates that kept the registration statement current — signaling that it still intended to go public but was holding for better market conditions ([TechCrunch, March 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/); [Forge Global](https://forgeglobal.com/turo_ipo/)). This patience would prove prescient: Turo's competitor Getaround went public via a SPAC merger in December 2022 and immediately lost most of its value (see S3.2), validating Turo's decision to wait.

**Why the private valuation stayed at ~$1.24 billion.** Despite strong revenue growth and profitability, Turo's private-market valuation remained anchored at approximately $1.24 billion — the post-money valuation set by IAC's $250 million Series E investment in July 2019 (extended by $30 million in February 2020). Since Turo did not raise any new funding rounds between 2020 and 2024, the 2019–2020 IAC round remained the most recent priced valuation mark, even as the company's financial performance improved dramatically ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)). Secondary-market estimates — where private shares are traded between accredited investors — would later suggest higher valuations (approximately $1.7 billion by September 2024, per [Forge Global](https://forgeglobal.com/turo_ipo/)), but these were estimates, not priced rounds. The company's decision to remain private, while its public competitor Getaround's valuation collapsed, would become one of the defining strategic contrasts of the P2P car rental industry.

### 2023–2024 — Growth deceleration, profit decline, rising private valuation, sustained S-1 filings

After two years of explosive post-pandemic growth, Turo's expansion slowed markedly in 2023 and 2024 — yet its private-market valuation climbed. This paradox lies at the heart of understanding why Turo's trajectory diverged so sharply from Getaround's during the same period.

**Revenue growth decelerates, profits shrink.** In 2023, Turo's revenue grew 18% to $879.7 million, down sharply from the 59% growth ($746.6 million) it had posted in 2022 and the 213% surge ($469 million) of 2021 ([TechCrunch, March 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/)). The deceleration reflected a return to a more stable competitive environment: traditional rental companies like Hertz, Avis, and Enterprise had rebuilt the vehicle fleets they sold off during the pandemic, eliminating the supply shortage that had driven renters to Turo's platform in 2021–2022 ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)). By 2024, revenue growth had slowed further to 9%, reaching approximately $958 million ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/); [Turo blog, March 2025](https://turo.com/blog/news/celebrating-13-years-of-drive/)).

Profitability also contracted. Net income fell from $154.7 million in 2022 to approximately $14.7 million in 2023 — a drop of roughly 90% ([TechCrunch, March 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/); [TradingCalendar, September 2024](https://www.tradingcalendar.com/post/turo-ipo-a-game-changer-in-the-car-sharing-industry)). Operating profit similarly declined to $13.7 million in 2023, down from $46.6 million in 2021. Gross margins — the percentage of revenue the company keeps after direct costs — compressed from 54.3% in 2022 to 51.4% in 2023, and then to approximately 46% in 2024, as customer acquisition costs and operational expenses rose ([TechCrunch, March 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/); [Sacra research, February 2025](https://sacra.com/research/turo-at-958m-revenue/)). Despite the decline, Turo remained profitable — a rarity among venture-backed tech companies preparing to go public. In the first half of 2024, the company posted net income of $9.92 million, swinging from a $22.92 million loss in the same period of 2023 ([MarketWatch, August 2024](https://www.marketwatch.com/story/car-sharing-marketplace-turo-swings-to-a-profit-in-a-boost-to-potential-ipo-526a8dc4)). Full-year 2024 net profit was approximately $26 million ([Sacra research, February 2025](https://sacra.com/research/turo-at-958m-revenue/)).

**A rising private valuation — with three different figures.** Despite slowing growth and shrinking profits, Turo's private-market valuation increased during this period. However, the exact figure depends on which source one consults (for how private-company valuations work, see Section 1.2). Three distinct estimates emerged:

- **~$1.24 billion** — This is the last *priced* funding round, the $250 million Series E from IAC in July 2019 (with a $30 million extension in February 2020), which set a post-money valuation of just over $1 billion. According to the research firm Sacra, "No new funding round or valuation mark has been announced since the 2020 round" ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)). This figure represents the last formal, company-acknowledged valuation.

- **~$1.7 billion** — In September 2024, Forge Global — a marketplace where private-company shares are bought and sold — recorded a secondary-market transaction valuing Turo at $1.7 billion. Forge labeled this a "Series E-2" valuation, with $59.92 million in shares traded at $13.35 per share ([Forge Global](https://forgeglobal.com/turo_ipo/)). Secondary markets allow early investors and employees to sell shares before an IPO, and the prices discovered in these transactions can exceed the last formal funding round.

- **~$2.7 billion** — Secondary-market estimates from The Information, TickerNerd, and TradingCalendar placed Turo's valuation at approximately $2.7 billion, based on private share transactions ([TickerNerd, March 2025](https://tickernerd.com/resources/ipo/turo-ipo/); [TradingCalendar, September 2024](https://www.tradingcalendar.com/post/turo-ipo-a-game-changer-in-the-car-sharing-industry)). The Information described Turo as "the rare startup with a rising valuation" in a December 2024 newsletter ([The Information, December 2024](https://www.theinformation.com/topics/plaid?page=4)).

The spread among these figures — from $1.24 billion to $2.7 billion — reflects different methodologies and different moments in time. The $1.24 billion is the last company-acknowledged valuation from a primary funding round. The $1.7 billion and $2.7 billion figures come from secondary-market transactions, where prices are set by supply and demand among private investors and can fluctuate based on sentiment, recent financial performance, and expectations about a future IPO. None of these figures is definitive; they represent estimates from different sources using different data.

**Sustained S-1 filings signal patience, not abandonment.** Turo first filed its S-1 registration statement on January 10, 2022 ([SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000119312522005696/d145731ds1.htm)). Rather than completing the offering, the company continued to file updated S-1/A amendments throughout 2022, 2023, and 2024, each time refreshing its financial disclosures. Amendment No. 5 was filed on June 9, 2023; Amendment No. 9 in March 2024; and Amendment No. 11 on August 8, 2024, which included the company's Q2 2024 financial results ([SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000162828024036158/turoinc-sx1a11.htm); [Forge Global, September 2024](https://forgeglobal.com/insights/turo-upcoming-ipo-news/)). By 2024, Turo had filed at least 11 amendments.

The company's decision to keep updating its filing rather than proceed to an IPO reflected several considerations. First, tech valuations in 2022–2024 remained depressed compared to the peak of 2021, meaning Turo could raise less money at a lower valuation — or, equivalently, would have to sell more shares to raise the same amount, diluting existing shareholders. Second, Turo's own growth deceleration made it a less attractive IPO candidate than it had been in 2022, when revenue was growing 59% annually. Third, the fate of its chief competitor served as a warning: Getaround's December 2022 SPAC debut and immediate 65% stock crash (detailed in Section 3.2) illustrated the risks of going public in a depressed market ([TechCrunch, March 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/)). Turo, by contrast, was self-funding from its operations and could afford to wait for more favorable market conditions.

**Workforce and platform scale.** Turo's employee count grew during this period, reflecting the company's expanding operations. Wikipedia reports 931 employees in 2023 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company))), and Turo's own blog stated the company had "nearly 1,000 employees" at the end of 2024 ([Turo blog, March 2025](https://turo.com/blog/news/celebrating-13-years-of-drive/)). The company did not conduct layoffs during 2023–2024 — a stark contrast to Getaround, which cut 10% of its workforce in February 2023 and another 30% in February 2024. IAC remained Turo's largest shareholder, holding approximately 31% of the company ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company))), though TechCrunch reported IAC held 39.2 million shares ([TechCrunch, March 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/)). Total funding raised stood at approximately $557.9 million ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/TURO.PVT/)), though Sacra and Forge Global cite $502.6 million — the discrepancy likely stems from whether certain funding tranches are counted.

By 2024, Turo's platform hosted approximately 360,000 active vehicle listings from 140,000+ active hosts, serving 3.5 million active guests across five countries (the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, France, and Australia) ([Turo blog, March 2025](https://turo.com/blog/news/celebrating-13-years-of-drive/); [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)). Electric vehicles represented 9% of listings, up from 8% in 2023 ([TechCrunch, March 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/)).

**Why Turo's valuation did not collapse.** The fundamental structural reason is that Turo remained a private company throughout this period — a status whose valuation mechanics are explained in Section 1.2. Turo's secondary-market valuation rose because investors were willing to pay more for its shares in private transactions, reflecting confidence in its profitability, its dominant market position, and the possibility of a future IPO. Getaround, by contrast, was subject to daily market pricing after its SPAC merger (Section 3.2), which immediately and continuously reflected its deteriorating financial condition, repeated losses, and inability to achieve profitability. Turo's patience as a private company — sustained by consistent (if declining) profitability and a self-funding business model — preserved its valuation in a way that Getaround's public-market exposure did not.

### 2025 — IPO withdrawal, New Year's Day attacks, layoffs, and business expansion

Turo's 2025 was a year of stark contrasts: reputational crisis and workforce reduction on one hand, and continued business expansion on the other. The company ended the year still private, still valued in the billions, and still growing — but the path it had hoped to take onto the public markets was definitively closed.

**January 1, 2025: Two deadly incidents.** On New Year's Day, two separate attacks in different cities involved vehicles rented through Turo's platform. In New Orleans, a man drove a rented Ford F-150 Lightning pickup truck into a crowd on Bourbon Street, killing 15 people (including himself) and injuring 35 others. The truck had been rented through Turo from a host in Houston, Texas ([ABC7 News](https://abc7news.com/post/new-orleans-terrorist-attack-car-driven-crowd-bourbon-street-rented-turo-app-houston-texas-sources-confirm/15737640/)). Hours later, in Las Vegas, a Tesla Cybertruck exploded outside the Trump International Hotel, killing the driver and injuring seven others. The explosion was later determined to be intentional ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/tesla-cybertruck-appears-burst-flames-trump-hotel-las-vegas-rcna185932); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Las_Vegas_Cybertruck_explosion)).

Turo CEO Andre Haddad issued a public statement expressing "outrage and heartbreak" over both incidents and appeared on CNBC's *Squawk on the Street* to discuss the company's cooperation with law enforcement ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/video/2025/01/03/turo-ceo-shares-details-of-investigations-into-attack-in-new-orleans-vegas-cybertruck-explosion.html); [ABC7 News](https://abc7news.com/post/new-orleans-las-vegas-attacks-andre-haddad-ceo-san-francisco-based-turo-releases-statement/15747966/)). Turo stated that neither renter had a criminal background that would have flagged them as a security threat during the company's screening process. Even so, the incidents represented a worst-case scenario for a brand on the cusp of a public market debut, drawing intense media scrutiny to the platform's trust-and-safety practices.

**IPO withdrawal (February 13, 2025).** On February 13, 2025, Turo filed a Form RW with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to withdraw its registration statement for an initial public offering, formally ending a three-year effort to go public. The company had first filed its S-1 prospectus in January 2022 but had repeatedly delayed the offering as market conditions shifted and growth decelerated. In the withdrawal filing, Turo stated only that it "does not wish to conduct a public offering of securities at this time" ([SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000162828025005472/turo-formrw.htm); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/)).

CEO Andre Haddad said the board "decided now is not the right time for Turo to pursue a public offering," and added that the company planned to "take advantage of our company's plans to remain private in order to make important investments in the business that will build long term value for all stakeholders" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/)). The withdrawal came just one day after Getaround — Turo's chief competitor — announced it was shutting down its U.S. operations entirely, underscoring the divergent fates of the two P2P car rental pioneers.

Several factors likely contributed to the withdrawal. Revenue growth had decelerated sharply: from 213% in 2021 to 59% in 2022 to 18% in 2023, with 2024 revenue of approximately $958 million representing only 9% year-over-year growth ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/)). Net income had fallen from $154.7 million in 2022 to approximately $14.7 million in 2023. The New Year's Day attacks had damaged the company's public image at a particularly sensitive moment. And the broader IPO market for venture-backed tech companies remained weak, with few new listings in 2024 or early 2025 ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/shrinking-ipo-pipeline-unicorn-turo-hits-pause/)).

**April 2025 layoffs.** In April 2025, Turo laid off approximately 150 employees — roughly 15% of its workforce, reducing headcount from about 1,000 to approximately 850. CEO Andre Haddad attributed the cuts to "ongoing economic uncertainty" and said the move was intended to "strengthen our position for long-term growth." He added that Turo "remains in a strong financial position" ([SFGate](https://www.sfgate.com/tech/article/turo-lays-off-150-workers-economic-uncertainty-20281905.php)). The layoffs marked a significant shift for a company that had grown its workforce steadily through 2023 and 2024 as revenue expanded. Unlike Getaround's layoffs — which were explicitly framed as emergency measures to "extend the cash runway" — Turo's were described as a strategic restructuring rather than a survival move. The company confirmed it remained profitable through the first nine months of 2024, with net income of $9.92 million in the first half of that year ([SFGate](https://www.sfgate.com/tech/article/turo-lays-off-150-workers-economic-uncertainty-20281905.php)).

**Business expansion: Kyte acquisition, Uber Rent, and Priceline.** Despite the negative headlines, Turo continued to expand its business through partnerships and acquisitions in 2025.

In July 2025, Turo acquired select assets from Kyte, a San Francisco-based on-demand car rental platform that had operated a fleet of professionally managed vehicles in cities across the United States. Kyte had ceased operations and sold its customer list to Turo before shutting down entirely. The acquisition allowed Turo to fold Kyte's customers — particularly those accustomed to operator-managed, on-demand rentals — into its platform, extending Turo's reach beyond its core peer-to-peer model into a segment closer to traditional rental car services. Specific financial terms of the deal were not disclosed ([PhocusWire](https://www.phocuswire.com/turo-acquires-select-assets-kyte); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/15/kyte-which-billed-itself-as-the-best-competitor-to-hertz-shuts-down/); [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)).

In May 2025, Uber launched an integration with Turo across its Uber Rent platform, making Turo's inventory of over 1,600 vehicle makes and models available to Uber customers in five countries: the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and France. The partnership, first announced in September 2024, allowed Uber users to book Turo vehicles directly within the Uber app, giving Turo access to Uber's massive user base ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/turo-and-uber-bring-peer-to-peer-car-sharing-to-uber-rent-302456313.html); [Turo blog](https://turo.com/blog/news/uber-and-turo-partner-to-share-vehicles-on-uber-rent/)).

Also in 2025, Priceline integrated Turo inventory into its booking flow as part of the travel platform's Spring 2025 product release. The integration allowed Priceline customers to book Turo vehicles directly within the Priceline app, matching travelers with specific car models in specific neighborhoods across the United States ([Priceline press release](https://press.priceline.com/spring2025release/); [PhocusWire](https://www.phocuswire.com/priceline-ai-neighborhood-turo)).

**John Wayne Airport license.** In May 2025, Orange County supervisors approved a five-year licensing agreement allowing Turo to operate at John Wayne Airport (SNA) in Santa Ana, California, through July 2030. The agreement codified a pilot program that had begun in August 2023. Under the terms, the county receives 10% of gross receipts from all Turo transactions at the airport. Since the pilot began, the county had already earned $1.2 million, and officials projected approximately $3.6 million in total revenue over the five-year term. Turo spokeswoman Catherine Mejia noted that the platform now operates at approximately 150 airports nationwide ([Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/socal/daily-pilot/news/story/2025-05-23/county-enters-5-year-contract-with-vehicle-sharing-app-turo-at-john-wayne-airport)).

**Revenue trajectory and valuation.** Turo's revenue reached approximately $958 million in 2024, up 9% from $879.7 million in 2023. The company stated it was on track to hit $1 billion in annual revenue in 2025 — a company-stated projection, not actual reported revenue ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)). By October 2025, Forbes reported that Turo had "recently crossed $1 billion in revenue on roughly $3 billion in gross booking value," with 350,000+ active vehicle listings and 3.5 million active guests worldwide ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jefffromm/2025/10/08/turos-1-billion-playbook-how-a-marketplace-scales/)). The company remained profitable, though at lower margins than its 2022 peak.

Turo's private valuation remained in the billions after the IPO withdrawal. As detailed in Section 2.3, estimates ranged from approximately $1.24 billion (the last priced funding round) to $1.7 billion–$2.7 billion (secondary-market estimates), reflecting different methodologies rather than a single definitive number. Turo's valuation did not collapse like Getaround's because, as a private company, its valuation only moves when new funding rounds or secondary transactions price it — and the company continued to grow revenue and maintain profitability throughout 2025 ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/); [Forge Global](https://forgeglobal.com/turo_ipo/); [TickerNerd](https://tickernerd.com/resources/ipo/turo-ipo/)).

Turo's total funding raised stood at $557.9 million as of 2025, with IAC remaining the largest shareholder at approximately 31–39% ownership. The company operated in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, France, and Australia, with approximately 850 employees after the April layoffs — a workforce still far larger than Getaround's remaining headcount as the rival wound down its U.S. operations.

## Getaround — Economic Events and Valuation Collapse, 2020–2025

### 2020 — COVID crisis: repeated layoffs, cash crunch, rescue Series E

Getaround entered 2020 already stretched. The company had spent heavily in 2019 to expand into Europe through acquisitions (see S1.1) and was reportedly raising new capital at a [post-money valuation](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/carsharing-startup-getaround-reportedly-raising-200m-at-1-7b-valuation/) of $1.7 billion, according to Crunchbase. That figure, reported in September 2019, would prove to be the peak of Getaround's valuation before a five-year collapse.

**Pre-pandemic workforce and January layoffs.** Getaround's pre-2020 headcount was approximately 550 employees, according to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround), though a January 2020 report by The Information — cited in [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround) and confirmed by [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/14/getaround-raises-a-140-million-series-e-amid-rebound-in-short-distance-travel/) — stated that the company planned to lay off roughly 150 staff, or about 25% of its workforce, which implies a total of approximately 600. The discrepancy likely reflects different measurement dates or whether part-time and contract workers were included. The January cuts reduced field operations and numerous global teams.

**March 2020: demand collapse, buyer search, second layoffs.** When the COVID-19 pandemic hit the United States in March 2020, Getaround's business contracted almost overnight. CEO Sam Zaid told TechCrunch that [bookings dropped 75%](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/14/getaround-raises-a-140-million-series-e-amid-rebound-in-short-distance-travel/) month-over-month. The company ran short of cash — meaning it did not have enough money on hand to cover its near-term expenses — and [Bloomberg reported on March 20, 2020](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-03-20/softbank-backed-getaround-looks-for-buyer-as-demand-evaporates) that Getaround was actively seeking a buyer. According to the [Business Times](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/startups-tech/startups/softbank-backed-getaround-looks-buyer-demand-evaporates), people familiar with the matter said the company might consider bankruptcy protection if it could not find a buyer or an infusion of cash. No buyer materialized.

A second round of layoffs followed in late March. [The Information reported](https://www.theinformation.com/briefings/f23eb5) that Getaround laid off approximately 100 U.S.-based employees. The [San Francisco Business Times](https://www.bizjournals.com/sanfrancisco/news/2020/03/27/getaround-issues-another-layoff-round-as-it-seeks.html) reported this as a further 25% cut, bringing the company down to approximately 300 workers from roughly 400. Combined with the January cuts, Getaround's workforce had shrunk by roughly half in under three months — from approximately 550–600 employees to around 300.

**Revenue trajectory: decline, then rebound.** Getaround's revenue followed a U-shaped curve in 2020. After the initial 75% demand drop in March, the company saw a recovery beginning in May, as consumers avoided public transportation and air travel but were willing to drive for short-distance trips. Getaround focused on marketing its contactless car-access technology as a safer alternative. By July 1, 2020, the company had [rehired all furloughed employees](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/14/getaround-raises-a-140-million-series-e-amid-rebound-in-short-distance-travel/) (workers who had been temporarily suspended from work without pay). By October, CEO Sam Zaid said that worldwide revenue had more than doubled from its pre-COVID baseline, and gross margins — the percentage of revenue left after subtracting the direct costs of providing the service — had continued to improve. The company reported more than 6 million users globally and over 1 billion miles driven on its platform.

**The October 2020 Series E.** On October 14, 2020, Getaround announced it had raised $140 million in a [Series E funding round](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/getaround-raises-140-million-series-e-financing-301151849.html). The round was led by PeopleFund, a venture capital firm, with participation from Reinvent Capital (the investment arm of LinkedIn co-founder Reid Hoffman and Zynga co-founder Mark Pincus), as well as existing investors including SoftBank Vision Fund, Menlo Ventures, and others. The company's [official press release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/getaround-raises-140-million-series-e-financing-301151849.html) stated the round brought Getaround's total amount raised to nearly $600 million. The company did not disclose its valuation in this round, but [Forge Global](https://forgeglobal.com/getaround-4_ipo/), a secondary-market data provider, listed a "Series E-1 Valuation" of approximately $990.8 million for October 2020 — a significant discount from the $1.7 billion reported in 2019. This suggests that despite the revenue rebound, investors valued Getaround lower than they had before the pandemic, reflecting the severe damage the crisis had inflicted on the business and the uncertainty about its long-term trajectory.

**Total funding discrepancy.** Most sources, including [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/14/getaround-raises-a-140-million-series-e-amid-rebound-in-short-distance-travel/), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html), and the company's own press release, report total funding of approximately $600 million. However, [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/), a private-market data aggregator, reports approximately $750 million. The discrepancy likely stems from whether the August 2018 $300 million SoftBank round — Getaround's [Series D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround) — is counted separately or whether additional smaller funding sources are included. The $600 million figure is the one most consistently cited by primary sources.

**Pre-2020 acquisitions and their financial impact.** Getaround's 2019 European acquisitions (see S1.1) had expanded its footprint to seven countries and given it a combined user base of over 5 million. However, these purchases consumed significant capital — roughly $312 million in total — just months before the pandemic struck. The Drivy acquisition, in particular, was financed largely from the $300 million Series D round that SoftBank had provided in August 2018. This meant that Getaround entered the pandemic with a depleted cash position and an expanded cost structure (more employees, more offices, more technology to maintain) but without the revenue scale to support it. When COVID-19 hit, the company's European operations could not offset the collapse in U.S. demand quickly enough to prevent a cash crisis.

**The broader context.** Getaround's 2020 crisis illustrates a pattern common among venture-backed startups: rapid expansion fueled by investor capital, followed by a sudden external shock that exposes the gap between revenue and costs. Unlike Turo, which also laid off 30% of its workforce in April 2020 but had a larger revenue base and a more capital-efficient business model, Getaround's smaller scale and heavier pre-pandemic spending left it with less [runway](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/r/runway.asp). The company's survival depended on the October 2020 Series E, which provided enough capital to stabilize operations but at a valuation that was likely less than half of its 2019 peak. The rescue round bought time, but it did not solve the fundamental problem: Getaround's revenue was too small and its losses too large to achieve [profitability](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/profitability.asp) in the near term. This challenge would define the company's trajectory through its 2022 SPAC merger and eventual collapse.

### 2021–2022 — DC AG settlement, SPAC IPO at $1.2B, and immediate 65% crash

**DC Attorney General settlement (July 2021).** In July 2021, the Attorney General for the District of Columbia, Karl A. Racine, announced a settlement with Getaround requiring the company to pay **$950,000** to the District, plus restitution to car owners who experienced theft or damage to their vehicles while listed on the platform. The settlement resolved allegations that Getaround violated the District's consumer protection laws by misrepresenting the safety and security of its platform, using fictitious owner profiles for fleet cars the company itself owned, misleading consumers about insurance coverage, operating without a required license, and failing to collect and pay District sales taxes. As part of the settlement, Getaround agreed to disclose the limitations of its security features, communicate all insurance terms and conditions, state accurate customer service hours, and maintain proper operating licenses ([DC Office of the Attorney General, July 23, 2021](https://oag.dc.gov/release/ag-racine-announces-car-sharing-company-getaround); [Reuters, July 23, 2021](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/car-sharing-company-getaround-pay-950000-dc-settlement-2021-07-23/)).

**SPAC market context.** Getaround chose to go public via a SPAC (defined in S1.2). By late 2022, the SPAC market had collapsed — the CNBC SPAC Post Deal Index had fallen over 60% in the year before Getaround's debut (see S1.2) — reflecting a broad loss of investor appetite for these deals.

**The SPAC merger announcement (May 2022).** On May 11, 2022, Getaround announced a merger with **InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp.** (NYSE: IPVA), a SPAC, to list on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker **GETR**. The deal valued the combined company at a pro forma equity value of approximately **$1.2 billion**, assuming no redemptions from InterPrivate's $259 million trust account. The transaction was expected to generate between $225 million and $434 million in gross proceeds for Getaround, including a convertible note commitment of up to $175 million from affiliates of Mudrick Capital Management, a distressed-debt investment firm. In its investor presentation, Getaround reported 2021 Gross Booking Value (GBV — the total value of all bookings on the platform) of $167 million, Net Marketplace Revenue of $81 million, and a 52% Trip Contribution Margin. The company said proceeds would fund expansion into new markets and product development ([SEC 8-K Exhibit 99.1, May 11, 2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000119312522147020/d289503dex991.htm); [Orrick, December 9, 2022](https://www.orrick.com/en/News/2022/05/Orrick-Advises-Getaround-on-Billion-SPAC-Merger)).

**The $1.2B valuation was already a steep discount.** The $1.2 billion SPAC valuation represented a significant markdown from the $1.7 billion private-market valuation Getaround had been assigned in 2019, before the pandemic. This discount reflected both the deterioration of Getaround's financial position during 2020 and the broader collapse of the SPAC market. By the time the deal was announced, SPACs had fallen out of favor with investors, and the window for such deals was effectively closing.

**Convertible notes and pre-SPAC debt.** Before the SPAC merger closed, Getaround issued **$35.4 million in convertible notes** across a series of closings in June, September, and November 2022. These notes were separate from the $175 million Mudrick Capital convertible note commitment that was part of the SPAC transaction itself. Convertible notes are debt instruments that can be converted into company shares at a later date, typically at a discount to the future stock price — a structure that can dilute existing shareholders when conversion occurs. The issuance of these notes signaled that Getaround needed capital before the SPAC proceeds were available, adding to the company's debt burden ([SEC 424B3 prospectus, November 23, 2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000119312522292093/d416454d424b3.htm)).

**The SPAC closes — and the stock crashes 65% (December 8–9, 2022).** The business combination closed on December 8, 2022, and Getaround's common stock and warrants began trading on the NYSE on December 9, 2022, under the tickers GETR and GETR-WS. On its first day of trading, shares dropped as much as **65%**, falling to approximately $3 per share. The company claimed approximately $228 million in gross proceeds from the transaction, including amounts retained in InterPrivate's trust account and the conversion of approximately $37.5 million in convertible notes ([BusinessWire, December 9, 2022](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20221209005329/en/Getaround-Announces-Closing-of-Business-Combination-with-InterPrivate-II-Acquisition-Corp); [TechCrunch, December 9, 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/12/09/getaround-braves-chilly-public-markets-with-spac-combination/)).

**Why the crash happened — both company-specific and market-wide factors.** The 65% first-day drop reflected a combination of forces. First, the broader SPAC market had collapsed (see S1.2 for CNBC SPAC Post Deal Index data), and several high-profile SPAC deals had been aborted in the weeks before Getaround's debut ([CNBC, December 9, 2022](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html)). Second, Getaround's own financials were weak. The company reported 2022 revenue of approximately **$62.3 million** and a net loss of approximately **$46.8 million** for the full year. Through the first nine months of 2022, Getaround's operating cash burn was $63.2 million — higher than the $53.3 million burned during the same period in 2021. Revenue had actually declined when comparing the first three quarters of 2022 to the prior year, and operating expenses had ticked higher. TechCrunch noted that "negative growth and rising losses are not usually the substance we see when a company goes public" and that Getaround's enterprise value (equity value minus cash) was effectively close to zero after the crash ([TechCrunch, December 9, 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/12/09/getaround-braves-chilly-public-markets-with-spac-combination/)). The company had entered the SPAC with just $27.2 million in cash and equivalents at the end of Q3 2022, making the deal a necessity rather than a choice.

**The Drivy acquisition's role in Getaround's strategy.** Getaround's 2019 acquisition of Drivy (detailed in S1.1 and S3.1) was central to its strategy of becoming a global carsharing leader. The deal gave Getaround a foothold in multiple European markets and brought Drivy's founder, Paulin Dementhon, on as CEO of European operations. However, the acquisition also consumed significant capital just before the pandemic, and the European operations did not generate sufficient returns to justify the purchase price. The Drivy acquisition exemplified Getaround's pre-pandemic spending pattern — raising large sums and deploying them aggressively on expansion and acquisitions — which left the company with a cost structure too large for its revenue base when the pandemic hit. The European business would later become the only part of Getaround to survive the US collapse, though it was ultimately sold for just €31.5 million in 2026 ([TechCrunch, April 24, 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/24/getaround-acquires-european-car-rental-platform-drivy-for-300-million/); [PR Newswire, April 24, 2019](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/getaround-becomes-global-carsharing-leader-with-300-million-acquisition-of-european-platform-drivy-300835461.html)).

**The structural consequence of going public via SPAC.** The SPAC merger gave Getaround access to public capital — approximately $228 million in gross proceeds — but it also exposed the company's weaknesses to daily market pricing (see S1.2 for private vs. public valuation mechanics). Getaround's deteriorating financials, declining revenue growth, and persistent losses were immediately priced in by public investors, producing a valuation that was a fraction of the $1.2 billion "paper valuation" announced at the SPAC deal. The crash also meant that the company's ability to raise additional capital through future stock offerings was severely impaired, as its shares were now worth pennies rather than the $10+ range at which SPAC shares typically begin trading. This set in motion a vicious cycle: declining stock price → reduced ability to raise capital → cost-cutting and layoffs → further decline — a cycle that would accelerate through 2023 and 2024.

### 2023 — Post-SPAC struggle: delisting warning, 10% layoffs, HyreCar acquisition, SF headquarters exit

Getaround's first full year as a public company was defined by a cascade of financial distress signals, cost-cutting measures, and a desperate attempt to pivot toward a niche market — all while its stock price continued to collapse.

**NYSE delisting warnings.** In February 2023, the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) issued a [continued-listing standard notice](https://techcrunch.com/2023/02/01/car-sharing-platform-getaround-gets-delisting-warning-from-nyse/) to Getaround because its share price had closed below $1.00 for 30 consecutive trading days. At the time, Getaround's stock was trading at approximately $0.64 — a stark fall from the roughly $10 per share at which it had debuted just two months earlier in December 2022. The NYSE gave the company six months to bring its stock price back above the $1 threshold or face removal from the exchange.

The stock-price warning was only the beginning. Getaround also received [separate delisting notices](https://techcrunch.com/2023/05/11/getaround-aims-to-scale-car-sharing-platform-with-hyrecar-acquisition/) from the NYSE for two additional deficiencies: its global market capitalization (total share price × shares outstanding) had fallen below $50 million over a 30-day period, and it had failed to file its earnings reports with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) on time. The late filings were attributed to the complexity of post-SPAC accounting — the process of reconciling the financial statements of the merged public company with those of the former private entity, which required extensive audit work.

**10% workforce reduction.** On February 2, 2023 — one day after the delisting warning — Getaround announced a [restructuring plan](https://techcrunch.com/2023/02/02/car-sharing-spac-getaround-lays-off-10-of-staff/) that included laying off approximately 10% of its workforce. Based on a LinkedIn headcount of roughly 421 employees at the time, the cuts affected an estimated 42 people, all in North American teams across all departments. CEO Sam Zaid described the restructuring as a response to "an uncertain near-term macroeconomic outlook, which has hit technology companies particularly hard," and said the goal was to achieve "sustainable profitability and long-term growth." The company projected annualized cost savings of $25–30 million from the layoffs, reduced contract workforce, and cuts to outside professional services. The layoffs were explicitly framed as a way to extend the company's cash runway — a term that signals financial distress rather than strategic optimization.

**HyreCar acquisition.** In May 2023, Getaround acquired the assets of [HyreCar](https://techcrunch.com/2023/05/11/getaround-aims-to-scale-car-sharing-platform-with-hyrecar-acquisition/), a gig-worker car-sharing platform that had filed for bankruptcy in February 2023, for $9.45 million in cash. HyreCar's business model focused on providing vehicles to gig-economy drivers (such as Uber and Lyft drivers) who did not own cars — a niche market distinct from Getaround's core peer-to-peer rental business. Getaround said the acquisition would bring access to tens of thousands of gig drivers and up to $75 million in run-rate annualized gross booking value. The company claimed the deal would accelerate its path to profitability. The stock briefly surged 135% in after-hours trading on the news, reaching $0.80 — still far below the $1 minimum needed to satisfy NYSE listing requirements. The acquisition was funded from cash on hand; Getaround had closed 2022 with $64.3 million in cash, according to [preliminary results](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000119312523086964/d490001dex991.htm) filed in March 2023.

**San Francisco headquarters exit.** In a move that underscored the company's cost-cutting imperative, Getaround [vacated its San Francisco headquarters](https://www.sfchronicle.com/realestate/article/getaround-leaving-s-f-headquarters-18623889.php) at 55 Green St. in late 2023. The company had signed a 10-year lease for the roughly 50,000-square-foot, five-story waterfront building that was set to run through 2029, but stopped paying rent in October 2023 and moved to terminate the agreement. In a December 2023 SEC filing, Getaround stated that because it had adopted a "remote first" work model during the COVID-19 pandemic, it "does not rely on the headquarters facility for any material part of its operations." The company ultimately paid $3.6 million as part of a negotiated settlement with its landlord. A spokesperson confirmed Getaround would not seek additional office space for the "foreseeable future," though it maintained a small office in Oakland and offices in Paris and Oslo. The exit from 55 Green St. was emblematic of a broader trend of San Francisco tech companies shedding office space as remote work became permanent.

**Financial results: revenue growth amid persistent losses.** Getaround's Q3 2023 results, [reported on December 14, 2023](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-reports-strong-3q-2023-results-with-42-year-over-year-growth-in-revenue), showed total revenue of $23.8 million — a 42% year-over-year increase — but a net loss of $27.3 million and an adjusted EBITDA loss of $11.3 million. While still negative, the adjusted EBITDA loss had narrowed 43% compared to the same quarter in 2022. For the full year 2023, Getaround reported [revenue of $72.7 million](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240328628539/en/Getaround-Reports-Full-Year-2023-Results) (up 22% year-over-year) and a net loss of $113.9 million. The company's cash position had deteriorated from $64.3 million at the end of 2022 to $22.2 million at the end of Q3 2023, reflecting continued cash burn.

**Employee headcount.** As of December 31, 2022, Getaround had [283 full-time employees](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm), the vast majority located in the United States, France, and Norway. By December 31, 2023, the company reported approximately 290 full-time employees — a modest increase that likely reflected the addition of HyreCar staff, partially offset by the February 2023 layoffs. The 10% workforce reduction in February 2023 affected roughly 42 employees, but the company's total headcount remained relatively stable through the year due to the HyreCar acquisition and some rehiring.

**Warrant delisting.** In November 2023, the NYSE [commenced delisting proceedings](https://ir.theice.com/press/news-details/2023/NYSE-to-Commence-Delisting-Proceedings-with-Respect-to-the-Warrants-of-Getaround-Inc.-GETR-WS/default.aspx) against Getaround's warrants (ticker: GETR-WS) — financial instruments that give holders the right to purchase common stock at a fixed price of $11.50 per share. The NYSE determined the warrants were no longer suitable for listing due to "abnormally low" price levels, and trading in the warrants was suspended immediately. The common stock (GETR) continued trading on the NYSE, but the warrant delisting was another signal that the company's equity had lost nearly all of its value — the warrants, which had been issued as part of the SPAC merger, were now so far out of the money (the stock was trading below $1, far below the $11.50 exercise price) that they were effectively worthless.

**Stock price and market cap trajectory.** Getaround's stock entered 2023 at approximately $0.64 and continued to decline throughout the year. By the end of 2023, the company's market capitalization had fallen to roughly $20 million, according to [Macrotrends data](https://www.macrotrends.net/stocks/charts/GETR/getaround/market-cap) — a decline of over 99% from the $1.2 billion implied equity valuation at the time of the SPAC merger announcement in May 2022. The stock had lost approximately 96% of its value from its first day of trading in December 2022. Despite the brief spike following the HyreCar acquisition announcement, the underlying trend was unmistakably downward, driven by persistent losses, shrinking cash reserves, and the growing likelihood of a full delisting from the NYSE.

In summary, 2023 was a year of managed decline for Getaround. The company cut costs, shed real estate, acquired a distressed competitor, and narrowed its losses — but none of these measures were sufficient to reverse the fundamental dynamics that had plagued it since going public: revenue too small to cover expenses, cash burning faster than it could be replenished, and a stock price in free fall that threatened the company's very listing status.

### 2024 — Freefall: $20M rescue round, 30% layoffs, NYSE delisting, market cap collapse

2024 was the year Getaround's post-SPAC decline became irreversible. What had begun as a stock-price slide in late 2022 accelerated through a cascade of emergency financing, mass layoffs, regulatory delisting, and a final collapse of the company's market value to near zero. Each event fed the next: the stock crash eroded confidence, which made fundraising desperate, which forced layoffs, which shrunk revenue, which triggered delisting, which destroyed the remaining share value.

**January 2024: A $20 million lifeline from Mudrick Capital**

In January 2024, Getaround secured a debt facility — a loan rather than a sale of new shares — with [Mudrick Capital Management](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-secures-$20-million-in-financing-following-strong-q3-2023-results-2024-01) for up to $20 million in additional capital, with an initial $5 million drawn immediately. The financing was intended to fund the company's 2024 operating plan. Jason Mudrick, founder of Mudrick Capital, was appointed Chairperson of the Board, and Ahmed Fattouh, CEO of Getaround's SPAC sponsor InterPrivate, stepped down from the board. Some data aggregators categorised this as an "Other Equity" round, but the company's own press release described it as a [debt facility](https://coverager.com/getaround-raises-20-million/), meaning Getaround borrowed the money rather than selling ownership stakes. The valuation at which this financing occurred was not publicly disclosed, but the terms — a distressed company accepting a loan from a hedge fund known for investing in troubled businesses — signalled that traditional equity investors were no longer willing to fund Getaround at any meaningful valuation. The stock briefly surged 89% on the news, reflecting how starved the company was for any positive signal ([InvestorPlace](https://investorplace.com/2024/01/why-is-getaround-getr-stock-up-89-today/)).

**February 2024: 30% of North American workforce laid off**

On February 7, 2024, Getaround announced a restructuring plan that included laying off approximately 30% of its North American workforce. The company said the cuts would save about [$7 million on an annualized basis](https://techcrunch.com/2024/02/08/car-sharing-company-getaround-cuts-one-third-of-u-s-workforce/) and incur up to $1 million in restructuring costs. Severance was up to 12 weeks, with benefits continuing through February 2024. The exact number of employees affected was not disclosed, but the company had reported [283 full-time employees](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm) as of December 31, 2022, before a 10% reduction in February 2023 and the HyreCar acquisition in May 2023. CEO Sam Zaid described the layoffs as necessary for "profitability and sustainable business growth" — language that underscored how far the company had fallen from its $1.2 billion SPAC valuation just fourteen months earlier ([SF Chronicle](https://www.sfchronicle.com/tech/article/getaround-layoffs-restructuring-18656885.php)).

**February–March 2024: Leadership overhaul**

The restructuring coincided with a dramatic leadership shake-up. On February 26, 2024, the Board appointed Eduardo Iniguez — formerly Getaround's SVP of Finance, Risk & Strategy, and before that CEO of HyreCar — as CEO, replacing founder Sam Zaid, who remained on the board ([SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024021669/getr-ex99_1.htm)). In March 2024, AJ Lee, previously General Manager of Gig, was promoted to Chief Operating Officer ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240311266076/en/Getaround-Announces-Promotion-of-AJ-Lee-as-COO)). The rapid turnover at the top — three CEO changes in just over two years (Sam Zaid → Eduardo Iniguez → AJ Lee) — signalled deep instability in the company's strategic direction.

**March–April 2024: New York State operations suspended**

On March 23, 2024, Getaround decided to suspend its consumer car-sharing operations in New York State effective April 1, 2024. The company cited the "extremely high cost of maintaining the insurance coverage required under the New York Peer-to-Peer (P2P) Carsharing Act" enacted in 2022, which requires car-sharing platforms to maintain insurance limits [fifty times greater](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-reports-full-year-2023-results-2024-03-28) than those required for traditional rental car companies. This suspension directly reduced revenue: Q3 2024 results later showed revenue of $22.4 million, down from $23.8 million year-over-year, "primarily due to suspended operations in New York State" ([StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/GETR/getaround-reports-third-quarter-2024-financial-wvedc64aodrd.html)).

**July 2024: NYSE delisting and transfer to OTC Markets**

On July 9, 2024, the New York Stock Exchange announced it would suspend trading of Getaround's common stock and commence delisting proceedings under Section 802.01B of the NYSE Listed Company Manual, because the company had fallen below the exchange's requirement to maintain an average global market capitalization of at least $15 million over 30 consecutive trading days ([NYSE Regulation](https://ir.theice.com/press/news-details/2024/NYSE-to-Commence-Delisting-Proceedings-Against-Getaround-Inc.-GETR/default.aspx)). At the time of delisting, Getaround's market capitalization was approximately [$12.19 million](https://www.investing.com/news/company-news/getaround-to-appeal-nyse-delisting-trades-on-otc-markets-93CH-3513784) — below the $15 million threshold. The stock began trading on the OTC Markets platform (under the ticker GETR) on July 10, 2024. For shareholders, this meant the stock became harder to buy and sell, and the company lost the prestige and visibility of an NYSE listing. Getaround stated it would appeal the delisting decision, but the appeal was unsuccessful.

**Market cap collapse: from ~$24 million to ~$20,000**

Getaround's market cap collapsed through 2024. Earlier in the year, before the delisting, the market cap had been estimated at roughly $24 million ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround)). By the time of the NYSE delisting in July, it had fallen to approximately $12.19 million ([Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/company-news/getaround-to-appeal-nyse-delisting-trades-on-otc-markets-93CH-3513784)). After transferring to OTC trading, the market cap collapsed further to approximately [$19,760](https://simplywall.st/stocks/us/transportation/otc-getr/getaround) — effectively worthless. To put this in perspective: the company had been valued at $1.2 billion at its SPAC merger in December 2022. In less than two years, over 99.9% of its market value had evaporated.

**Q3 2024 financials: the balance sheet deteriorates**

Getaround's third-quarter 2024 results, reported on November 14, 2024, revealed a company in deep financial distress ([StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/GETR/getaround-reports-third-quarter-2024-financial-wvedc64aodrd.html)):

| Metric | Q3 2024 | Q3 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $22.4M | $23.8M |
| Net loss | $15.5M | $27.3M |
| Adjusted EBITDA loss | $9.3M | $11.3M |
| Cash | $30.8M | — |
| Stockholders' equity | **-$33.5M** | +$3.1M |
| Accumulated deficit | **-$934.5M** | -$876.0M |

Stockholders' equity — the value of the company belonging to its shareholders after subtracting all liabilities — turned sharply negative, from a positive $3.1 million at the end of 2023 to negative $33.5 million by September 30, 2024. This meant the company's liabilities exceeded its assets. The accumulated deficit — the total losses the company had incurred since its founding — reached $934.5 million, meaning Getaround had burned through nearly a billion dollars more than it had ever earned in its thirteen-year history. While the net loss improved 43% year-over-year (from $27.3 million to $15.5 million), this was partly due to cost-cutting and the New York shutdown rather than genuine revenue growth.

**October 2024: AJ Lee appointed interim CEO**

On October 15, 2024, Eduardo Iniguez resigned as CEO for personal reasons, and the Board appointed AJ Lee — who had been COO since March 2024 — as Interim Chief Executive Officer ([TipRanks](https://www.tipranks.com/news/company-announcements/getaround-inc-appoints-aj-lee-as-interim-ceo)). The use of the word "interim" rather than "permanent" signalled that the company was in a transitional, unstable state. Lee had risen rapidly through the ranks — from General Manager of Gig to COO to interim CEO in the span of seven months — reflecting both his capabilities and the company's inability to retain senior leadership.

**The cascading collapse**

Getaround's 2024 freefall followed a clear causal chain. The SPAC merger in December 2022 had given the company a $1.2 billion paper valuation but also exposed its weaknesses to daily market pricing. When the stock crashed 65% on its first day of trading and continued declining, the company lost access to affordable capital. Revenue remained too small — $22.4 million in Q3 2024, a fraction of Turo's $958 million in 2024 — to cover operating costs. The $20 million debt facility from Mudrick Capital provided temporary relief but came with the cost of ceding board control to a distressed-debt investor. The 30% layoff saved $7 million annually but further reduced the company's ability to generate revenue. The New York State suspension eliminated a key market. The NYSE delisting removed the company's public-market credibility. And the transfer to OTC trading — where shares trade at fractions of a cent with minimal liquidity — effectively marked the end of Getaround as a meaningful public company. By the close of 2024, a company that had once been valued at $1.7 billion (in 2019) and $1.2 billion (at its SPAC merger) was worth approximately $20,000 — a decline of over 99.99%.

### 2025 — US operations shutdown, European survival, and path to dissolution

**The end of Getaround in the United States.** On February 11, 2025, Getaround announced it was ceasing all US operations — the car-sharing marketplace it had operated since 2011, plus the HyreCar gig-worker rental platform it had acquired in 2023. The company's board had approved the decision three days earlier, on February 7, 2025, after what interim CEO AJ Lee described as "an extensive analysis of all available alternatives" ([Getaround press release via Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-inc-announces-wind-down-us-operations-2025-02-11)). The stated reason was blunt: "Despite significant improvements in overall profitability and extensive restructuring efforts, the Company has faced an ongoing lack of liquidity which has made U.S. operations no longer viable" ([Getaround blog](https://getaround.com/blog/winding-down-our-carsharing-service-in-the-u-s/)). In plain language, the company simply did not have enough cash to keep running in the United States.

**What was affected.** The shutdown covered both of Getaround's US business lines: its core peer-to-peer car-sharing marketplace (where private car owners list vehicles for rent) and HyreCar, the gig-worker car-sharing platform it had acquired for $9.45 million in May 2023 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/)). Substantially all US-based employees were laid off. Most workers' final day was February 14, 2025, with a small number remaining to assist with the wind-down ([SF Chronicle](https://www.sfchronicle.com/bayarea/article/s-f-based-getaround-lay-nearly-u-s-based-20164114.php)). The company estimated it would incur **reduction in force charges** — costs associated with severance and benefits for terminated employees — of between $1.5 million and $2 million ([Getaround press release](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-inc-announces-wind-down-us-operations-2025-02-11)). Interim CEO AJ Lee, who had been appointed in 2024 during the company's freefall, stepped down as part of the shutdown. Customers with active rentals were given until the end of Wednesday, February 12, to return vehicles before Getaround's liability insurance coverage lapsed — a tight window that underscored how abruptly the shutdown was executed ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/)).

**European survival — for a time.** While the US business closed, Getaround continued to operate in several European markets through its Drivy subsidiary, which it had acquired in 2019 for $300 million. The European operations spanned eight countries: France, Germany, Spain, Austria, Belgium, the United Kingdom, Norway, and the Netherlands ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround)). The company's wind-down announcement noted that "Getaround is the leading car-sharing platform in Europe, which makes it uniquely well-suited to accelerate the Company's path to profitability" ([Getaround press release](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-inc-announces-wind-down-us-operations-2025-02-11)). The European business, headquartered in Paris, was the market leader in France and had a established presence across Western Europe ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/News/2026/05/Getaround-Combines-European-Operations-with-GoMore)). However, this survival was temporary.

**A stock price near zero.** By the time of the US shutdown, Getaround's stock — which had been delisted from the New York Stock Exchange in July 2024 and transferred to OTC Markets — was trading at approximately $0.0001 per share on the OTC Pink market. The company's **market capitalization** had collapsed to roughly $20,000 — effectively worthless for a company that had been valued at $1.2 billion just over two years earlier ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/GETR/); [TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/symbols/OTC-GETR/); [OTC Markets](https://www.otcmarkets.com/stock/GETR/security)). To put this in perspective: a $1.2 billion valuation had shrunk by more than 99.99% to about $20,000.

**The European sale and path to dissolution.** The European business survived the US shutdown but was ultimately sold. On April 30, 2026, Getaround sold its European subsidiaries — including Getaround SAS (France) and Getaround Norway AS — to GoMore ApS, a Denmark-based car-sharing company, for approximately €31.5 million (about $34 million at the time), plus up to €2.3 million in additional consideration tied to French research tax credits ([StockTitan 8-K filing](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/GETR/8-k-getaround-inc-reports-material-event-83400e038c70.html)). The sale created what was described as Europe's largest peer-to-peer car-sharing network ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/News/2026/05/Getaround-Combines-European-Operations-with-GoMore)).

The sale proceeds did not go to shareholders. Instead, they went to **Mudrick Capital**, a hedge fund that had become Getaround's primary creditor. Mudrick held $239.8 million in **Convertible Senior Secured PIK Toggle Notes** — a type of debt that can be converted into company stock — due 2027. In connection with the sale, Mudrick agreed to treat a **super priority secured promissory note** (a debt instrument with the highest repayment priority) as fully satisfied, canceling approximately $121.7 million of senior secured debt. Mudrick also provided a new $3.0 million super priority note to fund the wind-down process ([StockTitan 8-K filing](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/GETR/8-k-getaround-inc-reports-material-event-83400e038c70.html)).

On June 5, 2026, Getaround's board of directors determined that **dissolving and liquidating** the company — formally winding down all remaining operations and distributing any residual value to creditors and shareholders — was in the best interests of the company's "residual claimants" (those who would have a claim on any remaining assets). The board planned to seek stockholder approval for the dissolution and a related **plan of distribution** at a special meeting, under Section 275 of the Delaware General Corporation Law ([StockTitan 8-K filing](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/GETR/8-k-getaround-inc-reports-material-event-83400e038c70.html); [Globe and Mail via TipRanks](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/investing/markets/markets-news/Tipranks/2409747/getaround-plans-formal-dissolution-and-liquidation-process/)). This marked the formal beginning of the end for the company.

**Total investor capital lost.** Getaround had raised approximately $600 million to $750 million in total venture funding over its lifetime. The October 2020 Series E round alone brought total known venture funding to $600 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/14/getaround-raises-a-140-million-series-e-amid-rebound-in-short-distance-travel/); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2020/10/14/car-sharing-startup-getaround-raises-140-million/)), while TechCrunch reported the company had "raised more than $750 million from high-profile investors" including SoftBank Vision Fund, Menlo Ventures, and Reinvent Capital ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/)). The discrepancy likely stems from whether the 2018 SoftBank $300 million round is included in the total. After the €31.5 million European sale and the cancellation of $121.7 million in debt, the recovery for equity investors was effectively zero. The total loss of investor capital — from peak valuation to dissolution — was approximately $600–750 million.

**The five-year arc.** Getaround's collapse traces a clear trajectory: a $1.7 billion reported valuation in 2019, a $1.2 billion SPAC merger in December 2022, a 65% first-day stock crash, repeated layoffs (25% in January 2020, 10% in February 2023, 30% in February 2024), NYSE delisting in July 2024, US operations shutdown in February 2025, European sale in April 2026, and a board decision to dissolve in June 2026. The US entity — once the company's core market, founded in San Francisco in 2009 — has been wound down entirely. What remains is a shell company in liquidation, its stock trading at a fraction of a penny, its creditors partially repaid, and its equity holders with nothing.

## Comparative Analysis — Valuation and Workforce Divergence

### Side-by-side valuation trajectory comparison

The five-year period from 2020 to 2025 produced a stark divergence in the valuations of Turo and Getaround, the two largest peer-to-peer (P2P) car rental platforms in the United States. Turo's private-market valuation rose from approximately $1.24 billion to a range of $1.7–$2.7 billion, then remained in the billions after the company withdrew its initial public offering (IPO) filing in February 2025. Getaround's valuation collapsed from a reported $1.7 billion in 2019 to roughly $20,000 by late 2024 — a decline exceeding 99.99%. The structural reason for this divergence is straightforward: Turo remained a private company whose valuation only moved when new funding rounds or secondary-market transactions priced it, while Getaround went public via a SPAC and its valuation became subject to daily stock-market pricing that reflected its deteriorating financials.

**The valuation crossover.** At the start of 2020, Getaround's reported private valuation of approximately $1.7 billion (based on a 2019 funding effort tracked by [PitchBook and Crunchbase](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/carsharing-startup-getaround-reportedly-raising-200m-at-1-7b-valuation/)) exceeded Turo's $1.24 billion post-money valuation from the IAC-led Series E round in 2019–2020, as documented by [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/) and [Forge Global](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/TURO.PVT/). By December 2022, the two companies' valuations crossed. Getaround's SPAC merger with InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp. implied an equity valuation of $1.2 billion, as reported by [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html) and [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/carsharing-marketplace-getaround-go-public-via-12-bln-spac-deal-2022-05-11/) — already below Turo's $1.24 billion private mark. On its first day of trading (December 9, 2022), Getaround's shares crashed 65% on debut (see S3.2), meaning its actual market capitalization immediately fell to a fraction of the $1.2 billion SPAC figure. From that point forward, Turo's valuation exceeded Getaround's, and the gap widened dramatically each year.

**Key divergence points.** Three critical decisions and events drove the divergence:

1. **Pandemic response (2020).** Both companies were hit hard by COVID-19. Turo laid off 108 employees (30% of its workforce) in April 2020 but maintained its $1.24 billion private valuation because no new funding round repriced it. Getaround laid off approximately 150 employees (~25% of its workforce) in January 2020, then faced a second round of cuts in March 2020 as demand fell more than 75% and the company [sought a buyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround). Getaround's October 2020 $140 million Series E round kept it alive but did not restore its $1.7 billion valuation.

2. **Going public vs. staying private (2022).** Turo filed its S-1 registration statement for a traditional IPO in January 2022 but [delayed the offering](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/) as market conditions deteriorated, preserving its private valuation. Getaround chose the SPAC route, merging with InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp. in December 2022. The SPAC market had collapsed by late 2022 (see S1.2 for the CNBC SPAC Post Deal Index decline), and Getaround's shares crashed 65% on debut (see S3.2), immediately destroying billions in paper value.

3. **Profitability vs. losses (2023–2024).** Turo achieved net income of $154.7 million in 2022 and remained profitable through 2024 (revenue ~$958 million), which supported rising secondary-market valuations. Getaround posted a net loss of $46.8 million in 2022 and continued losing money, with Q3 2024 revenue of just $22.4 million and a net loss of $15.5 million, as documented in its [SEC filings](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm). The market punished this trajectory: Getaround's stock fell below $1 per share in February 2023 (triggering a [NYSE delisting warning](https://techcrunch.com/2023/02/01/car-sharing-platform-getaround-gets-delisting-warning-from-nyse/)), and by July 2024 the NYSE [suspended trading and commenced delisting proceedings](https://ir.theice.com/press/news-details/2024/NYSE-to-Commence-Delisting-Proceedings-Against-Getaround-Inc.-GETR/default.aspx) (see S3.4) because Getaround's market capitalization had fallen below the $15 million minimum requirement — it stood at just $12.19 million at the time, per [Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/company-news/getaround-to-appeal-nyse-delisting-trades-on-otc-markets-93CH-3513784).

**Interpreting Turo's valuation range.** Turo's valuation is not a single definitive number — different sources report different figures because they use different methods (last priced round vs. secondary-market transactions). As detailed in S2.3, the three key estimates are ~$1.24 billion (last priced round, per [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)), ~$1.7 billion (Forge Global secondary-market estimate, per [Yahoo Finance/Forge Global data](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/TURO.PVT/)), and ~$2.7 billion (secondary-market estimate from [TickerNerd](https://tickernerd.com/resources/ipo/turo-ipo/)). The range exists because private-company valuation is inherently imprecise. What is unambiguous is that Turo's valuation remained in the billions throughout 2023–2025, even after the company [withdrew its IPO filing](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/) on February 13, 2025, citing that "now is not the right time for Turo to pursue a public offering."

**Getaround's terminal collapse.** After being transferred to OTC Markets in July 2024 (see S3.4), Getaround's market capitalization collapsed to approximately $20,000, per [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/GETR/) and [Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/equities/interprivate-ii-acquisition) — effectively worthless. The stock traded at roughly $0.0001 per share on OTC Pink. On February 11, 2025, Getaround [announced the cessation of its US operations](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/), citing "ongoing lack of liquidity." The company's European business, operated through its Drivy subsidiary, survived the US shutdown but was ultimately sold for €31.5 million, and the board announced a plan to dissolve and liquidate the entire company in June 2026 (see S3.5). The total loss of investor capital across Getaround's lifetime was approximately $600–750 million.

The comparison reveals a fundamental lesson about the difference between private and public markets. Turo's patience as a private company — filing its S-1, waiting for better conditions, and ultimately withdrawing — preserved its valuation in the billions. Getaround's decision to go public via SPAC at the worst possible moment (December 2022, when the SPAC market had collapsed) exposed its weaknesses to daily market pricing and accelerated its collapse from $1.2 billion to near-zero in less than two years. Turo's growing revenue and sustained profitability gave private investors confidence to mark its valuation higher; Getaround's shrinking revenue and persistent losses gave public investors every reason to flee.

### Employee headcount trajectories and why they diverged

The workforce paths of Turo and Getaround between 2020 and 2025 could not have been more different. Turo shed 30% of its staff in a single pandemic-era cut, then grew steadily to nearly 1,000 employees before trimming 15% in 2025. Getaround, by contrast, shrank continuously through five separate layoff rounds — from roughly 600 employees to a skeleton crew, and finally to zero in its US operations. The divergence was not random; it was a direct consequence of each company's revenue scale, profitability, and access to cash.

**Turo: a single pandemic cut, then steady growth**

In April 2020, as COVID-19 lockdowns brought travel to a halt, Turo laid off 108 employees — 30% of its workforce at the time, implying a pre-layoff headcount of roughly 360 ([layoffs.fyi](https://layoffs.fyi/2020/04/21/turo-laid-off-108-employees/)). The cuts spanned all departments. But Turo's business rebounded faster than expected: revenue surged 213% in 2021 and the company posted its first operating profit. As growth resumed, so did hiring. By 2022, Turo had approximately 912 employees, and by 2023 that figure reached 931–936 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turo_(company)); [Revelio Labs](https://www.reveliolabs.com/companies/turo/employees)). In a March 2025 blog post, Turo's CEO Andre Haddad reported "nearly 1,000 employees" alongside 340,000 active vehicle listings and 140,000 active hosts ([Turo blog](https://turo.com/blog/news/celebrating-13-years-of-drive/)).

Turo's only major workforce reduction after 2020 came in April 2025, when the company laid off approximately 150 employees — about 15% of its staff, reducing headcount to roughly 850. CEO Andre Haddad attributed the cuts to "ongoing economic uncertainty" and said the move was necessary "to strengthen our position for long-term growth" ([SFGate](https://www.sfgate.com/tech/article/turo-lays-off-150-workers-economic-uncertainty-20281905.php)). Notably, Turo remained profitable through this period — the company reported net income in 2022, 2023, and the first nine months of 2024 ([Turo S-1/A filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1514587/000162828024047889/turoinc-sx1a12.htm)) — so the 2025 layoffs were a strategic restructuring rather than a survival measure.

**Getaround: five rounds of layoffs in five years**

Getaround's workforce story is one of continuous contraction. The company conducted five separate layoff rounds between 2020 and 2025, each detailed in the year-by-year sections above: approximately 150 employees (~25%) in January 2020 and a further ~100 in March 2020 as pandemic demand collapsed and the company sought a buyer (S3.1); 10% of staff (~42 employees) in February 2023 to "achieve a leaner path to profitability" (S3.3); 30% of the North American workforce in February 2024, saving ~$7 million annually (S3.4); and substantially all US-based employees on February 11, 2025, when the company ceased US operations entirely (S3.5). By December 31, 2022, Getaround had 283 full-time employees as disclosed in its annual SEC filing ([Getaround 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024038301/getr-20231231.htm)). The company did not disclose exact post-layoff headcount figures for the 2023–2024 rounds.

**Why the divergence: the causal chain**

The mechanism linking valuation decline to layoffs is straightforward. When a company's revenue is too small to cover its expenses — and it cannot raise new capital at a reasonable valuation — it must reduce its largest cost: people. Getaround's financial trajectory, detailed in S3.2 and S3.4, illustrates this dynamic: 2022 revenue of $62.3 million against a net loss of $46.8 million, and Q3 2024 revenue of $22.4 million against a net loss of $15.5 million, made workforce reduction inevitable. Each layoff round was explicitly described as "extending the cash runway." By cutting staff, Getaround reduced its monthly cash burn, buying time to reach profitability or find additional funding. But the underlying problem — revenue that was a fraction of Turo's — never resolved.

Turo, by contrast, operated in a virtuous cycle. Its revenue grew from $149.9 million in 2020 to $746.6 million in 2022 and approximately $958 million in 2024, with consistent profitability from 2021 onward (see S2.2 and S2.3 for detailed financials). Growing revenue funded hiring; a larger workforce supported further growth; and a rising private valuation made it easier to attract talent. Even Turo's 2025 layoffs — while painful for the roughly 150 affected employees — were a strategic choice by a profitable company, not a desperate bid for survival.

**The human cost**

Across Getaround's 2020–2025 restructuring events, well over 200 employees lost their jobs through the five layoff rounds detailed above (S3.1, S3.3, S3.4, S3.5). Turo's total layoffs over the same period were roughly 258 (108 in 2020 plus approximately 150 in 2025) — but those cuts bookended a period of substantial hiring that grew the workforce nearly threefold.

Getaround's persistently small revenue base, chronic losses, and shrinking cash reserves triggered a downward spiral in which each round of layoffs — while extending the cash runway — also reduced the company's capacity to grow, making the next round of cuts more likely. The result was a workforce that contracted by over 95% from its pre-2020 peak to the US shutdown in 2025.

### Key lessons: business model resilience, funding strategy, and market timing

The five-year trajectories of Turo and Getaround offer a stark natural experiment in how two companies in the same industry, founded in the same year and city, can end up in such different places. Below are the key lessons, grounded in the financial events documented throughout this report.

---

**Lesson 1: Going public is not always an upgrade — and can be actively harmful.**

Getaround's decision to go public via a SPAC in December 2022 proved catastrophic. The merger's $1.2 billion paper valuation (see S3.2) immediately evaporated as the stock crashed on its first day of trading (see S3.2; [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html)), and the decline never reversed. Once public, Getaround's valuation became subject to daily market pricing — meaning every quarterly loss, every layoff, and every delisting warning was immediately reflected in its share price, creating a downward spiral that made it harder to raise capital, attract talent, or inspire confidence.

Turo, by contrast, remained private throughout the period. Its valuation only moved when new funding rounds or secondary-market transactions priced it — and because it was growing revenue and maintaining profitability, those valuations trended upward rather than collapsing. When Turo finally withdrew its IPO filing on February 13, 2025, CEO Andre Haddad said the board "decided now is not the right time for Turo to pursue a public offering" and that the company would "take advantage of our company's plans to remain private in order to make important investments in the business that will build long term value" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/)). The withdrawal, while disappointing to those expecting a public debut, likely preserved the company's valuation. Had Turo gone public in 2022 or 2023 alongside Getaround, it might have faced similar market punishment despite its stronger financials — the SPAC market had already collapsed by late 2022 (see S1.2), and mergers completed in 2021–2022 lost an average of 59–67% of their value ([Yale Journal on Regulation](https://www.yalejreg.com/bulletin/was-the-spac-crash-predictable/)).

---

**Lesson 2: Profitability matters more than growth-at-all-costs.**

Turo achieved consistent profitability from 2021 onward, with net income peaking at $154.7 million in 2022 (see S2.2; [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/)). Even when growth decelerated and net profit fell to approximately $14.7 million in 2023, the company remained in the black. The CEO noted that Turo has been EBITDA-positive for four consecutive years ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)).

Getaround never achieved profitability in any year from 2020 to 2025. Its losses persisted throughout its public life — 2022 net loss of $46.8 million and Q3 2024 net loss of $15.5 million against revenue of just $22.4 million (see S3.2 and S3.4; [StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/GETR/getaround-reports-third-quarter-2024-financial-wvedc64aodrd.html)). By Q3 2024, Getaround's stockholders' equity was negative $33.5 million, and its accumulated deficit had reached negative $934.5 million (see S3.4). A company that cannot reach profitability eventually runs out of investor patience — and cash.

---

**Lesson 3: Revenue scale matters — and the gap was enormous.**

By 2024, Turo's annual revenue reached approximately $958 million (see S2.3; [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)), while Getaround's Q3 2024 revenue was $22.4 million (see S3.4) — roughly 1/40th of Turo's annual figure. This vast difference in scale meant Turo could spread its fixed costs (technology, insurance infrastructure, customer support) across a much larger revenue base, making profitability achievable. Getaround's revenue was simply too small to cover its operating costs, no matter how much it cut.

The divergence was also visible in platform size: Turo had approximately 360,000 active vehicle listings, 140,000+ active hosts, and 3.5 million active guests across five countries as of 2024 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/08/turo-car-rental-ipo-profitable-growth/); [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)). Getaround, at its peak, operated in over 1,000 cities but with a far smaller vehicle base and no comparable host network.

---

**Lesson 4: Pre-pandemic spending decisions had consequences.**

Getaround raised more total capital than Turo — approximately $600 million according to most sources, or up to $750 million including the 2018 SoftBank round (see S3.1; [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/14/getaround-raises-a-140-million-series-e-amid-rebound-in-short-distance-travel/); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2020/10/14/car-sharing-startup-getaround-raises-140-million/); [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)). But Getaround deployed much of that capital on acquisitions before the pandemic hit — Drivy, Nabobil, and later HyreCar (see S1.1 and S3.1) — consuming cash that would have been needed when the pandemic struck and demand collapsed by more than 75%.

Turo raised approximately $557.9 million in total funding — less than Getaround — but deployed it more efficiently. Rather than spending heavily on acquisitions, Turo invested in its platform, expanded organically into new markets (Australia, Canada, France, UK), and maintained a leaner cost structure. When the pandemic hit, Turo's asset-light model — it owns no vehicles, avoiding the debt and maintenance costs that burden traditional rental companies — proved to be a structural advantage. As CEO Andre Haddad explained: "We're an asset-light business. In many ways the pandemic has shown the advantage of the flexibility of our model" ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2021/03/29/business/rental-car-shortage-turo-airbnb-of-cars/index.html)). When traditional rental companies like Hertz, Avis, and Enterprise sold off their fleets during the pandemic, creating vehicle shortages and sky-high rental prices, Turo's marketplace of private car owners was perfectly positioned to absorb the demand surge — revenue grew 213% in 2021 ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)).

---

**Lesson 5: Market timing is critical.**

Getaround's SPAC merger was completed on December 8, 2022 (see S3.2) — arguably the worst possible moment. The SPAC market had already collapsed (see S1.2), tech stocks were in a broad decline, and investor appetite for unprofitable growth companies had evaporated ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html)). Getaround's $1.2 billion SPAC valuation (see S3.2) was already a steep discount from its $1.7 billion private valuation in 2019, and the market immediately discounted it further.

Turo filed its S-1 in January 2022 — also a difficult market — but then made the patient decision to wait. It continued filing updated S-1/A amendments through 2024, signaling intent without forcing a bad deal. When it finally withdrew in February 2025, it did so on its own terms, still profitable and still growing, preserving the option to go public later under better conditions ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/shrinking-ipo-pipeline-unicorn-turo-hits-pause/)).

---

**Lesson 6: The P2P car rental market is growing — but only one player captured that growth.**

The peer-to-peer car rental market was valued at approximately $8.4 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $22.7 billion by 2034, growing at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 11.6% ([DataIntelo](https://dataintelo.com/report/peer-to-peer-car-rental-market)). This growth is driven by rising vehicle ownership costs, urbanization, and consumer preference for access over ownership. Turo, with its 360,000+ vehicle listings and presence in five countries, is positioned to capture a significant share of this expanding market. Getaround, having shut down its US operations and sold its European business (see S3.5), will capture none of it.

---

**Lesson 7: Getaround's European survival suggests the model can work — but the US market proved too costly.**

Getaround's European operations, conducted through its Drivy subsidiary, survived the US shutdown (see S3.5; [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getaround)). This suggests that the P2P car rental model can be viable in certain markets — particularly in Europe, where car ownership costs are high, public transit is robust, and regulatory frameworks may be more favorable. However, the US market proved too competitive or too costly for Getaround to sustain. The company's US operations were burdened by higher insurance costs, regulatory complexity (including a $950,000 settlement with the DC Attorney General in 2021), and intense competition from Turo's larger platform.

---

**Outlook: 2025–2026 and beyond.**

Turo remains the dominant P2P car rental platform globally. The company is on track to reach $1 billion in annual revenue in 2025 (a company-stated projection, not yet confirmed by reported financials), maintains profitability, and continues to expand through partnerships (Uber Rent integration across five countries as of May 2025, Priceline inventory integration) and acquisitions (Kyte's select assets in July 2025) ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turo/)). Its private valuation remains in a range of approximately $1.24 billion to $1.7–$2.7 billion (see S2.3) — the wide range reflecting different methodologies rather than a single definitive number. Turo may still go public when market conditions improve, and its patience as a private company has so far preserved rather than destroyed value.

Getaround's story has effectively ended. The company sold its European business to GoMore for approximately €31.5 million in April 2026, and its board announced a plan to dissolve and liquidate the entire company in June 2026 (see S3.5; [StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/GETR/8-k-getaround-inc-reports-material-event-83400e038c70.html)). The total investor capital lost across Getaround's lifetime is estimated at approximately $600–750 million (see S3.1 and S3.5) — the amount raised from SoftBank, Reinvent Capital, and other investors, almost entirely wiped out. Common stockholders are likely to receive little to nothing, as the company's secured debt (approximately $239.8 million in convertible notes held by Mudrick Capital; see S3.5) far exceeds the value of remaining assets.

The comparison ultimately demonstrates a fundamental truth about venture-backed businesses: raising more money is not the same as building a stronger company. Getaround raised more capital but spent it on expansion and acquisitions before achieving profitability, then exposed its weaknesses to public market scrutiny at the worst possible moment. Turo raised less, spent it more carefully, achieved profitability, and preserved its value by staying private — even if that meant waiting years for an IPO that ultimately did not happen. For readers unfamiliar with venture capital, the lesson is clear: a company's valuation is only as durable as its underlying business model, and no amount of funding can substitute for a path to profitability.
