# Turo vs. Getaround: A Comparative Analysis of Performance, Economic Events, and Valuation Impact (2020–2025)

**Abstract**

Turo and Getaround, both founded in 2009 as peer-to-peer (P2P) car-sharing platforms, followed dramatically different trajectories between 2020 and 2025. Turo, which raised $596 million across 12 funding rounds and reached approximately $880 million in annual revenue by 2023, filed for an initial public offering (IPO) in January 2022 but ultimately withdrew its plans in January 2025, cutting 150 employees (approximately 15% of its workforce) in April 2025. Getaround, which raised approximately $400 million (including a $300 million round led by SoftBank's Vision Fund), went public via a special purpose acquisition company (SPAC) merger in December 2022 at a combined valuation of about $1.18 billion, only to see its stock price collapse to $0.0001 and face delisting from the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) by August 2024. The most important finding of this research is that the two companies' divergent fates were driven less by differences in their products or markets than by the timing and method of their capital market strategies—Turo's patience preserved its flexibility, while Getaround's rush to go public via SPAC at the market peak locked in a valuation it could never sustain.

## 1. Background and Key Concepts

### 1.1 What Is Peer-to-Peer Car Sharing?

Peer-to-peer (P2P) car sharing is a business model where ordinary people rent out their personal vehicles to other people through an online platform—think of it as "Airbnb for cars." Instead of going to a traditional rental company like Hertz or Enterprise, which owns large fleets of vehicles, a customer uses a smartphone app to find a car owned by a neighbor, book it for a few hours or days, and unlock it using their phone. The car owner earns money from a vehicle that would otherwise sit idle, and the renter gets access to a wider variety of cars, often at lower prices than traditional rental agencies charge.

Two companies dominate this space in the United States: Turo and Getaround. Both were founded in 2009 in San Francisco, California, and both launched their platforms around 2010–2011. Despite starting in the same city, in the same year, and in the same industry, they followed very different paths over the following decade and a half—a divergence that this report explores in detail.

### 1.2 Key Financial Terms Explained

Since this report is written for readers who may not be familiar with U.S. economics and venture capital, here are the key terms used throughout, explained in plain language:

- **Valuation**: How much a company is considered to be worth. For a private company, this is determined during fundraising rounds when investors agree to buy shares at a certain price. For a public company (one whose shares trade on a stock exchange), the valuation (often called "market capitalization" or "market cap") is calculated by multiplying the total number of shares by the current share price.

- **Venture capital (VC)**: Money that investment firms provide to startup companies in exchange for ownership shares (equity). Venture capitalists (VCs) hope the startup will grow rapidly and eventually go public or be acquired, allowing them to sell their shares at a much higher price. VC firms typically invest in high-risk, high-reward companies.

- **Funding round**: Each time a startup raises money from investors, it's called a "round." Rounds are labeled alphabetically—Series A (usually the first major round), Series B, Series C, and so on. Each round typically values the company at a higher price than the previous one, reflecting the company's growth.

- **IPO (Initial Public Offering)**: The first time a company sells its shares to the general public on a stock exchange. This is how private startups "go public." An IPO allows early investors and employees to sell their shares and gives the company access to a large pool of public capital. The process requires filing detailed financial documents (called an S-1 in the U.S.) with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the U.S. government agency that oversees financial markets.

- **SPAC (Special Purpose Acquisition Company)**: An alternative way for a company to go public without going through the traditional IPO process. A SPAC is a "blank check" company that raises money from investors first, then looks for a private company to merge with. When the merger is complete, the private company becomes publicly traded. SPAC mergers became extremely popular in 2020–2021 but resulted in many spectacular failures as the market cooled.

- **Delisting**: When a company's shares are removed from a stock exchange. This can happen because the company fails to meet the exchange's requirements (such as maintaining a minimum share price) or because the company requests removal. After delisting from a major exchange like the NYSE, shares may still trade on over-the-counter (OTC) markets, which have far less oversight and liquidity.

- **Reverse stock split**: A company reduces the number of its outstanding shares by combining multiple shares into one. For example, in a 1-for-10 reverse split, every 10 shares become 1 share. This artificially raises the per-share price (since there are fewer shares) but does not change the company's total value. Companies typically do this to meet a stock exchange's minimum price requirement.

### 1.3 Company Profiles

**Turo** was founded in 2009 by Shelby Clark under the name "RelayRides." It rebranded as Turo in 2015 to reflect a shift from short-term car sharing toward longer-term rentals and travel use cases. The company operates an "asset-light" model—it owns no vehicles itself, instead taking a commission (typically 10–40% of the rental price) on each transaction between car owners ("hosts") and renters ("guests"). Turo expanded internationally to Canada (2016), the United Kingdom, and Australia. By 2024, the company had reached $1 billion in cumulative gross revenue and operated across more than 11,000 cities. Key investors include Google Ventures (GV), Kleiner Perkins, and IAC/InterActiveCorp, which led a $250 million Series E round in 2019.[^1][2](https://fourweekmba.com/turo-business-model-2/)

**Getaround** was also founded in 2009, by Sam Zaid and Jessica Scorpio, and launched its platform in 2011. Unlike Turo, Getaround developed proprietary hardware called "Getaround Connect"—a device installed in cars that allows keyless entry and ignition via smartphone, eliminating the need for physical key exchange. Getaround won the TechCrunch Disrupt startup competition in 2011, giving it early visibility. In 2019, Getaround acquired Drivy, a European P2P car-sharing platform, for approximately $300 million, expanding its operations to France, Germany, Norway, Spain, and the United Kingdom. Getaround's most significant investor is SoftBank, the Japanese technology investment conglomerate, which led a $300 million Series D funding round in August 2018.[^3][4](https://www.weiyangx.com/299306.html)

## 2. Macro Environment and Industry Context (2020–2025)

### 2.1 The COVID-19 Pandemic and Travel

The COVID-19 pandemic, which caused global lockdowns beginning in March 2020, had a severe initial impact on the car rental and car-sharing industries. Travel ground to a near-halt—airlines cut flights, hotels closed, and tourism stopped. P2P car sharing was particularly hard hit because lockdowns specifically discouraged shared vehicle use on health grounds.[5](https://marketpublishers.com/report/services/other_services/peer-to-peer-car-sharing-market-strat.html)

However, the pandemic's effect was paradoxical. As lockdowns eased in the second half of 2020, P2P car sharing actually benefited from a shift in consumer behavior. People who previously used public transportation or ride-sharing services (like Uber and Lyft) now preferred the privacy and cleanliness of having their own vehicle. Traditional car rental companies also struggled—Hertz filed for bankruptcy in May 2020, creating a temporary vacuum in the rental market that P2P platforms could fill.[6](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/car-rental-industrys-credit-risk-a-bouncy-ride-during-the-pandemic)

For Turo, revenue grew from $142 million in 2019 to approximately $150 million in 2020—a modest 6% increase that masked the dramatic story within the year: revenue collapsed in the first half, then recovered in the second half with 7% year-over-year growth. CEO Andre Haddad told The Wall Street Journal that the company cut costs aggressively during the pandemic's early months and achieved its first quarterly profit, planning for a 2021 IPO.[7](http://m.toutiao.com/group/6943852354370273800/)

### 2.2 Interest Rates and the Venture Capital Climate

The years 2020–2021 marked an extraordinary boom period for venture capital and startup funding. The U.S. Federal Reserve (the country's central bank) cut interest rates to near zero in March 2020 to support the economy during the pandemic. This made borrowing extremely cheap and pushed investors to seek higher returns by investing in riskier assets like startup companies and stocks. The result was a frenzy of funding—VC firms poured record amounts of capital into startups, and IPO markets saw the most activity since the 1999 dot-com boom.[8](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/06/benchmarking-government-support-for-venture-capital-country-notes_2cacbf3f/canada_fdc9fc53.html)

This environment began to shift dramatically in 2022. Facing rising inflation (a general increase in prices across the economy), the Federal Reserve began raising interest rates aggressively—increases that continued through 2023 and into 2024. As borrowing became more expensive, investors became less willing to fund unprofitable startups. Venture capital investment dropped substantially in 2022 and fell further in 2023, creating what industry observers called a "VC winter." For companies that had grown accustomed to easy money, this was a seismic shock.[8](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/06/benchmarking-government-support-for-venture-capital-country-notes_2cacbf3f/canada_fdc9fc53.html)

The timing of this shift proved critical for both Turo and Getaround. Companies that raised money or went public during the 2020–2021 boom benefited from high valuations and abundant capital. Companies that waited—or that went public as the market turned—faced far harsher conditions. By 2024, only 12 technology companies went public, down from 67 in 2021.[9](https://www.ainvest.com/news/turo-strategic-layoffs-ipo-hesitation-crossroads-car-sharing-economy-2504/)

### 2.3 Regulation and Insurance Landscape

P2P car sharing operates in a regulatory environment that is fundamentally more complex than other sharing economy businesses like Airbnb (lodging) or Uber (ride-sharing). In the United States, vehicle insurance and liability are regulated at the state level—meaning each of the 50 states has its own rules about insurance requirements, taxation, and vehicle safety standards for P2P car-sharing programs. There is no federal (national-level) framework that preempts these state-level regulations.[10](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2023/pub/chap_244d.htm)

Both Turo and Getaround must provide insurance coverage during the rental period, but the specific requirements vary by state. Some states, like New York, California, Arizona, Texas, and Washington, have enacted specific legislation defining P2P car-sharing programs and their insurance obligations. This patchwork of regulations adds significant operational complexity and cost, particularly for companies trying to scale nationally.[10](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2023/pub/chap_244d.htm)

This regulatory burden is one reason why P2P car sharing has proven harder to scale than other sharing economy models. Cars are mobile, high-liability assets that can cause accidents, injuries, and property damage—unlike a spare bedroom on Airbnb, which is stationary and carries far less risk. Insurance costs, regulatory compliance, and the logistical coordination of vehicle maintenance all create structural challenges that limit profit margins.

## 3. Turo: Economic Events, Valuation, and Employment (2020–2025)

### 3.1 Funding History and Valuation Milestones

Turo's funding history spans 12 rounds totaling $596 million. The most significant rounds include:[1](https://startupintros.com/orgs/turo)

- **July 2019**: $250 million Series E, led by IAC/InterActiveCorp (the media conglomerate led by Joey Levin). This was the largest single round in Turo's history and brought the company's total funding to approximately $566 million.
- **February 2020**: $30 million additional Series E extension, with investors including Manhattan Venture Partners, Allen & Company, and a group of professional athletes (including NBA and NFL players like Larry Fitzgerald Jr. and rapper 2 Chainz).

According to PitchBook data, Turo was valued at approximately $1.5 billion in 2020, placing it in the "unicorn" category (private companies valued at $1 billion or more). A later estimate in March 2024 valued the company at approximately $1.25 billion. By 2025, after the IPO withdrawal, some reports referenced a private valuation of around $2.5 billion, though this figure is difficult to verify for a private company.[^11][12](https://microventures.com/2025-ipo-outlook)

### 3.2 The IPO Journey: Filing, Delays, and Withdrawal

Turo's IPO journey was one of the longest and most drawn-out in recent startup history:[^13][14](https://www.vstar.com/cn/article/turo-ipo-turo-stock-analysis)

**January 2022**: Turo publicly filed its S-1 registration statement with the SEC, announcing plans to list on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker symbol "TURO." Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan Chase were selected as lead underwriters (the investment banks that manage the IPO process). The filing came at the tail end of the IPO boom—Airbnb had successfully gone public in December 2020, and many travel startups were expected to follow.

**2022–2024**: Over the next three years, Turo updated its SEC filing 12 times—most recently in November 2024—repeatedly postponing the actual public offering. The timing could not have been worse: by the time Turo filed publicly in January 2022, the Federal Reserve had begun signaling rate increases, and the IPO market was cooling rapidly. The company attempted to restart its IPO process in late 2023, with investor roadshows (presentations to potential investors) reportedly planned for October 2023, but these never materialized into a public listing.

**January 2025**: Turo officially withdrew its IPO plans, citing an "unfavorable market environment" in its SEC filing. CEO Andre Haddad framed the decision as an opportunity to "make important investments in the business," emphasizing long-term value over short-term market pressures.[9](https://www.ainvest.com/news/turo-strategic-layoffs-ipo-hesitation-crossroads-car-sharing-economy-2504/)

The withdrawal was driven by a combination of factors:[9](https://www.ainvest.com/news/turo-strategic-layoffs-ipo-hesitation-crossroads-car-sharing-economy-2504/)

- **Slowing growth**: Revenue growth decelerated from a peak of 213% in 2021 to just 18% in 2023 and 8% year-over-year in early 2024. The explosive pandemic-recovery growth was not sustainable.
- **Margin erosion**: Gross margins (the percentage of revenue left after direct costs) fell from 52.1% in early 2023 to 44.8% by mid-2024, reflecting rising operational costs and pricing pressures.
- **IPO market drought**: Only 12 technology companies went public in 2024, down from 67 in 2021. Investors had become highly skeptical of unprofitable or margin-constrained business models.
- **Reputational crisis**: In January 2025, vehicles rented through Turo were used in two high-profile incidents—a deadly truck collision in New Orleans and a bomb-laden Tesla explosion in Las Vegas. While Turo cooperated with law enforcement and denied liability, the events eroded public trust at the worst possible moment for the company's public market ambitions.

### 3.3 Revenue and Growth Metrics

Turo's revenue trajectory tells the story of a company that rode the pandemic recovery wave but could not sustain its growth rate:[^15][16](https://itechnolabs.ca/blog/apps-like-turo/)

| Year | Revenue | Growth Rate | Net Income |
|------|---------|-------------|------------|
| 2019 | $142 million | — | -$98.6 million (loss) |
| 2020 | $150 million | +6% | -$97.1 million (loss) |
| 2021 | $469 million | +213% | -$129 million (loss, first 9 months) |
| 2022 | $746.6 million | +59% | +$154.7 million (profit) |
| 2023 | $879.7 million | +18% | +$14.7 million (profit) |
| 2024 | ~$958 million | +9% | Operating loss of $9.7M (first 9 months) |

The company reached $1 billion in cumulative gross revenue by 2024. As of the end of 2023, Turo's platform had approximately 360,000 active vehicles and about 3.7 million renters. Bookings growth (total days booked on the platform) also slowed dramatically: 75% growth from 2021 to 2022, 27.5% in 2023, and only 9% year-over-year in the first nine months of 2024.[15](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/turo-car-rental-marketplace-scraps-ipo)

### 3.4 Valuation Changes and Their Drivers

Turo's valuation story is less dramatic than Getaround's—precisely because the company never went public. Its valuation can be tracked through three phases:[^11][12](https://microventures.com/2025-ipo-outlook)

**Phase 1 (2020–2021): Peak optimism.** Valued at approximately $1.5 billion based on the 2019–2020 funding rounds. The pandemic recovery and the broader sharing economy boom (Airbnb's successful IPO in December 2020) created optimism that Turo could be the next big sharing economy success story.

**Phase 2 (2022–2024): Stagnation.** As growth slowed and the IPO window closed, Turo's valuation likely declined or stagnated. The March 2024 estimate of $1.25 billion represented a decline from the 2020 peak, reflecting the market's reduced appetite for growth-stage tech companies. During this period, Turo continued updating its IPO filing but could not find favorable market conditions.

**Phase 3 (2025): Private recalibration.** After withdrawing the IPO, Turo was reportedly valued at around $2.5 billion in private markets. However, this figure should be treated with caution—it comes from media reports rather than official disclosures, and private company valuations are inherently less transparent than public market prices. What is clear is that Turo's valuation did not collapse the way Getaround's did, because Turo never had to face the daily judgment of public market investors.

The key driver of Turo's valuation stability was its decision to stay private. Unlike Getaround, which locked in a public market valuation that then collapsed, Turo retained the flexibility to adjust its strategy, cut costs, and wait for better market conditions—flexibility that public companies do not have.

### 3.5 Employee Impact

Turo's employment story is relatively contained compared to Getaround's. According to public data, Turo had approximately 1,000 employees. The company's major workforce reduction came in April 2025, when it cut 150 positions—about 15% of its staff—following the decision to shelve its IPO plans. The layoffs were reported by Bloomberg and confirmed through state filings with California's Employment Development Department.[^17][18](https://www.page.ke/articles/a-comprehensive-list-of-2025-tech-layoffs-techcrunch)

The cuts followed a pattern common in the technology industry during this period: companies that had hired aggressively during the 2020–2021 boom were forced to right-size their workforces as growth slowed and funding dried up. For Turo, the layoffs represented a strategic recalibration—reducing costs to extend the company's financial "runway" (the amount of time it can operate before needing more capital) while maintaining its core marketplace operations.

Prior to the April 2025 layoffs, Turo had been actively hiring, with job postings for engineering, operations, and strategy roles appearing through 2024. The company's workforce had grown to support its international expansion and product development during the boom years, making the eventual cutback a correction rather than a sign of imminent failure.

## 4. Getaround: Economic Events, Valuation, and Employment (2020–2025)

### 4.1 Funding History and SoftBank Investment

Getaround's funding history is dominated by one massive round:[^19][20](https://finance.sina.cn/7x24/2018-08-22/detail-ihhzsnea5665870.d.html)

**August 2018**: $300 million Series D, led by SoftBank Vision Fund (the massive technology investment fund managed by Japan's SoftBank Group), with Toyota Motor Corporation participating. This round brought Getaround's total funding to approximately $400 million and was one of the largest investments ever in the P2P car-sharing space.

SoftBank's investment philosophy at the time was "growth at all costs"—the firm was known for pouring enormous sums into late-stage startups (like Uber, WeWork, and DoorDash) to help them achieve market dominance before worrying about profitability. This approach profoundly shaped Getaround's strategy, pushing the company toward aggressive expansion rather than sustainable unit economics.

**2019**: Getaround used some of this capital to acquire Drivy, a European P2P car-sharing platform, for approximately $300 million. This acquisition expanded Getaround's operations to several European countries but also added significant operational complexity and costs.

### 4.2 The SPAC Merger and Public Listing

In May 2022, Getaround announced a definitive business combination agreement with InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp. (NYSE: IPVA), a SPAC that had raised approximately $259 million in its own IPO in March 2021. The transaction valued Getaround's enterprise at approximately $900 million, with the combined company valued at about $1.176 billion.[^21][22](https://web-staging.clay.com/dossier/getaround-funding)

The deal was completed on December 8, 2022, and Getaround's shares began trading on the NYSE under the ticker symbol "GETR" on December 9, 2022. In conjunction with the merger, Getaround also secured $175 million in debt financing from Mudrick Capital Management, bringing total transaction proceeds to over $400 million.[22](https://web-staging.clay.com/dossier/getaround-funding)

The timing of this SPAC merger was deeply unfavorable. By December 2022, the SPAC market was already collapsing—SPAC IPOs had dropped from 613 in 2021 to 86 in 2022, and over 90% of companies that went public via SPAC were trading below their offering price. Getaround was going public at the tail end of a bubble that was rapidly deflating.[23](https://www.foley.com/zh/insights/publications/2025/09/spac-4-0-from-spectacular-failures-to-a-disciplined-renaissance/)

### 4.3 Post-Merger Collapse and Delisting

Getaround's experience as a public company was a textbook case of SPAC failure:[^24][^25][26](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000095017024127263/getr-ex99_1.htm)

**Revenue reality**: Despite a valuation of over $1 billion at merger, Getaround's quarterly revenue was only in the $17–24 million range throughout 2023–2024—roughly one-tenth of Turo's revenue. Annual revenue for 2023 was approximately $73–80 million, compared to Turo's $879.7 million. The gap between the company's valuation and its actual revenue was enormous.

**Stock price collapse**: Getaround's stock price fell steadily from its debut. By late 2023, the NYSE had already initiated delisting proceedings for the company's warrants (a type of security that gives the holder the right to purchase shares at a specific price). The company's common stock price continued to fall throughout 2023 and 2024.

**NYSE delisting**: On July 9, 2024, Getaround received notice from the NYSE that it would commence delisting proceedings. The NYSE's determination was based on the company's failure to meet the exchange's continued listing standards, primarily its abnormally low stock price. On July 17, 2024, Getaround effected a 1-for-10 reverse stock split—a last attempt to artificially raise its share price to meet listing requirements. The split failed to resolve the underlying issues, and on August 16, 2024, Getaround was officially delisted from the NYSE. Its shares moved to the OTC Pink Market (an over-the-counter market with minimal oversight and reporting requirements), trading under the symbol "GETR."[^24][25](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240709885399/en/NYSE-to-Commence-Delisting-Proceedings-Against-Getaround-Inc.-GETR)

**Current status**: As of 2025, Getaround's stock trades at approximately $0.0001 per share on the OTC market—effectively worthless. The company's market capitalization has been almost entirely erased, representing a near-total destruction of the $1.176 billion valuation from the December 2022 merger. In February 2025, Getaround announced it was winding down its North American operations and closing its HyreCar business (a gig-economy car rental platform it had acquired in May 2023 for $9.45 million).[27](https://www.leadiq.com/c/getaround/5a1d885d2400002400621673)

### 4.4 Employee Impact and Restructuring

Getaround's employee story is one of repeated and escalating workforce reductions, each round a more desperate version of the last:[^28][29](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20231214523493/en/Getaround-Reports-Strong-3Q-2023-Results-with-42-Year-Over-Year-Growth-in-Revenue)

**February 2023** (approximately two months after going public): The Board of Directors approved a restructuring plan to "streamline operations and reduce costs to achieve a leaner path to profitability." The plan targeted $25–30 million in annual cost savings. Affected employees were offered severance of 6–12 weeks depending on tenure, plus continued health insurance coverage. This first restructuring came almost immediately after the SPAC merger, suggesting that the company's public market reality did not match its pre-merger projections.

**February 2024**: Getaround announced a second restructuring plan "to accelerate the path to profitability." By this point, the company was already facing the prospect of NYSE delisting, and the restructuring was as much about survival as about efficiency. The exact number of employees affected in this round was not publicly disclosed, but it followed the same pattern of cost reduction.

**2025 wind-down**: As the company wound down North American operations, further job cuts occurred, though specific numbers were not publicly reported. The closure of the HyreCar business (acquired less than two years earlier) also resulted in workforce reductions.

The pattern of Getaround's employee impact follows what economists call a "downward spiral": each restructuring cut costs but also reduced the company's ability to grow revenue, which in turn required further cost cuts. Unlike Turo's single, strategic layoff event, Getaround's workforce reductions were a continuous process of retrenchment, reflecting the company's inability to find a sustainable business model.

## 5. Comparative Analysis: Turo vs. Getaround

The following table provides a side-by-side comparison of the two companies across key dimensions:

| Dimension | Turo | Getaround |
|-----------|------|-----------|
| Founded | 2009 (as RelayRides) | 2009 |
| Business model | P2P car-sharing marketplace (asset-light) | P2P car-sharing marketplace with proprietary hardware (Getaround Connect) |
| Total funding | ~$596 million across 12 rounds | ~$400 million+ (including $300M SoftBank-led Series D) |
| Key investors | GV, Kleiner Perkins, IAC, Menlo Ventures | SoftBank Vision Fund, Toyota, Mudrick Capital |
| Peak valuation | ~$1.5B (2020, private) → ~$2.5B (2025, private estimate) | ~$1.176B (Dec 2022, SPAC merger) → ~$0 (2025, OTC) |
| IPO path | S-1 filed Jan 2022, updated 12 times, withdrew Jan 2025 | SPAC merger with InterPrivate II, completed Dec 2022 |
| Current public status | Private (never went public) | Public on OTC Pink Market (delisted from NYSE Aug 2024) |
| Revenue (2023) | $879.7 million | ~$73–80 million |
| Revenue growth trend | 213% (2021) → 18% (2023) → 9% (2024) | Flat to declining (~$17–24M/quarter in 2024) |
| Profitability | Profitable in 2022 ($154.7M net income); declining margins | Never profitable; continuous losses |
| Employee count | ~1,000 (cut 150 in Apr 2025) | Not publicly disclosed (multiple restructuring rounds: Feb 2023, Feb 2024, 2025 wind-down) |
| Key acquisitions | Wheelz (2013) | Drivy ($300M, 2019), HyreCar ($9.45M, 2023, later closed) |
| International operations | US, Canada, UK, Australia, France | US, Europe (winding down North America in 2025) |
| Outcome (as of 2025) | Private, operating, launching new products | Near-total valuation destruction; winding down operations |

**The most striking finding** from this comparison is not the differences between the two companies' products or markets—they operate in essentially the same industry with similar models. Rather, it is the **divergence in capital market strategy** that explains their dramatically different outcomes. Three factors stand out:

**Factor 1: Timing.** Turo filed for an IPO in January 2022 but had the discipline to wait when market conditions turned unfavorable. Getaround went public via SPAC in December 2022—at the exact moment the SPAC bubble was bursting. The difference between "filing and waiting" versus "completing the merger at the wrong time" proved decisive.

**Factor 2: Revenue scale.** Turo's revenue was approximately 10 times Getaround's ($880M vs. ~$73–80M in 2023). This gap meant that Turo could potentially justify a billion-dollar valuation based on its actual business performance, while Getaround's valuation was almost entirely based on growth projections that never materialized.

**Factor 3: Investor philosophy.** Turo's investors (GV, Kleiner Perkins, IAC) generally followed a more traditional venture capital approach, supporting growth while maintaining some discipline on unit economics. Getaround's lead investor, SoftBank Vision Fund, was known for its "growth at all costs" philosophy, pushing companies to expand aggressively before achieving profitability—a strategy that spectacularly backfired when the market turned.

## 6. Critical Perspectives: Challenges, Risks, and Limitations

### 6.1 Business Model Sustainability

The P2P car-sharing model faces structural challenges that make it inherently harder to scale profitably than other sharing economy models. Unlike Airbnb, which connects renters with stationary rooms (relatively low-risk, low-maintenance assets), P2P car sharing involves mobile, high-liability vehicles that:[^9][30](https://www.gminsights.com/industry-analysis/carsharing-market)

- **Depreciate rapidly**: Cars lose value over time through wear and tear, creating ongoing costs for owners.
- **Require maintenance**: Regular servicing, cleaning, and repairs add operational complexity and cost.
- **Carry significant insurance liability**: Accidents involving rented vehicles can result in expensive claims, lawsuits, and reputational damage.
- **Are subject to regulatory complexity**: State-by-state insurance and taxation requirements create administrative overhead.

These structural costs explain why both Turo and Getaround have struggled to achieve sustained profitability. Turo's gross margins fell from 52.1% to 44.8% between 2023 and 2024, reflecting the rising costs of insurance, customer support, and platform operations. Getaround never achieved profitability at all.

### 6.2 Competitive Threats

P2P car-sharing platforms face competition from multiple directions:[^9][31](https://www.researchandmarkets.com/report/p2p-carsharing-market)

- **Traditional rental companies**: Enterprise, Hertz, and Avis dominate the U.S. car rental market with approximately 80% market share. These companies benefit from economies of scale, established brand trust, and decades of operational experience. After Hertz emerged from bankruptcy in 2021, traditional rental companies regained their footing.
- **Ride-sharing platforms**: Uber and Lyft, while addressing different use cases (same-day point-to-point transportation vs. multi-day vehicle rental), compete for the same consumer transportation budget. Uber also partnered with Turo in late 2024 to improve customer acquisition, blurring the competitive lines.
- **Automaker subscription services**: Several automakers have launched their own vehicle subscription or short-term rental services, leveraging their existing dealer networks.

### 6.3 Limitations of Available Data

Several limitations should be noted regarding the data in this report:

- **Private company financials**: As a private company, Turo is not required to publicly disclose detailed financial information. Revenue figures come from its S-1 filing (which was publicly available before withdrawal) and media reports, but current financials may differ.
- **Getaround's exact employee count**: The precise number of employees affected in each of Getaround's restructuring rounds was not publicly disclosed in most cases. SEC filings mention restructuring charges and severance terms but do not always specify headcount reductions.
- **Valuation estimates**: Private company valuations (like Turo's reported $2.5 billion in 2025) come from media reports and secondary market data, not official disclosures. These figures should be treated as estimates.
- **Getaround's current operational status**: As of 2025, Getaround appears to be winding down North American operations, but the full extent of its remaining activities is unclear from public sources.

### 6.4 The SPAC Market Context

Getaround's failure should be understood within the broader context of the SPAC market collapse. From 2021 to 2023, SPAC-related failures caused cumulative value destruction estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars. Over 90% of companies that went public via SPAC saw their stock fall below the offering price. Only 15–25% of SPAC mergers created lasting value. Getaround was far from alone in its SPAC failure—but its case is particularly extreme, representing one of the most complete valuation destructions in the SPAC era.[23](https://www.foley.com/zh/insights/publications/2025/09/spac-4-0-from-spectacular-failures-to-a-disciplined-renaissance/)

This context matters because it suggests that Getaround's failure was not purely a company-specific problem but also a systemic one—the SPAC mechanism itself, as used during the 2020–2022 bubble, tended to bring companies to market that were not ready for public scrutiny, with valuations disconnected from fundamental business performance.

## 7. Conclusion and Outlook

### Key Findings

This research set out to compare Turo and Getaround's performance from 2020 to 2025, detailing major economic events and their effects on valuation and employment. The most important findings are:

1. **Capital market strategy was the decisive differentiator.** Both companies operated in the same industry, with similar business models, and both were founded in the same year and city. Yet Turo survived (and remains operational) while Getaround's valuation was nearly completely destroyed. The primary reason was not product quality or market position, but the timing and method of going public: Turo had the patience to stay private when conditions were unfavorable; Getaround rushed to go public via SPAC at the worst possible moment.

2. **The COVID-19 pandemic created a temporary growth illusion.** Both companies benefited from the pandemic recovery in 2021–2022, as consumers shifted from public transit to private vehicles. Turo's revenue surged 213% in 2021. But this growth was a one-time recovery effect, not a sustainable trend—and when growth normalized, both companies struggled to maintain their pandemic-era valuations.

3. **P2P car sharing is structurally harder than other sharing economy models.** The combination of vehicle liability, insurance costs, regulatory complexity, and maintenance coordination creates unit economics that are fundamentally more challenging than lodging (Airbnb) or ride-sharing (Uber). Neither Turo nor Getaround achieved the kind of margins that would justify billion-dollar valuations based on traditional metrics.

4. **The "growth at all costs" era ended with a reckoning.** Getaround's SoftBank-funded expansion strategy—acquiring companies, entering new markets, and going public before achieving profitability—was a product of the 2020–2021 era of cheap money. When interest rates rose and investors demanded profitability, this strategy collapsed. Turo's more conservative approach (slower expansion, focus on unit economics, willingness to delay the IPO) proved more resilient.

### Outlook

For Turo, the path forward remains challenging but viable. The company continues to operate, generate revenue (approaching $1 billion annually), and launch new products (such as Turo Monthly, a long-term vehicle access offering launched in October 2025). However, its growth is decelerating, margins are under pressure, and the reputational damage from the January 2025 incidents remains a concern. The company's private status gives it flexibility, but at some point, its investors will need an exit—either through an IPO, an acquisition, or a secondary market sale.

For Getaround, the outlook is bleak. With its stock at $0.0001, its NYSE listing revoked, and its North American operations winding down, the company appears to be in terminal decline. Its European operations (acquired through the Drivy acquisition) may represent some residual value, but the overall enterprise has been a near-total loss for investors.

For the broader P2P car-sharing industry, the Turo-Getaround comparison offers a cautionary tale about the dangers of excess capital, the importance of timing in public market entries, and the structural challenges of applying the sharing economy model to mobile, high-liability assets. The industry's future will likely be shaped by whether any participant can solve the fundamental unit economics challenge—achieving sustainable profitability in a business where insurance, maintenance, and regulatory costs are structurally embedded.
