# Turo vs. Getaround: A Tale of Two Car-Sharing Companies (2020–2025)

*A research report written for readers with no background in US economics or venture capital*

## Executive Summary

Turo and Getaround were founded in the same city (San Francisco) in the same year (2009) on the same idea — letting ordinary car owners rent out their personal vehicles through an app, often described as "Airbnb for cars." Between 2020 and 2025, both companies lived through the same economic storms: the COVID-19 shock, a flood of cheap investment money, and then a painful reversal when US interest rates rose sharply in 2022. Yet their outcomes could hardly be more different. **Turo** survived as a profitable private company worth roughly **$2.2 billion** by early 2025, while **Getaround** — which chose to list on the stock market in December 2022 through a shortcut called a SPAC merger at a valuation of about **$1.2 billion** — lost more than **99%** of its market value, was kicked off the New York Stock Exchange, and **shut down its entire US business in February 2025**, laying off nearly all of its American staff. This report walks through the major economic events for each company year by year, explains what "valuation" means and why it rose or fell, and details how each decline translated into job losses.

## 1. Key Concepts in Plain Language

A few financial terms are essential to this story, and each is explained here before it is used. A company's **valuation** is simply an estimate of what the whole business is worth. For a private company (one whose shares are not traded on a stock exchange), the valuation is set whenever investors buy a slice of the company in a **funding round** — if an investor pays $250 million for roughly a quarter of a company, that implies the whole company is worth about $1 billion. Private companies backed by **venture capital (VC)** — investment funds that bet on young, fast-growing companies in exchange for ownership stakes — are expected eventually to give those investors a way to cash out, usually by going public.

**Going public** means selling shares to anyone on a stock exchange, a process called an **IPO** (initial public offering). Once public, a company's valuation is no longer negotiated privately with a handful of investors; it becomes its **market capitalization** (share price × number of shares), re-priced every second by millions of buyers and sellers. A **SPAC** (special purpose acquisition company) is a shortcut to going public: an empty shell company that already trades on an exchange merges with a real company, sparing it the lengthy traditional IPO process. Finally, the **Federal Reserve** (or "the Fed") is the US central bank. When it sets interest rates near zero, money is cheap and investors happily fund risky startups; when it raises rates to fight inflation, safe investments like government bonds suddenly pay decent returns, and investors become far less patient with companies that lose money. That single shift — cheap money to expensive money — is the invisible hand behind most of what happened to these two companies between 2020 and 2025.

## 2. The Economic Backdrop: From Free Money to the Great Tightening

When COVID-19 hit in March 2020, travel collapsed almost overnight — a disaster for any company in the business of renting cars. But the US government and the Federal Reserve responded with massive stimulus: interest rates were cut to near zero and trillions of dollars flowed into the economy. Two things followed that mattered enormously for startups. First, Americans who could not fly abroad discovered road trips and local travel, so car-sharing demand roared back by the summer of 2020. Second, with safe investments yielding almost nothing, venture capital and stock market investors piled into growth companies at historically generous valuations. SPACs became a Wall Street craze, raising a record **$83 billion in 2020 and $162 billion in 2021** ([Hong Kong Lawyer](https://www.hk-lawyer.org/content/hong-kong-spacs%E2%80%99-bedazzling-debut-%E2%80%93-part-2)).

The party ended in 2022. Inflation in the US hit levels not seen in forty years, and the Federal Reserve began raising interest rates aggressively. Stock markets fell, and investors abruptly lost their appetite for unprofitable growth companies. The US IPO market effectively slammed shut: just **71 IPOs raised $7.7 billion in all of 2022, the slowest year by proceeds in Renaissance Capital's 30-plus years of records**, and SPACs were hit by "regulatory uncertainty, high redemptions, merger terminations, and poor de-SPAC returns" ([Renaissance Capital](https://www.renaissancecapital.com/review/2022USReview_Public.pdf)). Companies that had gone public via SPAC during the boom performed dismally — of the 199 companies that listed through SPAC mergers in 2021, only **11% traded above their offer price by spring 2022, with an average return of −43%** ([Renaissance Capital](https://www.renaissancecapital.com/IPO-Center/News/92125/Special-Report-SPAC-merger-returns-crumble-upending-the-2022-SPAC-market)). This is the trap Getaround walked into in December 2022 — and the trap Turo avoided by never completing its IPO.

## 3. Turo: The Company That Waited

### 3.1 2020: The COVID Scare and a Rapid Rebound

Turo entered 2020 on a high, having raised **$250 million from internet conglomerate IAC in July 2019 at a valuation "past the billion-dollar mark"** — making it a "unicorn," the industry nickname for a private startup worth at least $1 billion — with about 400,000 listed vehicles and 10 million users ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/07/17/peer-to-peer-car-sharing-marketplace-turo-raises-250m-at-over-1b-valuation-from-iac/)). The pandemic's arrival was brutal: with shelter-in-place orders stopping nearly all travel, Turo **laid off 108 employees, roughly 30% of its staff**, in the spring of 2020 ([Layoffs.fyi](https://layoffs.fyi/2020/04/21/turo-laid-off-108-employees/)). This was the company's first major valuation-relevant event of the decade — a defensive cut to preserve cash when revenue evaporated.

The recovery came faster than anyone expected. Cooped-up Americans chose driving vacations over flights, and Turo's revenue rebounded to roughly **$150 million for 2020** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/)). Unlike a traditional rental company, Turo owns no cars — hosts supply the fleet — so it could shrink costs quickly and scale back up without buying vehicles. This "asset-light" model, a key difference from Getaround's hardware-heavy approach, proved decisive throughout the period.

### 3.2 2021: Boom Times and the IPO Decision

In 2021, Turo's revenue exploded to **$469 million, a 213% increase**, powered by the road-trip boom, a shortage of rental cars from traditional agencies (which had sold off their fleets in 2020), and used-car prices so high that renting out one's own car became unusually attractive ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/)). With growth like that in a red-hot market for new listings, Turo made its move: it **filed confidentially for an IPO in August 2021 and filed its public S-1 paperwork with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) on January 10, 2022**, planning to list on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker TURO at an estimated deal size of about **$300 million** ([Access IPOs](https://accessipos.com/turo-ipo-stock/), [Renaissance Capital](https://www.renaissancecapital.com/IPO-Center/News/109393/Car-sharing-platform-Turo-withdraws-estimated-$300-million-IPO)).

In hindsight, the timing was unlucky by a matter of months. The window in which investors would pay premium prices for listings like Turo's was already closing as the Fed signaled its coming rate hikes. Turo never set an IPO price, so no public valuation was ever tested — a fact that turned out to be a blessing.

### 3.3 2022–2024: Growth Slows, but Profits Arrive

Instead of pushing into a hostile market, Turo simply waited — keeping its IPO filing alive and updating it quarterly for three years. The business kept growing, though at a decelerating pace: revenue reached **$746.6 million in 2022 (+59%), $879.7 million in 2023 (+18%), and $958 million in 2024**, with the last nine-month figure of $722 million representing only about 8% year-over-year growth ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/), [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/shrinking-ipo-pipeline-unicorn-turo-hits-pause/)). Crucially, Turo also **turned profitable in 2022, 2023, and the first nine months of 2024** — net income was about $154.7 million in 2022 before falling to $14.7 million in 2023 — a rarity among gig-economy startups and the main reason it could afford patience ([SFGate](https://www.sfgate.com/tech/article/turo-lays-off-150-workers-economic-uncertainty-20281905.php), [AInvest](https://www.ainvest.com/news/turo-strategic-layoffs-ipo-hesitation-crossroads-car-sharing-economy-2504/)).

A planned IPO roadshow was reportedly readied for October 2023 but paused amid the outbreak of war in Israel and a chilly reception for other new listings ([Access IPOs](https://accessipos.com/turo-ipo-stock/)). Because Turo stayed private, its valuation never suffered a public crash. Private-market data providers estimated it at **$1.7 billion in September 2024 (Forge) and roughly $2.2 billion in the first quarter of 2025 (Caplight)** — modest growth over its 2019 unicorn mark rather than the catastrophic repricing its rival suffered ([Forge](https://forgeglobal.com/turo_ipo/), [Access IPOs](https://accessipos.com/turo-ipo-stock/)). The trade-off: employees and early investors could not easily sell their shares, and the slower growth rate capped how high the valuation could climb.

### 3.4 2025: The IPO Dream Ends and Jobs Are Cut

Two events in early 2025 sealed the decision. On New Year's Day, vehicles rented through Turo were used in two separate attacks — a truck driven into a New Orleans crowd that **killed 15 people (including the attacker)** and injured 35, and a Tesla Cybertruck exploded outside the Trump International Hotel in Las Vegas — generating the worst publicity in the company's history just as it prepared to court public investors ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/shrinking-ipo-pipeline-unicorn-turo-hits-pause/)). On **February 13, 2025 — one day after Getaround announced its US shutdown — Turo formally withdrew its IPO**, with CEO Andre Haddad saying the board "decided now is not the right time," ending a three-year effort ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/13/turo-scraps-plans-for-an-ipo/)).

The valuation consequence was subtle but real: without an IPO, Turo's private valuation stayed anchored around the $1.7–2.2 billion range rather than being tested (and possibly marked down) by public markets. The employee consequence was more concrete. In **April 2025, Turo laid off about 150 people — 15% of a workforce of roughly 1,000** — citing "ongoing economic uncertainty" and the need to "strengthen our position for long-term growth" ([SFGate](https://www.sfgate.com/tech/article/turo-lays-off-150-workers-economic-uncertainty-20281905.php)). Workforce data provider Revelio Labs estimates Turo's headcount fell from **954 employees in 2023 to 713 by 2026, a decline of about 25%** ([Revelio Labs](https://www.reveliolabs.com/companies/turo/employees)). The pattern is clear: each time Turo's valuation outlook dimmed — the 2020 COVID crash, the 2025 IPO withdrawal — a double-digit-percentage layoff followed, but the company itself never faced a survival crisis.

## 4. Getaround: The Company That Went Public at the Worst Possible Moment

### 4.1 2020: A Near-Death Start, Then a Rescue Round

Getaround entered 2020 already struggling. In **January 2020 it laid off 150 employees**, trimming field operations and global teams — reportedly connected to troubles at its biggest backer, SoftBank, which had led a $300 million investment in 2018 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/14/getaround-raises-a-140-million-series-e-amid-rebound-in-short-distance-travel/)). Then the pandemic hit: **bookings plunged 75% in March 2020, and Getaround laid off another roughly 100 employees** and furloughed others ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2020/10/14/car-sharing-startup-getaround-raises-140-million/)). Two rounds of cuts in a single quarter left the company badly weakened before the recovery began.

Like Turo, Getaround benefited from the road-trip rebound, and by mid-2020 CEO Sam Zaid said revenue had "doubled compared to the pre-COVID baseline"; all furloughed employees were rehired by July 1. In **October 2020, Getaround raised a $140 million Series E** led by PeopleFund, bringing its total venture funding to about **$600 million** — valuation undisclosed, but the company was firmly in "VC darling" territory, ultimately raising more than **$750 million** over its life from investors including SoftBank's Vision Fund, Menlo Ventures, and Reid Hoffman's Reinvent Capital ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2020/10/14/car-sharing-startup-getaround-raises-140-million/), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/)). Revenue, however, told a quieter story: **$40.4 million in 2019, $58.7 million in 2020, and $63.1 million in 2021** — a fraction of Turo's, and barely growing ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/12/09/getaround-braves-chilly-public-markets-with-spac-combination/)). Getaround's model required installing its proprietary "Connect" hardware in every car and carried heavy insurance and support costs, so it lost money on a large share of trips.

### 4.2 2022: The SPAC Trap

With a traditional IPO unrealistic for a company of its size and losses, Getaround took the SPAC shortcut. In **May 2022 it agreed to merge with InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp. at a pro forma equity value of about $1.2 billion**, expecting between $225 million and $434 million in gross proceeds, which the company said would be enough to reach breakeven ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000119312522147020/d289503dex991.htm)). This was the single most consequential decision of the company's life — and it closed, on **December 8, 2022**, into the worst IPO market in three decades.

The damage was immediate and structural. First, SPAC shareholders exercised their right to take their money back rather than stay invested — these "redemptions" were so heavy that Getaround's own filings later admitted the deal left it with **"insufficient capital to fund its business operations and continue as a going concern"** within a year ([SEC filing via Edgar Online](https://content.edgar-online.com/ExternalLink/EDGAR/0000950170-23-070796.html?hash=e29afdd541ef278d58090953be7c514cec1b21e547d7fd2f505a177d918db4a7&dest=getr-20240710_htm)). Second, public investors instantly repriced the company: **the stock fell about 65% in its first moments of trading on December 9, 2022, and was down 76% within days** — erasing roughly $900 million of paper value almost overnight ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/09/getaround-shares-tank-after-going-public-in-spac-deal.html), [San Jose Business Journal](https://www.bizjournals.com/sanjose/news/2022/12/12/getaround-stock-plummets-after-spac-merger.html)). Where a private valuation falls only when new investors renegotiate it, a public valuation can collapse in hours — and that collapse itself becomes a business problem, as the next two years showed.

### 4.3 2023: The Downward Spiral — Delisting Warning, Layoffs, and Survival Mode

A falling stock price triggers consequences that feed on themselves, and Getaround's 2023 is a textbook example. On **January 30, 2023, the NYSE warned the company** that its stock had traded below $1 for 30 consecutive days, the first step toward delisting ([Barchart](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/15590216/getaround-announces-preliminary-financial-results-for-the-full-year-2022-and-provides-full-year-2023-outlook)). Days later, on **February 2, 2023, Getaround laid off 10% of its staff — about 42 of its 421 employees** — to cut costs ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/02/02/car-sharing-spac-getaround-lays-off-10-of-staff/)). A depressed share price made it nearly impossible to raise fresh money by selling stock, and its debt agreements made things worse: the company disclosed that a delisting would count as a "Fundamental Change" letting convertible-note holders demand immediate cash repayment, and as an "Event of Default" under its Mudrick Capital loan — meaning the stock's collapse could directly trigger a cash crisis ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1839608/000114036124032420/ny20031467x2_def14a.htm)).

Operationally, the company kept losing enormous sums relative to its size: net losses of **$136.1 million in 2022 and $113.9 million in 2023** against revenue that grew to only **$72.7 million in 2023** ([Getaround FY2023 results](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/getaround-reports-full-year-2023-results-2024-03-28)). In **May 2023 it bought the assets of HyreCar**, a car-sharing service for gig-economy drivers, for just **$9.45 million** — a cheap attempt to buy growth ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/)). Auditors' "going concern" language, repeated in filings, formally warned that the company might not survive another year.

### 4.4 2024: A Third of the Workforce Goes, Then the NYSE Ejection

On **February 7, 2024, Getaround cut approximately 30% of its North American workforce**, expecting to save only about $7 million a year — a telling sign of how small the company had already become — while paying up to $1 million in restructuring costs ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240207550685/en/Getaround-Announces-Restructuring-Plan-to-Accelerate-the-Path-to-Profitability)). This was the third major layoff in four years, and each round corresponded directly to a new low in the company's valuation and cash position. Employees reported on Blind that equity had become "worthless" and that layoffs had been "poorly executed," illustrating how a collapsed stock price destroys the compensation that startup workers were promised ([Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/company/Getaround/reviews)).

In **July 2024 the NYSE suspended trading in Getaround's stock and began formal delisting**, because the company's average market capitalization had fallen below the exchange's $15 million minimum — it stood at roughly **$12 million**, down from $1.2 billion at the merger just nineteen months earlier ([Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/company-news/getaround-to-appeal-nyse-delisting-trades-on-otc-markets-93CH-3513784), [SEC 8-K](https://content.edgar-online.com/ExternalLink/EDGAR/0000950170-24-082596.html?hash=46242d2b3fa2bc0e769489529e95bef84ef74d4b8b49104756a73ee6c5a04980&dest=getr-20240710_htm)). The shares moved to the over-the-counter (OTC) market, a backwater for small and distressed stocks. Leadership churned too: founder Sam Zaid was replaced, and the CFO departed in mid-2024 as the stock sat **down ~99% from its SPAC debut** ([The Bear Cave](https://thebearcave.substack.com/p/the-bear-cave-231)).

### 4.5 2025: Total US Shutdown

The end came quickly. On **February 7, 2025, Getaround's board approved an "orderly wind-down" of its entire US business**, including HyreCar, with a reduction-in-force of **"substantially all" US employees**, most departing on February 14; severance costs were estimated at just $1.5–2 million, again underscoring how few workers remained ([SEC 8-K via OTC Markets](https://www.otcmarkets.com/filing/html?id=18179651&guid=Skt-kH__r4wfJth)). Customers were told to return rental cars within about a day because the company was "at risk of no longer being able to provide liability insurance coverage" — a chaotic end for a company once backed by three-quarters of a billion dollars ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/12/getaround-abruptly-shuts-down-us-car-sharing-operations/)). Interim CEO AJ Lee cited "an ongoing lack of liquidity" — in plain terms, it ran out of cash and could find no one willing to lend or invest more, because a ~99% stock collapse had destroyed every financing channel.

By the shutdown, Getaround's shares traded at about **two cents, and its entire market capitalization was roughly $2 million** — about 0.2% of its $1.2 billion SPAC valuation twenty-six months earlier ([SFGate via Owler](https://www.owler.com/company/getaround), [Bitget](https://www.bitget.com/wiki/getr-stock)). The European business (built on the 2019 Drivy acquisition) survived independently in six countries and was later merged with Danish peer-to-peer platform GoMore in a deal announced in 2025, while the US parent company moved toward formal dissolution ([Owler](https://www.owler.com/company/getaround), [Startups Center](https://startups.center/zh/w/getaround-2025-us-wind-down-dissolution)). For employees, the scorecard was stark: 150 jobs cut in January 2020, ~100 more in March 2020, 10% in February 2023, 30% of North American staff in February 2024, and effectively all remaining US jobs in February 2025.

The divergence comes down to three factors, each traceable in the record above. First, **scale and unit economics**: Turo's asset-light marketplace reached nearly $1 billion in revenue and real profits, so it could fund itself; Getaround's hardware-and-insurance-heavy model produced roughly one-thirteenth the revenue and persistent nine-figure losses, making it permanently dependent on new capital. Second, **the financing choice**: Turo stayed private when markets turned, so its valuation was never marked down in public and its cash was never hostage to daily share-price moves; Getaround's SPAC both delivered far less cash than promised (due to redemptions) and exposed the company to a self-reinforcing spiral in which a falling stock triggered debt defaults, delisting, and finally an inability to raise any money at all. Third, **timing luck**: Getaround's deal closed in December 2022, arguably the single worst month to debut in a decade, while Turo's three-year delay — frustrating as it was for employees holding shares — meant it never had to discover what public investors thought it was worth in a bear market.

## 6. What a Lay Reader Should Take Away

The headline lesson is that **a company's valuation is an opinion about the future, and opinions change violently when the cost of money changes**. Between 2020 and 2021, near-zero interest rates made investors generous: both companies were treated as billion-dollar successes. When the Fed raised rates in 2022–2023, investors began demanding actual profits — and only Turo had any. Getaround's story also shows how **a falling valuation can itself cause the fall to continue**: the stock decline triggered delisting, which threatened to trigger debt repayment, which scared away the new investors the company needed to survive. Economists call this a liquidity death spiral, and it is why Getaround's employee count went from hundreds of Americans to nearly zero in three years while Turo, which cut jobs twice (30% in 2020, 15% in 2025), still employed hundreds of people and generated nearly a billion dollars in annual revenue at the end of 2025.

Finally, the comparison illustrates why "going public" is not automatically a success. For employees, a crashed public stock means worthless equity and layoffs; for a fragile business, staying private longer can be the difference between life and death. Turo's withdrawn IPO was widely reported as a failure — but measured against the alternative actually experienced by its closest competitor, it looks more like a narrow escape. Both companies began the decade as peers; only one ended it as a going concern in its home market.
