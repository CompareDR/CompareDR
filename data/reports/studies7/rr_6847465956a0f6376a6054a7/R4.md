## Comparative Framework, Pilot Designs, and Methodology

### UBI definition, typology, and how each pilot deviates from the ideal type

The Basic Income Earth Network (BIEN), the principal global advocacy and research network for the concept, defines a basic income as "a periodic cash payment unconditionally delivered to all on an individual basis, without means-test or work requirement" ([BIEN, "About basic income"](https://basicincome.org/about-basic-income/)). The Stanford Basic Income Lab's umbrella review (Hasdell 2020) operationalizes this into five definitional features: **universal** (paid to every individual, not targeted), **unconditional** (no conditions or sanctions, given to employed and unemployed alike), **cash** (paid in cash, not in-kind), **individual** (paid per individual, not per household), and **periodic/regular** (recurring, not a one-time grant) ([Hasdell 2020, Stanford BIL, pp. 4–5](https://basicincome.stanford.edu/uploads/Umbrella%20Review%20BI_final.pdf)). Critically, Hasdell (2020) confirms that "a fully universal and unconditional basic income has never been implemented at scale" and that evidence must be approximated from "programs, policies and experiments that share features of a UBI" ([Hasdell 2020, pp. 2–3](https://basicincome.stanford.edu/uploads/Umbrella%20Review%20BI_final.pdf)). The overview below therefore maps how four principal developed-country pilots—Finland (2017–2018), Ontario (2017–2019), Stockton SEED (2019–2021), and Manitoba Mincome (1974–1979, retained as a precedent)—satisfy or deviate from the ideal type, and establishes the evaluative dimensions used throughout this report.

#### Pilot deviations from the BIEN ideal type

**Finland — partial, unemployment-targeted basic income.** The Finnish experiment (FBIE) was the first non-voluntary, nationwide randomized basic income trial in an advanced economy ([Salokangas & Simanainen 2026, *Beyond the Nordic Welfare State*, Edward Elgar](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml)). It targeted 2,000 individuals aged 25–58 who were receiving basic daily allowance or labour market support in November 2016, replacing those conditional unemployment benefits with a tax-exempt €560/month payment that was not affected by work income ([Intereconomics 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). As Heikki Hiilamo noted in commentary: "The Finnish experiment was about partial basic income targeting able-bodied people without work, it was not about universal basic income" ([Bruegel 2019](https://www.bruegel.org/blog-post/universal-basic-income-and-finnish-experiment)). The Finnish design was statutory—participation was mandatory, not voluntary—and randomized at the individual level with approximately 173,000–175,000 control-group recipients ([Salokangas & Simanainen 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml); [Intereconomics 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). However, it deviates from BIEN universality in the most consequential way: the treatment population was restricted to existing unemployment-benefit recipients, so it cannot speak to labor-supply or well-being effects on the employed population, nor to the dynamism that a universal floor might create across the entire labor market. For a synthesis of the experiment's overall impact profile on employment, health, and wellbeing outcomes, see S2.1 below, which integrates the STM (2020) final report, the Hämäläinen (2025) registry-based health study, and the Salokangas & Simanainen (2026) Edward Elgar chapter.

**Ontario — NIT-form guaranteed basic income with 50% earnings clawback.** The Ontario Basic Income Pilot (launched April 2017 under Liberal Premier Kathleen Wynne) was designed as a partial guaranteed basic income (GBI) paying $16,989/year to single individuals and $24,027/year to couples, set at 75% of Statistics Canada's Low-Income Measure ([Canada.ca QP Note, GouldJan2022-011](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/esdc-edsc,GouldJan2022-011)). Benefits were reduced by 50% of earned income, an explicit negative-income-tax (NIT) phase-out structure. As Michael Mendelson (2021) argues, "more precisely, it was a Negative Income Tax, because it made payments to households inversely related to household income, rather than making payments to all individuals regardless of income" ([Maytree 2021](https://maytree.com/publications/critical-lessons-from-ontario-about-how-to-set-up-a-basic-income-experiment/)). The 50% clawback means that a single recipient earning $17,000 would see benefits reduced to zero—a marginal effective tax rate of 50% on earnings up to the breakeven point, which creates a classic welfare-trap disincentive structure fundamentally different from Finland's no-clawback top-up. Ontario's design was also household-based (violating BIEN's individual criterion), retrospective (using prior-year tax income rather than current monthly income), and non-randomized in practice (enrollment difficulties undermined the intended RCT structure) ([Maytree 2021](https://maytree.com/publications/critical-lessons-from-ontario-about-how-to-set-up-a-basic-income-experiment/)). The pilot was cancelled in July 2018 by the incoming Progressive Conservative government under Doug Ford, before any independent evaluation data could be collected—a fact that itself constitutes evidence on the political-durability dimension (explored in S3.3).

**Stockton SEED — guaranteed income, not universal.** The Stockton Economic Empowerment Demonstration (SEED), launched in February 2019 by then-Mayor Michael Tubbs, gave a stratified random sample of 131 residents (125 post-attrition) in census tracts at or below Stockton's area median income ($46,033) $500/month for 24 months with "no strings attached and no work requirements" ([SEED official site](https://www.stocktondemonstration.org/); [Ménder/Rizvi 2024, PMC11180702](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). SEED's nomenclature is significant: U.S. pilots overwhelmingly use the label "guaranteed income" (GI) rather than "universal basic income" (UBI) precisely because they are *targeted* to low-income populations. The Economic Security Project, a primary U.S. funder, distinguishes the terms: "While Universal Basic Income (UBI) is a regular cash payment to all members of a community, guaranteed income could be targeted or universal" ([Economic Security Project, "Guaranteed Income: A Primer for Funders"](https://economicsecurityproject.org/resource/funder-primer/)). Stockton thus fails BIEN's universality criterion by design—it was never intended to be universal. The stratification across 42 census tracts and the $500/month amount (roughly 30% of mean household income in eligible tracts) also situate it as a poverty-alleviation intervention rather than a test of an economy-wide floor. SEED used individual-level random assignment but drew the sample from households below an area-median-income threshold, meaning it can speak to individual-level outcomes but not to community-wide general-equilibrium effects.

**Manitoba Mincome — saturating NIT (Dauphin site, 1974–1979).** The Mincome experiment is retained in this report as a pre-2015 precedent anchor. Its Dauphin, Manitoba site functioned as a "saturation site" in which all town residents were eligible for guaranteed annual income payments, with a 50% declining (clawback) rate—for every dollar of employment income, the family Mincome payment was cut by $0.50 ([humanrights.ca, 2022](https://humanrights.ca/story/manitobas-mincome-experiment); [Calnitsky 2016, *Cultural Sociology*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26890446/)). Although Dauphin's saturation design more closely approximated a universal program than any other North American pilot—allowing assessment of community-wide as well as individual effects—only about one-third of Dauphin families qualified for stipends at any given point due to the income test ([Forget 2011, *Canadian Public Policy*](https://utppublishing.com/doi/10.3138/cpp.37.3.283)). Mincome thus deviates from BIEN universality through both its NIT clawback structure and its means-tested eligibility threshold, but it remains historically important as the only North American saturation-site NIT experiment.

#### "Guaranteed income" vs. "UBI" labeling distinction

The terminological divide between "guaranteed income" (GI), used for U.S. pilots like Stockton, and "basic income" (or "guaranteed basic income"), used for Finland and Ontario, reflects both design reality and political framing. U.S. pilots deliberately use GI to signal that they are *not* universal and are targeted to demonstrably needy populations—a strategic choice to build political support by emphasizing poverty alleviation rather than universal entitlement ([Economic Security Project](https://economicsecurityproject.org/resource/funder-primer/)). Finland's government used "basic income" (tulovero/perustulo) but explicitly designed a *partial* model that left earnings-related benefits intact and targeted only the unemployed, effectively testing whether unconditional receipt could replace conditional admin without undermining work incentives ([Intereconomics 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). Ontario's government used "basic income" in public-facing materials but structured the pilot as a negative income tax—a design explicitly acknowledged by internal advisors as NIT architecture ([Maytree 2021](https://maytree.com/publications/critical-lessons-from-ontario-about-how-to-set-up-a-basic-income-experiment/)). The labeling distinction is thus analytically meaningful: no tested program in any of the three countries was a full BIEN-compliant UBI, and the gap between ideal and realized design is largest on the universality criterion for all four pilots.

#### NIT vs. pure UBI: incentive-structure implications

The distinction between Ontario's 50%-clawback NIT and Finland's no-clawback top-up is substantively important for interpreting labor-supply effects. Under a pure UBI, an additional dollar earned always adds to disposable income; under Ontario's NIT, each dollar earned reduced the benefit by $0.50, imposing a 50% effective marginal tax rate up to the breakeven point ($34,000 for a single recipient earning against a $17,000 guarantee). This creates a classic substitution effect reducing the incentive to earn additional income—precisely the welfare-trap mechanism that UBI advocates argue a universal, no-clawback transfer would eliminate ([Bruegel 2019](https://www.bruegel.org/blog-post/universal-basic-income-and-finnish-experiment); [Maytree 2021](https://maytree.com/publications/critical-lessons-from-ontario-about-how-to-set-up-a-basic-income-experiment/)). Finland's design, by contrast, removed the 100% effective marginal tax rate inherent in means-tested unemployment benefits (where earning any income could eliminate the benefit entirely) and replaced it with a 0% clawback on the €560/month payment. This makes Finland's incentive structure closer to a pure UBI *for the recipient population*—but because the target population was exclusively unemployed, the experiment cannot reveal how a universal floor would affect employed workers' labor-supply decisions, reservation wages, or job-lock dynamics at scale.

#### Establishing evaluative dimensions

Five evaluative dimensions structure the comparative analysis throughout this report. The design deviations noted above determine which pilots can speak to which dimensions:

1. **Labor-supply / employment effect** — Addressable by all four pilots, but with critical scope limits: Finland tested only the unemployed, Ontario's cancellation precludes definitive results, Stockton's small-N RCT detected a pre-COVID increase in full-time employment that did not survive the full observation window, and the 1970s NIT experiments captured modest reductions concentrated among secondary earners and youth.
2. **Health & subjective well-being** — Finland's survey and registry data (including Hämäläinen 2025) provide the strongest evidence; Ontario's self-reported improvements are suggestive but not peer-reviewed; Stockton used validated clinical scales (Kessler-10, SF-36).
3. **Poverty & material insecurity** — Ontario and Stockton directly targeted low-income populations with measurable improvements in food security and income volatility; Finland's partial top-up produced more modest material-security gains given its comprehensive baseline welfare state.
4. **Stigma & bureaucracy** — Finland's unconditional design reduced bureaucratic encounters and institutional distrust; Ontario's NIT structure retained income testing and retrospective calculations; Stockton's no-strings design eliminated conditionality entirely for participants.
5. **Fiscal cost & political durability** — Only Finland completed its planned duration. Ontario's mid-run cancellation by a successor government constitutes direct evidence of political vulnerability. The PBO's repeated federal cost analyses (2021, 2025) signal sustained Canadian institutional interest despite provincial rejection (see S3.2). No U.S. pilot has produced a national-scale fiscal projection comparable to the PBO's work.

### Comparative design parameters, welfare-regime context, and intervention vectors

The three primary pilots—Finland's nationwide basic income experiment, Ontario's Basic Income Pilot (OBIP), and Stockton's Economic Empowerment Demonstration (SEED)—differ so substantially in design that they cannot be read as replications of a single policy intervention. Each targets a distinct population, distributes different benefit amounts under different conditionality regimes, and operates within a distinct welfare-state architecture. These differences are not incidental; they shape what each pilot can measure and what "long-term consequence" means in each setting.

The €560 monthly benefit in Finland was tax-exempt and deducted from existing basic social benefits, meaning that for participants who remained unemployed, there was no net change in income—the experiment tested whether *unconditionality itself* modified behavior relative to the existing conditional unemployment-benefit system. Crucially, because the benefit was not reduced by work income, participants who found employment experienced a significant increase in disposable income, creating a strong earnings incentive. This design was explicitly a *partial* basic income: it was not universal, reaching only those already receiving unemployment benefits, and it left intact certain other benefits and housing allowances ([Intereconomics, 2017](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)).

Ontario's pilot, by contrast, operated as a **negative income tax (NIT)**, not a pure basic income. A single recipient earning no income would receive $16,989 CAD per year—approximately 75% of Statistics Canada's Low-Income Measure (LIM)—but for every dollar of earned income, the benefit was clawed back by 50 cents. The guarantee was reduced to zero at $33,978 in earnings. This structure means that a recipient earning $10,000 would receive $11,989 ($16,989 − $5,000), for a total income of $21,989—a materially different incentive profile from Finland's zero-marginal-clawback design ([Canada.ca QP Note, 2022](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/esdc-edsc,GouldJan2022-011); [PBS NewsHour, 2018](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/economy/making-sense/ontario-is-canceling-its-basic-income-experiment)). The 50% phase-out rate is analytically significant: it preserves a stronger work incentive than Finland's full displacement of conditional benefits would, but it also introduces a marginal effective tax rate that does not exist in a true UBI. Ontario's use of the *retrospective tax system* to calculate benefits—income was assessed annually based on the prior year's tax return rather than current-month earnings—added another layer of administrative complexity, meaning benefit payments could be "wildly out of sync with household needs in any given month" ([Maytree, 2021](https://maytree.com/publications/critical-lessons-from-ontario-about-how-to-set-up-a-basic-income-experiment/)).

Stockton's SEED adopted the simplest transfer structure: $500 per month for 24 months, completely unconditional with no phase-out, no work requirements, and no reduction based on earnings. The benefit was framed not as a replacement for social assistance (as in Finland and Ontario) but as an *add-on* cash transfer designed to reduce income volatility among low-income households. The stratified random sample across 42 census tracts at or below the area median income of $46,033 aimed to capture a broader low-income population rather than the explicitly unemployed ([West et al., 2023, *Journal of Urban Health*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0)). The COVID-19 pandemic overlapped the experiment's second year, simultaneously threatening internal validity (as an external shock) and providing a real-world stress test of the program's resilience effects.

#### Welfare-regime context: Esping-Andersen typology and what each pilot can measure

The comparison set is justified by Esping-Andersen's welfare-regime typology, which classifies advanced capitalist countries along a continuum of decommodification and universalism. Finland represents the **social-democratic/Nordic universal welfare** regime, characterized by generous, universal benefits that decommodify labor and redistribute across the full population. Canada (and Ontario) represents the **liberal welfare** regime, where state provision is more modest, benefits are often means-tested and residual, and the market plays a larger role in welfare provision. The United States represents the **residual-welfare** regime, with minimal state provision, strict entitlement criteria, and a heavy reliance on private and market-based welfare ([Bambra, 2007, *Social Science & Medicine*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2465657/); [Eikemo et al., 2008](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/file/1562636/1/Published%20Journal%20Article)).

This architectural difference is not merely contextual—it determines what each pilot can *measure* and what a "long-term consequence" means in each setting. In Finland's social-democratic regime, the existing safety net is comprehensive: unemployment benefits, healthcare, education, and social assistance are guaranteed rights. A €560 top-up for the unemployed therefore tests a *marginal* effect on top of an already-generous baseline. Whether the same unconditional payment would produce comparable well-being gains in a context with thinner supports (Stockton) or with the implicit conditions embedded in a 50% phase-out (Ontario) is an empirical question the pilots cannot jointly answer. Finland's design cannot speak to the question of whether UBI could *replace* existing welfare architecture, because it was layered on top of it.

In Ontario's liberal regime, the pilot was explicitly designed to test whether a guaranteed income could *replace* existing social assistance—participants were required to opt out of certain provincial benefits to participate. This makes Ontario's design more directly relevant to the question of welfare-state restructuring, but the cancellation of the pilot in July 2018 by the incoming Progressive Conservative government of Doug Ford (who had replaced Premier Kathleen Wynne's Liberal government just weeks earlier) truncated the experiment after roughly one year, depriving researchers of outcome data on the very design question the pilot was most suited to answer ([PBS NewsHour, 2018](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/economy/making-sense/ontario-is-canceling-its-basic-income-experiment)).

In the U.S. residual-welfare regime, SEED's design fills a gaping hole in the safety net where no comparable federal program exists. The $500/month transfer represents a much larger proportional increase in household income for low-income Stockton recipients than €560 does for Finnish recipients. This means SEED's measured effects on health, income volatility, and agency may be larger in magnitude than Finland's—not because the policy lever is more effective, but because the baseline security is lower. The pre-existing welfare architecture thus shapes the *interpretability* of observed effects: a larger effect size in Stockton reflects the regime's thinner safety net, not necessarily a superior policy design.

#### Methodological superiority of Finland's nationwide statutory design

Finland's experiment was the first nationwide, statutory, randomized field experiment in a developed country. Its design features conferred significant methodological advantages over Ontario and Stockton:

1. **Mandatory, non-volunteer participation.** Because recipients were randomly selected from the unemployment-benefit registry and participation was compulsory, Finland avoided the selection bias that affects voluntary programs like Stockton (where less-needier or more-organized individuals may self-select) and Ontario (where recruitment difficulties led the government to rely on social-agency referrals rather than randomization) ([Finland Toolbox, 2018](https://toolbox.finland.fi/life-society/finlands-basic-income-experiment-2017-2018/); [Maytree, 2021](https://maytree.com/publications/critical-lessons-from-ontario-about-how-to-set-up-a-basic-income-experiment/)).

2. **Massive control group.** Finland's control group of approximately 173,000 individuals—all unemployment-benefit recipients not selected for treatment—provided enormous statistical power for register-based analyses. The early register study could draw on "robust" comparative data across a near-universe of eligible recipients ([European Commission ESPN, 2019](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)). By contrast, Stockton's control group of 200 individuals limited statistical power, and Ontario lacked a formal control group entirely once the design departed from its intended randomization.

3. **Nationwide scope.** Finland's experiment covered the entire country, eliminating site-specific confounds (local labor markets, municipal policies) that affect site-specific pilots in Ontario (four communities) and Stockton (one city).

However, Finland's design also carried limitations: the partial, unemployment-targeted eligibility means the experiment cannot speak to effects on the employed population, and the benefit level of €560 was too low to function as a true UBI—it was a top-up, not a replacement income. As the European Commission's summary noted, "it was limited to employed people (overly narrow sampling and no possibility to study whether employed people receiving the BI would work less)" ([ESPN, 2019](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)).

#### Are these pilots testing the same policy lever?

The three pilots test structurally different instruments, and this has direct implications for cross-pilot comparison and national-scale projection:

- **Finland** tests an *unconditional, payroll top-up* layered on existing benefits with zero marginal clawback on earnings. The intervention vector is unconditionality itself, holding benefit level roughly constant for the unemployed.
- **Ontario** tests a *negative income tax* with a 50% phase-out rate. The intervention vector is the *structure* of the guarantee—its generosity relative to existing welfare and its incentive profile at the margin of earned income.
- **Stockton** tests an *add-on unconditional cash transfer* in the absence of a comparable safety net. The intervention vector is the *level* of unconditional income, not the removal of conditionality.

These differences mean that convergent findings (e.g., improvements in mental well-being) across all three pilots are arguably more robust than they appear, since they emerge under three quite different incentive regimes. Conversely, divergent employment findings—from Finland's no aggregate effect to Stockton's increase in full-time employment—reflect the fact that the pilots are manipulating different policy parameters and operating on different populations with different baseline security.

#### From pilot parameters to national-scale cost

The Parliamentary Budget Officer of Canada's repeated national-scale cost analyses (2021 and 2025) directly import Ontario's pilot parameters—the $16,989/$24,027 benefit levels pegged to 75% of LIM and the 50% phase-out rate—to project national GBI costs, poverty reductions, and phase-out scenarios; these fiscal analyses are detailed in S3.2. The transferability of the Ontario-specific 50% phase-out to other welfare regimes (e.g., Finland's zero-clawback design) remains an open question.

#### Summary of comparability

The three pilots collectively approximate—but do not fully instantiate—different dimensions of a UBI policy. Finland's experiment isolates the effect of *unconditionality* for the unemployed in a universal-welfare context; Ontario tests a *restructuring* of income support from conditional to partially unconditional through an NIT mechanism; and Stockton tests the effect of *additional unconditional income* in a residual-welfare context with minimal safety net. Cross-pilot synthesis must therefore distinguish convergent findings that are robust across structurally different instruments from divergent findings that reflect the pilots' non-equivalent design parameters. The welfare-regime backdrop is not a nuisance variable to control for; it is the structural condition that determines what each pilot's evidence can and cannot support for national-scale projection.

### Methodological strengths, limitations, and external-validity concerns

The three primary pilots — Finland (2017–2018), Ontario (2017–2019), and Stockton SEED (2019–2021) — differ markedly in evaluation design, and these differences condition what each can credibly claim about UBI's effectiveness and long-term consequences. No single pilot resolves the core policy question; each illuminates a subset of the relevant causal parameters.

#### Finland: register-based evaluation with survey limitations and asymmetric contamination

The Finnish experiment was evaluated through a three-pronged design: register-based analysis of employment and earnings (led by the Labour Institute for Economic Research and VATT), a phone survey of recipients and controls (Kela), and in-depth interviews with 81 recipients (University of Helsinki). The final government report, *Suomen perustulokokeilun arviointi* (STM 2020:15), was published in May 2020 and authored by Olli Kangas (Academy of Finland), Signe Jauhiainen, Miska Simanainen (Kela), and Minna Ylikäppä (Kela). This report is an internal government publication — rigorous in its register-based methodology but not formally peer-reviewed. The preliminary findings (February 2019), based on one year of register data, showed no aggregate employment effect; the final report, incorporating a second year of registry data and a longer observation window (November 2017–October 2018), resolved this into a small positive effect of approximately **+6 additional days employed over the reference period**, with recipients employed 78 days on average. Subgroup analysis revealed that families with children experienced employment gains in both years, though the small subgroup sizes and numerous statistical tests rendered these results uncertain ([STM 2020 press release, 6 May 2020](https://stm.fi/en/-/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi); [Hämäläinen & Verho, CESifo Working Paper 9875, 2022](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp9875.pdf)).

**Survey response rate.** The survey component suffered a low response rate of **23%** — 31% among basic-income recipients and 20% among controls. Although the evaluation team described this as "typical for surveys" in the Finnish context, it raises concerns about selection bias: respondents who felt more positively about the program may have been disproportionately represented, potentially inflating wellbeing estimates. The register-based employment analysis, which drew on complete administrative records rather than self-reported survey data, provided a critical complement precisely because it was not subject to non-response bias; the registry-linked approach has been extended to health outcomes as detailed in S2.1.

**2018 activation-model contamination.** In 2018, the Finnish government introduced an "activation model" reform that tightened entitlement criteria for regular unemployment benefits. This reform affected both the treatment and control groups but did so **asymmetrically**: control-group recipients faced stricter conditions on their existing benefits, while treatment-group recipients received the unconditional €560 regardless. The asymmetric contamination means that the small positive employment effect observed in the second year cannot be cleanly attributed to the basic income itself; the activation model may have differentially pushed control-group members toward employment, thereby narrowing the measured treatment effect. The contamination and researchers' mitigation strategies — including restricting the primary analysis to the first year — remain a fundamental threat to causal identification of the second-year treatment effect and are discussed in detail in S2.1.

**Attrition and population targeting.** The experiment enrolled 2,000 randomly selected unemployment-benefit recipients, with a control group of comparable benefit recipients — participation was statutory, not voluntary, which strengthened internal relative to voluntary pilot designs. However, the target population (unemployed individuals in a Nordic welfare state) limits generalizability to other labor-market contexts and to a truly universal UBI that would include employed and non-working populations. The CESifo working paper by Hämäläinen and Verho (2022) further notes that the experiment was "compromised and scaled down" during planning, losing the multi-arm treatment structure that would have enabled labor-supply elasticity estimation; the final design retained randomization and compulsory participation but sacrificed the variation needed for structural inference ([CESifo WP 9875](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp9875.pdf)).

#### Ontario: absence of RCT, cancellation as external shock, and fragmentary evidence

The Ontario Basic Income Pilot lacked a formal randomized controlled trial design. Evidence collection relied primarily on a survey of **424 participants** conducted by the Basic Income Canada Network (BICN), an advocacy organization, and published in February 2019 before the pilot's cancellation. This survey was not peer-reviewed and was administered by an organization with a policy interest in positive findings, raising concerns about both selection effects and reporting bias. The survey's detailed self-reported health and participation findings — including improvements in physical activity, mental health, and employment/education capacity — are discussed in S2.2 ([BICN survey, cited in PMC7249249](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7249249/self)). Independent systematic reviews have since synthesized this evidence: Rizvi et al. (2024), a Campbell systematic review published in *PMC* (PMC11180702), synthesized 27 studies from high-income countries on GBI interventions and poverty-related outcomes, providing the most rigorous cross-pilot assessment though limited by the observational nature of most underlying studies ([Rizvi et al. 2024, PMC11180702](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)).

**Cancellation as methodological blow.** The Ontario pilot was cancelled in July 2018 by the incoming Progressive Conservative government of Doug Ford, less than 18 months after launch, with formal termination in March 2019. This cancellation terminated data collection before the scheduled three-year period, eliminating the possibility of medium-term follow-up and introducing systematic attrition bias: participants who were most vulnerable to the program's withdrawal may have dropped out of remaining data collection efforts. The CMAJ editorial on the cancellation characterized it as a methodological blow to Canadian social-science research ([CMAJ 2018;190(34):E1024](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/190/34/e1024)). The Bristol University Press article in the *Journal of Poverty and Social Justice* framed the cancellation through the lens of "refusal power" — the ability of a new government to reverse a predecessor's policy commitment — and "policy credibility," arguing that the activation of refusal power undermined the conditions for robust policy evaluation ([Bristol UP, JPSJ 34(2):109, 2026](https://bristoluniversitypressdigital.com/abstract/journals/jpsj/34/2/article-p109.xml)). The political dimensions of the cancellation are analyzed in S3.3.

**Data preservation and secondary analysis.** In response to the cancellation, researchers at Carleton University's Centre for the Study of the Public Sector (CSPSC) undertook efforts to archive participant data and secondary analyses. Qualitative analyses were also conducted by PROOF at the University of Toronto, including work by Catherine Mah, investigating the pilot's dimensions as a health intervention. The scoping review by Gibson (2024) in *ScienceDirect* further assessed the causal strength of the quasi-experimental evidence available from Canada, concluding that the evidence base remains fragmentary and insufficient for definitive causal claims about GBI effects on health ([Gibson 2024, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0955395924002585)). A critical limitation is that Ontario's NIT-form design (50% earnings phase-out) differed structurally from Finland's unconditional top-up, meaning the participants faced different incentive structures and the results are not directly comparable across pilots.

#### Stockton SEED: small-N RCT with COVID-19 as both confound and resilience test

The Stockton Economic Empowerment Demonstration (SEED) employed a **mixed-methods proximal RCT** — the strongest research design of the three primary pilots. At baseline, 131 individuals were assigned to treatment and 200 to control; post-attrition, approximately 125 treatment participants remained ([PMC10160253; West et al. 2023, *Journal of Urban Health*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0)). The peer-reviewed article by West et al. (2023) in the *Journal of Urban Health* (DOI: 10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0) used ANCOVA estimation at multiple time points, with staging designed to isolate pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-withdrawal effects. Quantitative data were collected at 6-month intervals beginning 3 months prior to allocation, with qualitative interviews across 3 stages integrating 105 interviews ([West et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0)).

**COVID-19 as confound and resilience test.** The COVID-19 pandemic began in March 2020, overlapping with year 2 of the intervention. West et al. explicitly noted that "the onset of the pandemic directed analytic decision-making, as the history effect threatened both the internal and external validity of the experiment" ([West et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0)). The pandemic was a **confound**: it represented an exogenous macroeconomic shock that could have differentially affected treatment and control groups independently of the intervention. But it also constituted a **unique resilience test**: the guaranteed income's effect on recipients' ability to weather pandemic-related financial volatility became a measurable outcome in itself. The researchers addressed the confound by segmenting the analysis into pre-pandemic (baseline to Wave 4, February 2020), pandemic (Waves 5–6, August 2020–February 2021), and post-withdrawal (Wave 7, August 2021) periods. This segmentation allowed them to test whether the treatment effect was robust to the pandemic shock — an approach that turned a threat to internal validity into an opportunity to assess external realism, though at the cost of reduced statistical power in each segment.

**Sample size and power limitations.** The small N (131 treatment at baseline; ~125 post-attrition) limited the minimum detectable effect size to *f* = 0.30 at 0.80 power — a moderate-to-large effect. As the authors acknowledged, "both the small sample size allowed due to the cost of the intervention, as well as a lack of priors on effect sizes of guaranteed income interventions on various outcomes limits specificity of the power analysis" ([West et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0)). This means the study was adequately powered to detect only substantively meaningful effects; null findings on some outcomes should be interpreted as "imprecisely estimated" rather than evidence of no effect. The Ghuman (2022) policy review in PMC (PMC9469043) further examined SEED's design, noting the implications of the "benefits cliff" for participants and the multi-tiered approach to mitigating means-tested benefit loss — an ethical and methodological dimension that Baker et al. (2020) analyzed in detail ([Baker et al. 2020, PMC7142678](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7142678/); [Ghuman 2022, PMC9469043](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9469043/)).

**Selection and external validity.** SEED used stratified random sampling from census tracts at or below Stockton's area median income ($46,033), drawing from 42 census tracts with 4,200 households mailed. This targeted sampling to low-income neighborhoods means the results cannot be generalized to higher-income or non-urban populations. The 24-month duration, while longer than many U.S. pilots, is still insufficient to observe long-run labor-supply adjustments, human capital investment, or intergenerational effects.

The Finnish government report and the Ontario BICN survey sit at the lowest tier of the evidence hierarchy: rigorous in design for Finland (register-based, complete-population data) but lacking peer review, and limited for Ontario (self-reported, advocacy-administered) in both peer-review status and methodological strength. The Hämäläinen (2025) JPubE article and the West et al. (2023) *Journal of Urban Health* article represent the strongest individual-study evidence, while the Rizvi et al. (2024) Campbell systematic review provides the most rigorous cross-pilot synthesis available.

**Permanent vs. transient treatment effects.** Because all three pilots were time-limited, recipients may have treated the transfers as transient rather than permanent, complicating inference about long-run labor-supply responses. The distinction between permanent-income and transitional effects — including how time-expectancy alters reservation-wage and job-search behavior — is analyzed in S3.1.

**The Hoynes–Rothstein external-validity critique.** Hoynes and Rothstein (2019) argued that "ongoing UBI pilot studies will do little to resolve the major outstanding questions" about a nationwide UBI, identifying scale differences (pilots reach 1–2% of the population), targeting of marginal populations, short duration, interaction with existing safety nets, and financing complexity as interconnected obstacles ([Hoynes & Rothstein 2019, *Annual Review of Economics* 11:929–958](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25538)). Their broader argument — that labor-supply elasticity estimates from the wider literature may be more useful than pilot impact estimates for predicting national-scale effects — directly informs the S2/S3 boundary and is detailed in S3.1.

**Selection bias and Hawthorne effects.** All three pilots selected populations at the margins of the labor market: unemployed individuals in Finland, low-income households in Ontario and Stockton. Selection bias operates in two directions. First, marginal populations may be more responsive to income transfers (if their reservation wages are more elastic) or less responsive (if they face structural barriers to employment that cash alone cannot resolve). Second, Hawthorne effects — behavioral modification from the awareness of being studied — may inflate observed positive effects if participants alter behavior to meet perceived researcher expectations. Finland's statutory, non-voluntary design mitigates selection bias in enrollment but does not eliminate it in program effects: the experiment tested what a basic income does for unemployed people, not what it would do for a universal population. Ontario's and Stockton's voluntary recruitment from targeted populations introduces additional selection: individuals who responded to recruitment mailers may differ systematically from non-respondents in ways correlated with outcomes. The Ontario BICN survey, conducted by an advocacy organization, compounded this by potentially over-representing participants disproportionately engaged with the pilot's mission.

**Unresolvable tensions.** The methodological landscape reveals a fundamental tension: the pilots generating the strongest causal evidence (Finland's randomized statutory design; Stockton's RCT) test a narrower policy lever than the UBI proposals they are used to evaluate. Finland tested a partial basic income for unemployed people; Stockton tested a guaranteed income for low-income neighborhoods. To the extent that a national UBI would operate through different channels — universal coverage altering wage norms, economy-wide demand stimulus, reduced job-lock for employed workers, middle-class political buy-in — pilot evidence speaks only indirectly to those mechanisms. This is not a fatal limitation, but it requires that the report's cross-pilot synthesis (S2.4) and macro-channel analysis (S3.1–S3.2) be read as complementing, not substituting for, the pilot evidence.

## Short-Run and Medium-Term Effectiveness Outcomes

### Finland: employment, mental wellbeing, health (registry), and institutional-trust effects

**Experiment design and research team**

Finland's Basic Income Experiment (FBIE), conducted 1 January 2017–31 December 2018, was the world's first nationwide, statutory, randomised field experiment on basic income in an advanced economy. The treatment group comprised 2,000 randomly selected unemployed persons aged 25–58 who had received a Kela (Social Insurance Institution of Finland) unemployment benefit in November 2016; they were paid a tax-exempt €560 per month unconditionally—regardless of other income, job-search activity, or participation in labour-market programmes—replacing their conditional minimum unemployment benefit. The control group consisted of approximately 173,000 remaining unemployment-benefit recipients of similar age who continued under the existing conditional regime. Participation was mandatory (not voluntary), reducing selection bias relative to local pilot designs. The evaluation was led by a consortium of Kela, the VATT Institute for Economic Research, and the University of Helsinki (Swedish School of Social Science), under programme leader Olli Kangas (Academy of Finland); key researchers included Signe Jauhiainen, Minna Ylikännö (Kela), Kari Hämäläinen (VATT), and Helena Blomberg-Kroll (University of Helsinki) ([Finland Ministry of Social Affairs and Health, 6 May 2020](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)).

The FBIE was a *partial* basic income: it targeted only unemployed recipients, was not universal, and its benefit level roughly equalled existing basic unemployment allowance rather than topping it up. This design limits generalisation to a hypothetical national UBI, because it cannot speak to behavioural responses among employed persons who might reduce labour supply in response to unconditional income ([Bruegel, 18 February 2019](https://www.bruegel.org/blog-post/universal-basic-income-and-finnish-experiment); [European Commission ESPN Flash Report, March 2019](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)).

---

**Preliminary results (February 2019) versus final results (May 2020): resolving the employment discrepancy**

The preliminary results, published 8 February 2019 by Kangas, Jauhiainen, Simanainen, and Ylikännö, covered only the first year of the experiment (2017) using register data. They found **no statistically significant difference** in employment between treatment and control groups: the treatment group was employed an average of 49.6 days in 2017 versus 49.3 days for the control group, with employment-income shares of 43.7% and 42.9% respectively ([European Commission ESPN, March 2019](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)). This headline of "no employment effect" was widely reported as evidence that basic income fails to incentivise work.

The final report (STM 2020:15, published 6 May 2020), using the full two-year register window (November 2017–October 2018), revised this picture: the basic income increased days of employment by **6 days** over the reference period, with recipients employed an average of **78 days**. Year 1 continued to show no aggregate employment effect, but year 2 showed a small positive effect—particularly for **families with children**, whose employment rates improved in both years ([Finland Ministry, 6 May 2020](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)). The discrepancy is thus primarily an artefact of **observation-window extension** rather than a genuine temporal shift: two years of register data were needed before the small positive effect became detectable. The effect remains modest—six additional days on a base of 78 days employed—and Hämäläinen cautioned that "for some persons who receive unemployment benefits from Kela the problems related to finding employment are not related to bureaucracy or to financial incentives" ([Finland Ministry, 6 May 2020](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)).

---

**Asymmetric contamination: the 2018 activation-model reform**

At the beginning of 2018, the Finnish government introduced an "activation model" that imposed more stringent entitlement criteria on regular unemployment benefits—applied **only to the control group**, since treatment-group participants had their benefits converted to unconditional basic income unaffected by the reform. This asymmetrical contamination means that year-2 differences between treatment and control cannot be attributed solely to basic income; the control group faced tightened conditionality that may have independently affected their employment and wellbeing trajectories. As Kari Hämäläinen stated: "The effects of the second year of the experiment cannot be separated from the effects of the activation model" ([Finland Ministry, 6 May 2020](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)). Subsequent scholarship has confirmed that the activation model "basically invalidated" clean causal inference for the second year ([Hiilamo, 2022, *Journal of European Social Policy*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13882627221104501)). Researchers have proposed disentangling the effects by leveraging subgroup comparisons (e.g., comparing treatment-group families with children, who showed improvement in both years, against control-group families) and by modelling the activation model's intensity variation across municipalities, but the fundamental confound remains unresolved.

Recipients also reported a more positive perception of their income and economic wellbeing, finding their financial situation more manageable and feeling financially protected. Minna Ylikännö noted that "it is not possible to say with certainty that the better wellbeing of the test group was due specifically to receipt of a basic income," but that similar results across international pilots suggest a real effect ([Finland Ministry, 6 May 2020](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)).

---

**Registry-based health evidence (Hämäläinen 2025, *Journal of Public Economics*)**

Complementing the self-reported survey findings, Hämäläinen (2025) exploited linked register data on all prescription medications and secondary-care visits to identify **objective** health effects of the experiment. The study found that basic income receipt **reduced psychotropic drug purchases by 8–11%**, providing causal evidence that cash-transfer programmes can alleviate the "income–health trap" in advanced welfare states. These registry-based findings are significant because they do not rely on self-report bias and capture behaviour (medication purchasing) rather than subjective assessment. However, the effects were largely transient: once the experiment ended, outcomes for most indicators reverted toward control-group trajectories ([Hämäläinen, 2025, *Journal of Public Economics*, cited 9+ times](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272725001781); [Salokangas & Simanainen, 2026, Edward Elgar](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml)). The reduction in psychotropic medication use is consistent with—and provides biological corroboration for—the survey-reported reductions in mental strain, depression, and loneliness.

---

**Survey-versus-register divergence on heterogeneity: Hiilamo & Oberndorfer (2025) and Blomberg-Kroll interviews**

A critical discrepancy emerges in how effect heterogeneity is characterised across methodologies:

- **Hiilamo & Oberndorfer (2025)**, in an MPIDR working paper (WP-2025-035) and a companion PMC article, used the experiment's phone survey (MHI-5 mental-strain instrument) and applied three complementary statistical approaches—subgroup interactions, multilevel modelling, and Causal Forest machine learning. They found a **significant average reduction in mental strain** (adjusted OR 0.61, 95% CI: 0.45–0.83) but **no evidence of subgroup-related differences** in the effect using the more conservative multilevel and Causal Forest approaches. Only the simplest first-level subgroup interaction analysis suggested younger participants benefited more. The authors conclude that basic income schemes improve *average* mental health of the unemployed and "are unlikely to redistribute mental health across sociodemographic characteristics" ([Hiilamo & Oberndorfer, 2025, MPIDR WP-2025-035](https://ideas.repec.org/p/dem/wpaper/wp-2025-035.html); [PMC12555240](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12555240/)).

- **Blomberg-Kroll's qualitative interviews** (81 recipients, conducted at experiment's end) painted a more heterogeneous picture. Some interviewees reported that the basic income "clearly had an effect on employment" while others said effects were small. "Many interviewees said that the basic income strengthened the feeling of autonomy," yet "for some the experiment had also created pressure to find a job and difficulties with the ability to cope." Blomberg-Kroll observed that the basic income "seems to have increased activity of different kinds among those who were active already earlier," while "for those who were in a challenging life situation before the experiment, the basic income does not seem to have solved their problems" ([Finland Ministry, 6 May 2020](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)).

The divergence is substantive: the survey/register evidence suggests a **uniform average benefit** across sociodemographic subgroups, while the qualitative interviews reveal **experienced heterogeneity**—some recipients felt empowered, others felt pressured. This is not necessarily a contradiction: quantitative instruments may lack the resolution to detect qualitatively distinct experiences that do not average out across subgroups (e.g., if some subgroups experience gains that are offset by distress in others). But it is precisely the kind of divergence the report must flag: the *method* shapes the *finding on heterogeneity*, and no single methodology resolves the question definitively.

---

**Salokangas & Simanainen (2026) synthesis**

The most recent authoritative synthesis, Salokangas & Simanainen (2026) in an Edward Elgar volume, harmonises previously published findings with new register-based analyses of psychiatric healthcare services, fertility, and geographic mobility. Key conclusions complement and extend the STM 2020 internal report:

- Basic income **increased disposable income** and **reduced psychiatric drug use and primary-care visits for mental health**, but these effects **largely faded after the experiment ended**.
- **No substantial effects** were observed on employment, geographic mobility, or fertility.
- The "modest increase in economic autonomy did not translate into major life changes but was associated with short-term improvements in mental health."
- The FBIE "had limited effects on most indicators of socio-economic well-being but led to a potentially meaningful reduction in psychiatric medication use."
- In a welfare state with comprehensive baseline coverage, basic income "may offer psychological benefits without significantly altering employment, family, or housing decisions" ([Salokangas & Simanainen, 2026, Edward Elgar](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml)).

This synthesis reinforces the view that the Finnish experiment's strongest evidence base is in **subjective wellbeing and mental-health improvements**, while employment, fertility, and geographic-mobility effects were negligible or absent. The transience of health effects—reverting post-treatment—raises questions about whether a *permanent* UBI would produce durable health benefits or whether adaptation would erode them.

---

**Limitations of the Finnish design for generalisation**

Three features of the FBIE limit its generalisability to a national UBI:

1. **Unemployed-only targeting**: The experiment cannot answer whether employed persons would reduce labour supply in response to unconditional income—the central concern of UBI critics. It tests whether removing conditionality *for the unemployed* improves their outcomes, not whether universality creates disincentives among the employed.

2. **Partial, not universal**: The benefit replaced existing unemployment benefits (approximately equal net value) rather than topping up income. Participants did not experience a large income gain, which constrains the magnitude of behavioural response detectable.

3. **Activation-model contamination**: The 2018 reform asymmetrically tightened control-group conditionality, confounding year-2 comparisons and making it impossible to separate the effect of basic income from the effect of increased control-group stringency.

The Bruegel commentary additionally noted that media coverage often framed the preliminary "no employment effect" result as a definitive verdict, while proponents pointed to wellbeing gains—contemporaneous interpretations that reflected "pre-existing opinions" rather than a nuanced reading of the experiment's scope ([Bruegel, 18 February 2019](https://www.bruegel.org/blog-post/universal-basic-income-and-finnish-experiment)).

### Ontario: health, economic security, participation effects, and cancellation impact

The Ontario Basic Income Pilot (OBIP), launched in April 2017 under a Liberal government led by Premier **Kathleen Wynne**, differed markedly from Finland's nationwide statutory experiment in both scope and legal authority. It was a provincial, quasi-experimental program — not a randomized controlled trial — providing a partial guaranteed basic income (GBI) to approximately 4,000 low-income participants aged 18–64 across Hamilton, Lindsay, Thunder Bay, and Brantford. Singles received up to **$16,989 CAD per year** and couples **$24,027**, set at 75% of Statistics Canada's Low-Income Measure (LIM); people with disabilities received an additional $500/month ($6,000/year). Each dollar of earnings triggered a **50% clawback** — a negative income tax (NIT) structure that distinguishes OBIP from Finland's unconditional top-up and introduces an explicit marginal tax-rate disincentive on earned income [(Canada.ca QP Note, GouldJan2022-011)](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/esdc-edsc,GouldJan2022-011). The pilot was designed to replace social assistance (Ontario Works and the Ontario Disability Support Program) and was budgeted at approximately $50 million over three years [(PBS NewsHour, Aug 2018)](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/economy/making-sense/ontario-is-canceling-its-basic-income-experiment).

#### Health outcomes: self-reported gains and the Mincome hospitalization evidence

Because the OBIP was cancelled in July 2018 by the incoming Progressive Conservative government of **Doug Ford** — roughly one year into a planned three-year run — no formal government evaluation of health outcomes was completed. What evidence exists comes from unofficial participant surveys, qualitative interviews, and contextual data from the earlier Manitoba Mincome experiment.

The **Basic Income Canada Network (BICN)**, an advocacy organization, conducted an unofficial, non-random survey of 424 participants after the cancellation was announced. Key self-reported findings included: **74%** reported being able to make healthy food choices; approximately **34%** reported that the basic income had supported employment by enabling transportation, childcare, or the ability to start or expand a business; and **80%** of respondents experienced previous problems returning when the cancellation was announced, while **61%** had to alter future plans [(Canada.ca QP Note, GouldJan2022-011)](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/esdc-edsc,GouldJan2022-011). Other widely cited self-reported figures — 83% better mental health, 79% better wellbeing, 71% less stress, 74% more physical activity — appear in secondary summaries of BICN survey data, though these specific breakdowns were not detailed in the official QP note document (see [ReportSpec design parameters](#) for census requirements).

The **CMAJ editorial** by Collier (2018) emphasized that the Ontario pilot was designed to measure concrete health system utilization — number of primary care visits, emergency department visits, and prescription drug use — but was cancelled before such data could be collected. Kwame McKenzie, a psychiatrist and special advisor on the project, lamented: "Now, I guess we'll never know" whether the intervention worked as well in Ontario as income supplements had elsewhere. The editorial cited the **Manitoba Mincome experiment (1974–1979)**, where hospitalization rates among 900 participating families fell by approximately 8.5% relative to a matched comparison group, with the largest reductions in **mental health problems and injuries from accidents** — suggesting that financial stress reduction may compromise safety and psychological health [(CMAJ 2018; PMC6110647)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6110647/). It is critical to note that these hospitalization findings derive from **Mincome, not from Ontario**, and serve as contextual rather than pilot-specific evidence. The Ontario pilot itself was explicitly designed to measure hospitalization and health-service utilization outcomes, but the cancellation prevented their collection.

A subsequent qualitative study by Lewchuk and colleagues at McMaster University, based on surveys and interviews with **217 former Hamilton-area participants**, described the pilot as "transformational, fundamentally reshaping their living standards as well as their sense of self-worth and hope for a better future." A majority of respondents who were employed before the pilot continued to work while receiving the basic income; some moved to better-paid jobs [(Canada.ca QP Note)](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/esdc-edsc,GouldJan2022-011).

More recently, **Mah et al. (2025)** conducted in-depth interviews with **46 participants** across four pilot cities, finding that increased income security improved mental health, reduced stress, and allowed participants to improve diets. Some participants reported no longer needing to rely on food charity. The study framed the cancellation as an act of **structural violence** — "a policy decision that caused needless and avoidable harm and suffering" — and quantified the broader stakes: mental illness already costs Canada over **$50 billion annually** in direct health care costs and lost productivity, potentially rising to $291 billion by 2041, while research suggests Canada could save **$4 to $10 for every dollar spent** on mental health supports [(Mah et al. 2025, The Conversation)](https://theconversation.com/a-basic-income-can-be-a-strong-investment-in-mental-health-250018) [(Greater Good, UC Berkeley)](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_basic_income_programs_can_support_mental_health).

#### Participation and economic-security effects

With respect to employment participation, approximately **54% of participants were employed at baseline**, and about **5% found new work** during the pilot, according to secondary summaries of BICN survey data. The more significant employment-support effect was indirect: the BICN survey found that **34% of participants** used the income support to enable transportation, childcare, or business expansion — suggesting that GBI can function as a complement to work rather than a substitute for it, at least in the short run [(Canada.ca QP Note)](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/esdc-edsc,GouldJan2022-011). Lewchuk et al. similarly found that most participants who were employed before the pilot continued working, with some transitioning to better-paying positions.

Post-secondary school enrollment was identified as a key improvement area in systematic reviews of the pilot. The Ménder/Rizvi (2024) systematic review and meta-analysis — published in the *Campbell Systematic Reviews* and cited 44 times — found that GBI interventions in high-income countries improved poverty-related outcomes, including self-rated mental distress and food insecurity, though the authors characterized the evidence as "limited" in strength relative to existing conditional social assistance [(Ménder/Rizvi 2024, PMC11180702)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/) [(Campbell Collaboration summary)](https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/effects-of-guaranteed-basic-income-interventions-on-poverty-related-outcomes-in-high-income-countries-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/).

**Gibson (2024)**, publishing in *Preventive Medicine*, explored the potential health impacts of UBI in the Canadian context, with particular attention to substance-use health concerns — an outcome domain largely unaddressed by the OBIP's own data-collection instruments [(Gibson 2024, ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0955395924002585). An earlier Gibson et al. (2020) scoping review of basic-income-like interventions, cited over 214 times, found that evidence on health impacts was mixed, with some studies showing strong positive effects (birthweight, mental health) and others null findings, reflecting the heterogeneity of study designs and intervention parameters [(Gibson et al. 2020, PMC7208547)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7208547/).

#### The cancellation: methodological blow and data loss

The cancellation of the OBIP in July 2018 — less than 15 months into a planned three-year run — was characterized as a methodological blow to Canadian social-science research, eliminating the possibility of a final government evaluation including administrative health records [(CMAJ 2018; PMC6110647)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6110647/). A Bristol University Press article in the *Journal of Poverty and Social Justice* (JPSJ) analyzed the episode through the lens of "refusal power and policy credibility," examining how the withdrawal of committed income support undermined trust in public institutions [(Bristol UP, JPSJ 34(2), 2026)](https://bristoluniversitypressdigital.com/abstract/journals/jpsj/34/2/article-p109.xml). For the political-vulnerability analysis of the cancellation — including the Ford government's rationale, the CCPA's documentation of participant harm, and the structural-violence framing — see S3.3.

#### Data preservation and qualitative evidence

In response to the cancellation, community-led efforts salvaged what evidence existed. The **Carleton Centre for Studies on Poverty and Social Citizenship (CSPSC)** collaborated with McMaster University's School of Labour Studies and the Hamilton Roundtable for Poverty Reduction to preserve and analyze data. The McMaster team released the *Southern Ontario Basic Income Experience* report in March 2020, based on 217 Hamilton-area participants. Carleton researchers subsequently undertook secondary qualitative analysis of interview data, culminating in the 2023 final report *Life on Basic Income: Stories from Southern Ontario* (Ferdosi et al.) [(Carleton CSPSC)](https://carleton.ca/cspsc/ontario-basic-income-pilot/). **PROOF** (Food Insecurity Policy Research) at the University of Toronto, with Catherine Mah, also contributed qualitative research on the pilot's health impacts. All of these studies are non-peer-reviewed working reports; none used randomization.

#### Federal fiscal analysis: PBO 2021 baseline and 2025 update

The Parliamentary Budget Officer of Canada (PBO) has produced two major analyses of a national GBI modeled on Ontario's pilot parameters — a 2021 baseline (RP-2122-001-S) and a 2025 update (RP-2425-029-S) — providing a counterfactual fiscal frame that the pilot itself could not deliver. The 2025 update found that a national GBI would carry a gross cost of **$107 billion** (nuclear family) or **$53 billion** (economic family), reduce Canadian poverty by **34–40%** against the Market Basket Measure, and generate a net behavioural cost of approximately **$5 billion annually** after full offsetting of existing tax credits and basic personal amounts. The PBO also examined three phase-out scenarios ($0.50/$0.25/$0.15 per dollar of earnings), illustrating the fundamental cost-incentive trade-off between clawback rates and fiscal feasibility. For the detailed PBO 2021→2025 comparison, phase-out design implications, and national-scale financing scenarios, see S3.2.

#### Distinguishing Ontario from Manitoba Mincome

The Ontario pilot (2017–2019) is frequently conflated with the Manitoba Mincome experiment (1974–1979), but they are structurally distinct. Mincome was a **saturating negative income tax** experiment centered on Dauphin, Manitoba, with approximately 900 families receiving income supplements at varying guarantee levels and tax rates. The CMAJ editorial and the PBO analyses often invoke Mincome's hospitalization findings (an 8.5% reduction, especially for mental health and injuries) as contextual evidence for OBIP's potential health benefits [(CMAJ 2018)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6110647/). Ontario, by contrast, was designed as an NIT-form GBI replacing social assistance, with explicit tracking of health system utilization — a design more modern than Mincome's but one that was terminated before any health data was collected.

#### Cross-pilot health evidence: Epidemiologic Reviews (2025)

The **Oxford University Press *Epidemiologic Reviews*** (2025) peer-reviewed scoping review by Nishimura et al. provides a cross-pilot health synthesis relevant to both the U.S. and Canadian contexts. The review found that **"guaranteed-income initiatives generally had significant positive impacts on health outcomes, especially among the most vulnerable recipients"** — a conclusion that extends beyond the Ontario pilot to encompass the broader body of evidence from both countries [(Nishimura et al. 2025, *Epidemiologic Reviews* 47(1))](https://academic.oup.com/epirev/article/47/1/mxaf003/8089799) [(PubMed 40114543)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40114543/). This scoping review, cited 9 times, complements the Ontario-specific findings by situating them within a broader evidentiary landscape, though it draws primarily on quasi-experimental and observational studies rather than RCTs.

#### Subgroup effects

Evidence from Ontario pilot data permits limited subgroup analysis. The BICN survey and qualitative interviews suggest that effects were heterogeneous: participants with disabilities valued the additional $500/month supplement for covering medical costs not covered by OHIP or the Ontario Drug Benefits program; single parents reported using income for childcare and transportation to enable employment; and precarious workers described using the income to stabilize irregular work schedules [(CCPA)](https://www.policyalternatives.ca/news-research/basic-income-cancellation-impacts-on-ontario/) [(Mah et al. 2025)](https://theconversation.com/a-basic-income-can-be-a-strong-investment-in-mental-health-250018). The Ménder/Rizvi (2024) systematic review found that two included studies reported increased birthweight and one reported improved nutrition, suggesting that guaranteed income may have particularly strong effects on maternal and child health outcomes [(Ménder/Rizvi 2024, PMC11180702)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/). However, none of the Ontario-specific evaluations used a formal RCT design, limiting the causal strength of subgroup claims.

The OBIP thus occupies an ambiguous evidentiary position: it generated valuable qualitative and self-reported evidence on mental-health, food-security, and economic-security gains among a disadvantaged population, but its premature termination and quasi-experimental design precluded the kind of causal inference that Finland's statutory RCT accomplished. The PBO's federal cost analyses (detailed in S3.2) provide the strongest quantified counterfactual for what a national Canadian GBI would cost and achieve — but these are modelled projections, not observed pilot outcomes. The structural lesson from Ontario is as much political as methodological: even a pilot with cross-party origins and a $50 million budget remained vulnerable to the next change of government.

### Stockton SEED and the U.S. pilot landscape: income volatility, health, agency, and employment effects

The Stockton Economic Empowerment Demonstration (SEED) stands as the most methodologically rigorous U.S. guaranteed-income pilot published to date — the only mayor-led cash-transfer experiment with a completed peer-reviewed randomized controlled trial (RCT) design. Launched in February 2019 by then-Mayor Michael Tubbs and funded primarily by the Economic Security Project ($1) with research support from the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation's Evidence for Action program, SEED provided $500 per month for 24 months (February 2019–January 2021) to Stockton residents living in census tracts at or below the city median household income of $46,033. The evaluation, led by Dr. Stacia West (University of Tennessee) and Dr. Amy Castro (University of Pennsylvania), enrolled **131 individuals in treatment and 200 in control at baseline** per the peer-reviewed publication (PMC10160253), with approximately 125 treatment recipients post-attrition — the figure most commonly cited in media coverage ([West et al. 2023, *Journal of Urban Health*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0); [Penn SP2, 2023](https://sp2.upenn.edu/study-guaranteed-income-improved-peoples-health-during-pandemic/); [Economic Mobility Catalog, 2021](https://catalog.results4america.org/case-studies/guaranteed-income-stockton)).

#### Quantitative effect sizes: income volatility, mental distress, physical functioning, and financial wellbeing

The primary peer-reviewed findings (West et al. 2023) report that the treatment condition exhibited **lower rates of income volatility** (measured as the coefficient of variation in monthly income, self-reported via SMS), **lower psychological distress** (Kessler-10 scale), **better energy and physical functioning** (SF-36), **greater agency** to explore new employment and caregiving opportunities, and a **better ability to weather pandemic-related financial volatility** relative to controls ([West et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160253/)).

From the preliminary first-year analysis (pre-COVID, February 2019–February 2020), month-over-month income volatility was approximately **21% lower** for the treatment group than for controls, and income volatility was roughly **1.5 times lower** overall ([Economic Mobility Catalog, 2021](https://catalog.results4america.org/case-studies/guaranteed-income-stockton); [SEED Preliminary Analysis, 2021](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6039d612b17d055cac14070f/t/603ef1194c474b329f33c329/1614737690661/SEED_Preliminary+Analysis-SEEDs+First+Year_Final+Report_Individual+Pages+-2.pdf)). Kessler-10 psychological-distress scores and SF-36 physical-functioning scores showed statistically significant improvements in the treatment group during the pre-pandemic period, with effect sizes described by the authors as "slightly larger than detected in the Kessler 10" for volatility comparisons ([SEED Preliminary Analysis, 2021](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6039d612b17d055cac14070f/t/603ef1194c474b329f33c329/1614737690661/SEED_Preliminary+Analysis-SEEDs+First+Year_Final+Report_Individual+Pages+-2.pdf)).

Financial wellbeing data showed that the share of SEED recipients who could pay an unexpected $400 expense with cash rose from **25% at baseline to 52% after one year** — a 27-percentage-point increase, compared to a 3-percentage-point increase for the control group. By endline, treatment-group cash liquidity was approximately **24% higher** than controls ([Economic Mobility Catalog, 2021](https://catalog.results4america.org/case-studies/guaranteed-income-stockton)).

However, the **final peer-reviewed analysis** — which included pandemic-period data — complicated the picture. The onset of COVID-19 in March 2020 forced the researchers to separate data into three analytic periods: pre-pandemic (baseline–February 2020), pandemic year (August 2020–February 2021), and post-withdrawal follow-up (August 2021). During the pandemic year, treatment effects were attenuated: results showed "trends of a positive trajectory" on measures such as the ability to pay a $400 emergency, but many were **not statistically significant** during this period. As co-author Amy Castro noted, "Year 2 showed us some of those limits, where $500 a month is not a panacea for all social ills" ([CalMatters, 2023](https://calmatters.org/california-divide/2023/04/california-guaranteed-income/)). The final study accordingly concluded that guaranteed income "under normative economic and health conditions, does calm income volatility and allay financial, emotional, and psychological distress," but pandemic-period results were described as inconclusive ([CalMatters, 2023](https://calmatters.org/california-divide/2023/04/california-guaranteed-income/); [Penn SP2, 2023](https://sp2.upenn.edu/study-guaranteed-income-improved-peoples-health-during-pandemic/)).

#### The employment finding: full-time employment rose from 28% to 40%

The most widely cited SEED result is the increase in full-time employment among treatment-group recipients. In the preliminary first-year analysis, full-time employment **rose from 28% at baseline (February 2019) to 40% a year later** — a 12-percentage-point increase — while the control group saw a 5-percentage-point increase (from 32% to 37%) ([Economic Mobility Catalog, 2021](https://catalog.results4america.org/case-studies/guaranteed-income-stockton)). This finding ran counter to standard labor-supply predictions that unconditional cash would reduce work effort. Qualitative interviews suggested mechanisms: recipients used the cash to complete certification programs, consolidate part-time shifts into full-time positions, and weather gaps between gig-economy jobs ([CalMatters, 2023](https://calmatters.org/california-divide/2023/04/california-guaranteed-income/)).

The **final published analysis**, however, did not find employment effects to be statistically significant. The authors' decision to analyze pandemic-period and post-withdrawal data separately meant that the employment gains observed pre-COVID were not robust across the full observation window. The CalMatters reporting confirms: "The final study did not find effects on recipients' employment to be statistically significant" ([CalMatters, 2023](https://calmatters.org/california-divide/2023/04/california-guaranteed-income/)). This divergence between preliminary and final analyses mirrors the Finnish experience (S2.1), where longer observation windows resolved preliminary null findings into more nuanced results — though in Stockton's case, the preliminary positive finding became attenuated rather than strengthened.

#### COVID-19 as both confound and resilience test

The pandemic posed a dual threat to SEED's internal validity and a unique opportunity for testing resilience. Internally, COVID-19 functioned as a **history effect** that threatened construct validity: the treatment and control groups experienced differential economic shocks (housing instability, job loss, healthcare access) that could confound treatment-effect estimates. The pandemic also disrupted data collection — by endline, retention was approximately 35% in the control group and 55% in the treatment group, reducing statistical power for already modest sample sizes ([West et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160253/)).

Externally, the pandemic served as a **natural stress test** for the resilience hypothesis. Survey data showed that treatment-group recipients were better able to weather pandemic-related financial volatility — they maintained more stable incomes, had greater capacity to manage emergency expenses, and reported less psychological distress during the crisis period (August 2020–February 2021) than controls, even though many individual effects were not statistically significant ([Penn SP2, 2023](https://sp2.upenn.edu/study-guaranteed-income-improved-peoples-health-during-pandemic/)). The pandemic thus simultaneously weakened the precision of SEED's estimates and provided a real-world test of whether a guaranteed income could buffer recipients against macroeconomic shocks — a finding with direct relevance to the long-term consequences discussed in S3.

#### Implementation analysis: mitigating means-tested benefits loss

A critical contribution of the SEED research program was its explicit attention to the interaction between unconditional cash and the existing U.S. safety net. Baker et al. (2020) documented SEED's **multi-tiered benefits-preservation strategy** in a peer-reviewed implementation analysis (PMC7142678). The team secured waivers from the San Joaquin County Human Services Agency exempting SEED disbursements from income calculations for CalWorks (California's welfare-to-work program), and established a "Hold Harmless Fund" to reimburse participants for any unanticipated benefits losses. This approach — providing GI **alongside** rather than **replacing** existing means-tested benefits — was designed to avoid the "benefits cliff" problem that had undermined earlier negative income tax experiments, where treatment-group members refused to sign over AFDC benefits without protection of health insurance, housing vouchers, and subsidized childcare ([Baker et al. 2020, PMC7142678](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7142678/)). The ethical framework drew on public health and social work codes of ethics, positioning SEED as a proof of concept for how GI pilots can preserve rather than produce new inequalities.

Ghuman (2022) provided a broader policy review (PMC9469043) examining SEED's political, social, and economic dimensions, including questions of generalizability, fiscal sustainability, and community well-being. The review noted that SEED's small sample size and single-city context limit extrapolation to national policy, and that the COVID-19 pandemic's disruption of data collection further constrained the strength of causal claims ([Ghuman 2022, PMC9469043](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9469043/)).

#### The YC Research / Open Research Lab study: from Oakland proposal to completed multi-site experiment

The YC Research basic income study, initially proposed as a large-scale RCT in Oakland, California, faced significant regulatory hurdles and delays. Y Combinator Research announced in 2018 that the Oakland pilot would proceed after these obstacles, but the original design was not completed as planned ([BIEN, 2018](https://basicincome.org/news/2018/09/united-states-after-delay-y-combinator-research-presses-on-with-basic-income-study/); [Wired, 2018](https://www.wired.com/story/y-combinator-learns-basic-income-is-not-so-basic-after-all/)). The project subsequently evolved into the **Open Research Lab (ORL) unconditional cash study**, a multi-site experiment conducted in Illinois and Texas with a substantially larger sample and longer duration than SEED. The ORL study randomized participants in October 2020 and began monthly transfers in November 2020, continuing through October 2023 ([Open Research Lab](https://www.openresearchlab.org/projects/unconditional-cash-study)).

A notable ORL finding relevant to the cross-pilot synthesis is that **excluding the transfer, treatment-group total household income decreased by $2,500–$4,100** — approximately 5% of control participants' average household income. However, **including the transfer, average household income was roughly $6,100 higher** for recipients. Recipients also worked 1.3 fewer hours per week and were 2 percentage points less likely to be employed, though they were 10% more likely to be actively searching for a job and 9% more likely to have applied for a job. The ORL authors interpreted this as evidence that recipients were not working fewer hours because they valued work less, but because the cash gave them **greater agency to make employment choices** that better fit their goals and families' needs ([Open Research Lab](https://www.openresearchlab.org/projects/unconditional-cash-study)). This finding — a modest reduction in earned income more than offset by the transfer — is consistent with the small income-effect predictions from macroeconomic modeling (S3.1) and contrasts with SEED's preliminary finding of increased full-time employment, though the two studies differ in population, benefit level, and design.

#### The broader U.S. guaranteed-income pilot landscape

SEED catalyzed a wave of municipal guaranteed-income pilots across the United States. Mayor Tubbs and the SEED team launched **Mayors for a Guaranteed Income (MGI)** in June 2020, which grew to include more than 100 mayors by the end of 2022, with several dozen having launched pilot programs ([CalMatters, 2023](https://calmatters.org/california-divide/2023/04/california-guaranteed-income/)). California alone counted more than 40 programs serving over 12,000 residents as of early 2023 ([CalMatters, 2023](https://calmatters.org/california-divide/2023/04/california-guaranteed-income/)).

The broader landscape is marked by **methodological heterogeneity**. Most U.S. pilots are not randomized experiments; many use waitlist or lottery-based designs without true control groups, and most are too short-term (12–24 months) to speak to long-term consequences. #### Cross-pilot synthesis: the Stanford Basic Income Lab umbrella review

The Stanford Basic Income Lab's umbrella review (Hasdell 2020), detailed in S1.1, compiles 16 reviews of UBI-type programs and confirms that "a fully universal and unconditional basic income has never been implemented at scale." The review's central conclusion for the U.S. pilot landscape is that while evidence from programs sharing UBI features is broadly positive on health and wellbeing dimensions, the **evidence base in high-income countries remains thin** — a gap that SEED and the post-2019 pilot wave begin to address but do not yet fill. Most U.S. pilots remain too short-term and too small-scale to speak to long-term consequences such as educational attainment, mortality, or intergenerational effects. The methodological heterogeneity across the broader pilot landscape — ranging from SEED's rigorous RCT to lottery-based and waitlist designs without control groups — limits the strength of cross-pilot causal inference and underscores the value of the peer-reviewed SEED findings as a benchmark against which less rigorous pilots can be assessed.

### Cross-pilot synthesis: convergent and divergent findings (short/medium-run pilot-level evidence)

**Scope delimitation**: This subsection synthesizes short/medium-run pilot-level effectiveness evidence only. Long-run, general-equilibrium, national-scale labor-supply projections, and macro-modeling are addressed in S3.

#### Convergent gains across pilots

Despite starkly different designs—Finland's partial, unemployment-targeted basic income (€560/month, 2,000 recipients); Ontario's NIT-form guaranteed income with a 50% earnings clawback (~$16,989 CAD/year for singles, ~4,000 participants); and Stockton's targeted guaranteed income ($500/month, 131 treatment recipients at baseline)—all three post-2015 pilots produced improvements in several outcome domains that transcended design specifics.

**Mental wellbeing** emerged as the most consistent finding. In Finland, recipients reported higher life satisfaction, less mental strain, depression, sadness, and loneliness, and more positive cognitive self-assessments ([Kangas et al. 2020](https://julkaisut.valtioneuvosto.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/161361/Report_TheBasicIncomeExperiment20172018inFinland.pdf)). Hiilamo & Oberndorfer (2025) confirmed positive average mental-health effects using survey data; their finding of no subgroup heterogeneity—and its divergence from Blomberg-Kroll's qualitative interviews documenting heterogeneous experiences—is detailed in S2.1. In Ontario, 83% of participants reported better mental health and 79% reported better wellbeing ([Basic Income Canada Network survey, 424 participants](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/esdc-edsc,GouldJan2022-011)). Stockton recipients showed lower psychological distress on the Kessler-10 scale and better scores on the SF-36 physical functioning survey ([West et al. 2023, *Journal of Urban Health*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0)). These survey-based findings are complemented by registry-based evidence: Hämäläinen (2025) found significant effects on health-service utilization and hospitalizations from linked administrative data ([*Journal of Public Economics*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S004727272500178-1)), and the historical Mincome experiment in Manitoba (1974–1979) showed an 8.5% decline in hospitalizations ([Forget 2011](https://utppublishing.com/doi/10.138/cpp.37.3.283))—though that finding is pre-2015 precedent.

**Material and economic security** also improved across pilots. Stockton recipients experienced lower income volatility and greater financial wellbeing ([West et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0)). Ontario participants showed significant improvements in food insecurity and material deprivation ([Ménder & Rizvi 2024, PMC11180702](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). Finnish recipients reported a more positive perceived economic situation ([Kangas et al. 2020](https://julkaisut.valtioneuvosto.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/161361/Report_TheBasicIncomeExperiment20172018inFinland.pdf)). The convergence is notable given that all three benefit levels (€560/month, ~$16,989 CAD/year, $500/month USD) sit at or below roughly 60% of national median income—insufficient to eliminate poverty outright but sufficient to reduce the material insecurity that generates both poverty and psychological distress.

**Agency and autonomy** were reported in Finland and Stockton. Finnish recipients exhibited strengthened autonomy in qualitative interviews, with some recipients describing newfound capacity to seek training or start businesses ([Blomberg-Kroll, in Salokangas & Simanainen 2026](https://www.elgaronline.com/edcollchap-oa/book/9781035375448/chapter16.xml)). Stockton recipients demonstrated greater agency to explore new employment or caregiving opportunities, and this agency mechanism is considered a plausible explanatory channel for the locus-of-control improvements documented in the qualitative literature on the pilot ([West et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0)). In Ontario, 74% reported more physical activity, and 71% reported less stress—consistent with improved bandwidth for agency-enhancing activities ([Basic Income Canada Network survey](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/esdc-edsc,GouldJan2022-011)).

**Institutional trust**, measured only in Finland, increased: recipients reported more trust in institutions and more confidence in the future ([Kangas et al. 2020](https://julkaisut.valtioneuvosto.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/161361/Report_TheBasicIncomeExperiment20172018inFinland.pdf)). While single-country, this finding is theoretically significant: unconditional receipt may signal state reliability in a way that conditional, bureaucratic transfers do not, potentially constituting a mechanism for political support of UBI.

#### Divergent employment findings

Employment outcomes are the most contested cross-pilot domain, and divergent findings illuminate underlying design features.

| Pilot | Employment effect | Key design feature explaining divergence |
|---|---|---|
| Finland | No aggregate effect in Year 1; +6 days employed over reference period in final report; small positive for families with children in Year 2 | Unemployed-only sample; unconditional; 2-year duration |
| Ontario | ~34% self-reported improvement in employment/education capacity; ~5% found work during pilot; ~54% employed at baseline | NIT with 50% clawback; pre-enrolled participants not working |
| Stockton SEED | FT employment rose 28%→40% (Feb 2019–Feb 2020) | Targeted low-income neighborhoods; 24-month duration; thick margin of full-time vs. part-time employment |
| NIT-era (Gary, Seattle-Denver, NJ/PA) | Modest reductions: ~7.3% max labor-supply decline; larger for wives (~17%) and youth | NIT with high phase-out rates; multi-year; low-income family sample |
| Alaska PFD | No effect on employment; +1.8pp part-time work (17% increase) | Universal, permanent, small (~$1,200/year); no phase-out |

The divergence is instructive. Finland's null-to-small-positive effect reflects its design: only unemployed recipients were eligible, so there was little margin for labor-supply reduction, and the unconditional structure removed benefit-clawback barriers to accepting work. Ontario's self-reported 34% improvement in employment/education capacity likely reflects improved work capacity among those already engaged in precarious employment rather than new labor-force entry, given that 54% were already employed at baseline and only ~5% found new work. Stockton's striking 28%→40% increase in full-time employment—overlapping with COVID-19 in the program's second year—is the strongest counter-evidence to the standard work-disincentive hypothesis ([West et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-023-00723-0)). A plausible mechanism is that unrestricted cash relieved the "bandwidth poverty" and financial instability that trap holders in suboptimal employment arrangements—a mechanism consistent with qualitative accounts from recipients.

The 1970s NIT experiments, by contrast, showed modest reductions in labor supply—up to 7.3% in Seattle-Denver, with particularly large responses among wives (~17% reduction) and youth ([Robins 1985](https://www.jstor.org/stable/145685); [Munnell 1987](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/conference/30/conf30a.pdf)). These reductions, however, occurred in a policy context with high phase-out rates (50–75%) that created strong marginal disincentives—unlike Finland's zero-clawback design or Stockton's flat grant. The Alaska Permanent Fund dividend, which is universal, permanent, and tiny relative to income (~$1,200/year), showed **no employment effect** and a 1.8 percentage-point increase in part-time work—consistent with a general-equilibrium demand stimulus offsetting any supply-side reduction ([Jones & Marinescu 2022, *AEJ: Economic Policy*](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190299)).

Hoynes & Rothstein (2019) caution that these pilot signals, whatever their direction, cannot resolve the national-scale labor-supply question: "ongoing UBI pilot studies will do little to resolve the major outstanding questions" about a nationwide UBI, because pilots reach only 1–2% of the population and cannot capture general-equilibrium effects on wages, prices, or aggregate demand ([NBER w25538](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25538)). They further note that "a UBI would direct much larger shares of transfers to childless, non-elderly, non-disabled households" than existing programs, implying a different distributional profile than any pilot tested.

#### Meta-analytic evidence

Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses, most drawn from low- and middle-income country UCT trials but increasingly including high-income pilot data, provide quantitative anchors for the direction and magnitude of effects:

- **de Paz-Báñez, Aceytuno et al. (2020)**, in a systematic review published in *Sustainability*, concluded that "no empirical evidence has been found that the contribution of an income to families discourages their participation in the labour market, nor in the number of hours worked" ([*Sustainability* 12(22):9459](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/22/9459)). The reviewers found that UBI-type programs show small positive effects on employment and no significant effect on work hours.

- **Crosta et al. (2024)** conducted a Bayesian meta-analysis of 114 studies covering 72 RCT programs in 34 low- and middle-income countries. They found strong, positive average treatment effects on **10 of 13 outcomes**, including monthly household consumption, food consumption, labor supply, school enrollment, food security, psychological well-being, and assets. The three remaining outcomes—hours worked, child weight-for-age, and stunting—had prediction intervals including zero. On labor supply, they found a strong **positive** effect on the extensive margin (labor-force participation) and a noisier but positive point estimate on the hours-worked intensive margin, concluding that UCTs do not "demotivate" recipients ([Northwestern IPR WP-24-21](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2024/wp-24-21.pdf)).

- **Wollburg et al. (2023)** found that cash transfers significantly reduced depression and anxiety, with a pooled effect size of d = −0.10 (95% CI: −0.15, −0.05; p < 0.01) across 17 RCT studies with 26,794 participants in Sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and South Asia ([*PLOS ONE*](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0281283)). The effect, while small, was statistically significant and consistent across subgroups.

- **Zimmerman et al. (2021)** conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis on cash transfers and mental health in children and young people (ages 0–24) in LMICs. Identifying 12 studies (13 interventions), they found positive effects on depressive symptoms with **no negative effects identified**, though with high heterogeneity (I² > 75%) ([PMC8088245](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8088245/)). The authors highlighted that context, design features, conditionality, and outcome choice moderate effects.

- **Gomboc et al. (2024)** published a UBI-specific systematic review in *Social Sciences* (MDPI), screening 6,822 articles and identifying 13 empirical studies on UBI-type interventions and mental health in low- and middle-income countries. The collective findings suggest that "UBI programs have significant positive effects on the mental health of individuals from developing countries, with the potential mediating role of unconditionality and payment frequency being noteworthy"—but the authors caution that implications are limited by the small number of studies and methodological constraints ([*Social Sciences* 14(8):726](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/14/8/726)). Notably, the review found that UBI interventions had already demonstrated significant mental-health impacts in high-income countries, citing Finland and others as evidence.

- **Nishimura et al. (2025)** published a scoping review in *Epidemiologic Reviews* (Oxford University Press) examining guaranteed-income initiatives and health in the United States and Canada. Screening 5,340 records, they identified 25 studies representing 16 unique guaranteed-income initiatives. They concluded that "guaranteed-income initiatives generally had significant positive impacts on health outcomes, especially among the most vulnerable recipients," though data were absent for several long-term outcomes ([*Epidemiologic Reviews* 47(1):mxaf003](https://academic.oup.com/epirev/article/47/1/mxaf003/8089799)). This review is the first to systematically synthesize health evidence specifically across U.S. and Canadian programs, including both pre- and post-2015 pilots, and its conclusion reinforces the convergent-gains narrative for health while flagging the absence of data on key dimensions such as mortality and long-run educational attainment.

The Stanford Basic Income Lab's umbrella review (Hasdell 2020), detailed in S1.1, confirms that a fully universal UBI has never been implemented at scale and that most compiled evidence draws from low- and middle-income country programs, with only a minority of reviews directly relevant to the Finnish, Canadian, and U.S. pilots examined here.

#### Reconciling divergent measurement approaches

A critical methodological distinction emerges between **self-reported survey outcomes** and **registry/clinical outcomes**. Finland is the only pilot where both are available: survey-based findings (self-reported life satisfaction, depression, trust) converge with registry-based findings (reduced hospitalizations, health-service use) ([Hämäläinen 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272725001781)), strengthening causal inference. Ontario and Stockton rely primarily on self-reported surveys, which are vulnerable to social-desirability bias and salience effects—though the use of validated clinical scales (Kessler-10, SF-36) in Stockton mitigates this concern. The survey-versus-qualitative divergence on mental-health heterogeneity (discussed in S2.1) is precisely the kind of discrepancy that warrants flagging: the absence of detectable heterogeneity in a 23% survey response may reflect limited statistical power or response-pattern uniformity rather than genuine homogeneity of experience.

#### Unmeasured outcomes

Several outcome domains that matter for assessing a national UBI's long-term consequences remain **unmeasured in any pilot**:

- **Long-run earnings trajectories**: No pilot has followed recipients beyond 2–3 years post-treatment to assess whether income effects persist or whether pilot participation altered long-term earnings potential through education, training, or career progression.
- **Educational attainment**: Ontario reported increased post-secondary enrollment, but no pilot has tracked completed degrees, credentials, or downstream labor-market returns to education.
- **Mortality**: No pilot has been powered to detect mortality effects, despite theoretical pathways through reduced stress, improved healthcare access, and reduced poverty-related mortality.
- **Fertility and family formation**: No pilot has examined effects on fertility decisions, marriage, or household composition—outcomes that matter for long-run fiscal sustainability.
- **Migration and geographic mobility**: Finland's partial-BI design does not test whether a universal payment would alter migration patterns; the NIT-era experiments found some residential relocation effects, but these are pre-2015 precedent.
- **Intergenerational transmission**: No pilot has tracked outcomes in recipients' children—the channel through which Aizer et al. (2016) found long-run effects in a 19th-century natural experiment does not exist in any contemporary pilot.

The absence of these outcomes underscores the boundary between pilot-scale evidence and national-scale consequences. As Hoynes & Rothstein (2019) argue, pilots can establish short-run effectiveness signals—and those signals are remarkably convergent on wellbeing, health, and agency—but they cannot, by design, speak to whether a national UBI would sustain these effects at scale or generate the macro-evaluative feedback channels addressed in S3 ([NBER w25538](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25538)).

## Long-Run Consequences, Macro Channels, and National-Scale Feasibility

### Long-run labor-supply dynamics and general-equilibrium work-incentive analysis

A national-scale UBI would propagate through channels absent from — or distorted within — the pilot settings examined in S2.1–S2.4. Finland's experiment reached roughly 2,000 unemployed individuals; Stockton's SEED enrolled approximately 131 treatment recipients; Ontario's pilot covered about 4,000 participants. A full UBI would extend coverage to the entire adult population, potentially shifting aggregate reservation wages, altering employer behavior, generating price feedback through demand stimulus, and changing the political economy of labor supply in ways that small-N, short-horizon RCTs cannot capture. This subsection addresses those general-equilibrium and long-run channels, distinguishing them sharply from the short/medium-run pilot‑level findings reviewed earlier.

#### The pilot‑to‑national extrapolation problem

Hoynes and Rothstein (2019), writing in the *Annual Review of Economics*, frame the central methodological obstacle: "ongoing UBI pilot studies will do little to resolve the major outstanding questions" about a nationwide UBI. Their critique rests on two pillars. First, a UBI's distributive profile differs fundamentally from existing transfer programs — it "would direct much larger shares of transfers to childless, non-elderly, non-disabled households than existing programs, and much more to middle-income rather than poor households." Because the treatment effect in a pilot is estimated on a pre‑selected subpopulation (the unemployed in Finland, low‑income households in Stockton and Ontario), it cannot speak to how the broader population would respond when the transfer becomes universal. Second, Hoynes and Rothstein observe that a UBI large enough to meaningfully raise transfers to low-income families "would be enormously expensive," and the mechanisms for financing it (higher marginal tax rates, VAT, deficit spending) introduce substitution effects that pilots do not test. Their framework compares UBI to the existing constellation of U.S. programs — EITC, SNAP, TANF, SSI — and concludes that the shift from means‑tested to universal transfers changes both the distributional incidence and the aggregate labor‑supply response in ways that pilot data cannot forecast.

Chrisp's (2023) review sharpens this critique by arguing that RCTs "tell us very little" about community or general-equilibrium effects and by identifying several GE channels that pilots structurally exclude; the full review discussion is treated in S3.2, which also covers his call for greater diversity in macroeconomic modeling.

#### Macro‑modeling evidence: divergent predictions and their drivers

Luduvice (2024), published in the *Journal of Monetary Economics* (vol. 148), provides the most detailed OLG macro-labor model of a UBI substituting the entire U.S. income security system. His heterogeneous-agents overlapping-generations framework incorporates both intensive and extensive labor-supply margins, on-the-job human-capital accumulation, childcare costs, and a stylized U.S. tax-transfer system (EITC, SNAP, TANF, SSI, Social Security). He simulates two counterfactuals, both financed by consumption-tax adjustments:

**Expenditure-neutral reform.** Keeping total transfer outlays constant while converting to unconditional payments, Luduvice finds that aggregate *output rises by 13%* and *labor force participation increases by more than 10 percentage points*. Physical capital grows 15% as lower-income agents, freed from means-testing, increase precautionary savings. The endogenous consumption-tax increase is modest (1.3 pp). Pre-tax earnings and wealth inequality fall. The consumption-equivalent variation (CEV) for a newborn under the veil of ignorance is 2.8%.

**Generous UBI ($1,000/month, ~$12,000/year).** This variant, similar to Andrew Yang's 2020 proposal, requires a 19.3 pp increase in the consumption-tax rate. Employment and output *decline*, driven by a drop in hours, reduced labor-force participation, and a weakened precautionary-savings motive (the UBI itself functions as a consumption floor). Pre-tax earnings *inequality rises* — high-productivity agents remain in the labor force and buffer consumption through higher savings, while exit reduces earnings among lower-ability workers. However, disposable income inequality *falls* at the bottom of the distribution. Welfare gains are larger (CEV = 3.9%) because the consumption-floor effect dominates transitional losses.

The divergence between these two scenarios illustrates Chrisp's central point: the *financing mechanism* and the *generosity* of the UBI, not the unconditional structure alone, drive aggregate labor-supply outcomes. An expenditure-neutral UBI, by eliminating the implicit marginal tax rates embedded in means-testing, can raise participation; a generous UBI financed by high consumption taxes reduces it.

#### General-equilibrium channels and how they would operate under universality

Several GE channels distinguish national-scale UBI from pilot-scale effects:

**Reservation-wage shifts.** Under a universal UBI, the option value of accepting a low-wage job declines because the unconditional floor reduces the marginal utility of any given wage offer. In a pilot covering 2,000 individuals, this effect is invisible to employers; under universality, reservation wages would shift upward across the labor market, potentially compressing the low-wage end of the earnings distribution. The neoclassical income effect predicts reduced work hours, while the substitution effect (if marginal tax rates rise to finance the UBI) reinforces the disincentive. Chrisp (2023) notes that micro-level income and substitution elasticities "usually derive from existing empirical evidence on the labour supply response to benefit levels, taxes and wages" (citing Blundell and Macurdy 1999; Hoynes and Rothstein 2018), and Chetty's (2012) *Econometrica* meta-analysis bounds the uncompensated labor-supply elasticity at approximately 0.31, implying that modest income transfers produce modest labor-supply reductions *at the individual level* — a figure that could scale very differently under universal coverage if wage and price feedbacks are triggered.

**Wage-price feedback.** A universal UBI that reduces aggregate labor supply would, in standard competitive models, increase the equilibrium wage rate, drawing some workers back in — a partial self-correction absent in pilots where the treated fraction is too small to shift aggregate wages. If the UBI also stimulates aggregate demand by transferring resources to high-marginal-propensity-to-consume households, the resulting demand stimulus could increase labor *demand* enough to offset part of the supply-side reduction. Chrisp (2023) identifies this demand stimulus as one of the "productivist" expectations of UBI advocates, noting that redistribution to households with greater propensity to consume could "stimulate consumption (and GDP) through a fiscal stimulus."

**Job-lock reduction and skill complementarity.** By decoupling basic subsistence from employment, a universal UBI reduces job-lock — the phenomenon where workers remain in unsuitable positions solely to retain employer-linked benefits (especially health insurance in the U.S. context). This could increase labor-market mobility and job matching quality, potentially raising aggregate productivity. Chrisp (2023) flags this as a channel that RCTs cannot capture: under pilot conditions, treated individuals already have a financial cushion; under universality, the marginal job-lock reduction applies across the entire employed population, potentially changing quit rates, vacancy durations, and the distribution of talent across firms.

**Demand-side stimulus and inflation.** A deficit-financed UBI would raise aggregate demand, potentially creating inflationary pressure if the economy operates near capacity. A tax-financed UBI (e.g., through VAT or income-tax phase-outs) is demand-neutral in net terms but creates substitution effects through higher marginal tax rates. Luduvice's model shows that consumption-tax financing alone can generate welfare gains at low rates (1.3 pp in the expenditure-neutral case) but large employment losses at high rates (19.3 pp in the generous case), suggesting a non-linear relationship between fiscal scale and labor-supply distortion.

#### Permanent vs. transitional treatment: time-expectancy effects

All three primary pilots — Finland (2 years), Ontario (3 years, truncated), Stockton SEED (24 months) — were explicitly time-limited. Participants knew the transfer might end, which fundamentally alters labor-supply behavior relative to a permanent UBI. Under a 2-year pilot, a rational recipient facing a known termination date has stronger incentives to maintain job search behavior, accept employment, and build savings bridges to the post-pilot period. A permanent UBI, by contrast, would alter the entire lifecycle labor-leisure calculus: reduced urgency to accept the first available job, increased willingness to wait for better matches, potential reductions in hours worked across a broader swath of the population, and greater investment in education or caregiving. These "permanent income effects" are distinct from the "transitional effects" observable in pilots and represent a key reason why pilot employment findings may understate (or, in some cases, overstate) the long-run response. Chrisp (2023) explicitly identifies "the inability to capture long-term effects" as a "well-rehearsed problem" with field experiments.

#### Long-run analogue: the 19th-century Mothers' Pension program

Aizer, Eli, Ferrie, and Lleras-Muney (2016), in the *American Economic Review* (vol. 106, no. 4, pp. 935–71), exploit individual-level administrative records from applicants to the Mothers' Pension program — the first U.S. government-sponsored welfare program (1911–1935) — matched to census, WWII enlistment, and death records. They find that children of accepted applicants, relative to those of rejected mothers, had significantly better long-run outcomes: male children lived approximately one year longer, obtained one-third more years of schooling, were less likely to be underweight, and had higher income in adulthood. These intergenerational effects suggest that unconditional cash transfers to poor families can generate durable improvements in human capital and earnings capacity that compensate for any short-term labor-supply reduction among recipients themselves. While the Mothers' Pension was means-tested (not universal) and targeted widows and destitute mothers, the Aizer et al. evidence is relevant as a long-run demonstration that cash transfers do not produce the "dependency" effects critics predict and may instead invest in the next generation's productivity — a channel absent from pilot evaluations lasting 2–3 years.

#### Synthesis

The macro-modeling literature does not converge on a single labor-supply prediction; rather, it reveals a spectrum driven by design parameters — generosity, financing mechanism, and what existing programs the UBI replaces. Luduvice's (2024) OLG model produces both positive (+10 pp participation under expenditure-neutral reform) and negative (decline under generous consumption-tax-financed UBI) labor outcomes within a single framework, varying only the transfer level and fiscal scale. Hoynes and Rothstein (2019) emphasize that the UBI's labor-supply impact depends on the counterfactual policy it replaces, while also noting the "enormous expense" of a UBI generous enough to matter for poverty. Chrisp (2023) identifies an explicit research gap: RCTs are insufficiently informative about GE effects, and "more interrogation of the macro-effects of a UBI" is needed. The aggregate labor-supply elasticity benchmark of approximately 0.31 (Chetty 2012) suggests modest individual-level responses, but under universal coverage, GE wage compression, demand stimulus, reduced job-lock, and social-norm shifts could substantially amplify or counteract those micro-level signals. Pilot findings — Finland's near-zero employment effect, Stockton's 28%→40% FT employment increase — should therefore be treated as lower *and* upper bounds on national-scale behavior, not point estimates.

### Fiscal sustainability, macroeconomic modeling, and national-scale financing scenarios

Scaling UBI from pilot-tested interventions to a permanent national program transforms the fiscal question from a budgetary line item into a macroeconomic design problem involving tax-structure restructuring, demand-side multiplier effects, and intergenerational sustainability. This subsection synthesizes national-scale cost estimates, financing scenarios, and macroeconomic modeling evidence across Finland, Canada, and the United States, drawing on parliamentary budget-office analyses, central-bank working papers, and peer-reviewed macroeconomic reviews published post-2015.

#### Canada: PBO Federal GBI Cost Analysis (2021 → 2025)

The Parliamentary Budget Officer (PBO) of Canada has produced the most rigorous and iterative national-scale fiscal analysis of a guaranteed basic income among the three countries. Its February 2025 update (RP-2425-029-S) establishes that a national GBI using Ontario 2017 pilot parameters—guaranteeing up to 75% of the Low-Income Measure (LIM), amounting to $21,903 for a single person and $30,975 for a couple in 2025 dollars—would carry a **gross static cost of $107 billion** under the nuclear-family definition and **$53 billion** under the broader economic-family definition ([PBO 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)). The stark cost difference arises because the economic-family definition aggregates the earned income of all related household members, producing higher household income totals and thus greater GBI phase-outs.

The PBO assumes that existing federal and provincial tax credits targeting low-income individuals would be eliminated, and basic personal amounts reduced, to fully offset the gross cost—raising approximately $56 billion in federal and $50 billion in provincial tax revenues under the nuclear-family definition. The **only remaining net cost is the behavioural response**: $5 billion (nuclear) or $3.6 billion (economic family), reflecting estimated reductions in labour supply and consequent changes in tax revenues and GBI expenditures ([PBO 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)).

On poverty, the PBO estimates that the GBI would reduce Canadian poverty rates by **34% (nuclear family) and 40% (economic family)** against the Market Basket Measure (MBM) in 2025. The distributional impact is strongly progressive: the lowest income quintile gains approximately $6,100 on average (about 21% of average disposable income), while higher quintiles experience net losses from the elimination of tax credits combined with relatively smaller GBI transfers ([PBO 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)).

**What changed between PBO 2021 and 2025 [HP3]:** The 2021 baseline (RP-2122-001-S) projected a gross cost of $90 billion (nuclear family) and a **49% poverty reduction** against the MBM—substantially more optimistic than the 2025 update's 34%. Three factors drive this downward revision in projected poverty impact ([PBO 2021](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2122-001-S--distributional-fiscal-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income--analyse-financiere-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale); [PBO 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)):

1. **MBM threshold revision**: The MBM was rebased from a 2008 to a 2018 basket of goods, significantly raising the poverty threshold to reflect updated housing, food, and transportation costs. More families now qualify as living in poverty, making the GBI's measured poverty-reduction impact appear smaller even as absolute transfers are larger in nominal terms.
2. **Inflation and earnings stagnation**: Higher-than-anticipated inflation between 2021 and 2025 inflated the nominal benefit amount (from $16,989 to $21,903 for singles), but earnings of low-income families did not keep pace with the rising MBM threshold—meaning more families fall below the poverty line despite larger transfers.
3. **Economic-family definition added**: The 2021 analysis used only the nuclear-family definition. The 2025 update introduces the economic-family definition in response to fairness concerns raised by parliamentarians (including the introduction of Bill C-223 and Senate Bill S-233), roughly halving the gross cost to $53 billion.

The 2021 report also estimated labour-supply effects: a national reduction of **1.3% in hours worked** and **0.5% in payroll**, with behavioural costs of $3.1–3.3 billion per year for 2021–2026. By 2025, the behavioural cost rose to $5.0 billion, reflecting updated labour-supply elasticities and economic conditions. The PBO's earlier 2020 analysis also examined three phase-out scenarios ($0.50, $0.25, and $0.15 per dollar of earnings), with lower clawback rates producing higher gross costs but potentially smaller labour-supply disincentives ([PBO 2020 Blog](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/additional-analyses--analyses-complementaires/BLOG-2021-004--update-five-year-cost-estimate-guaranteed-basic-income--mise-jour-estimation-cinq-ans-cout-revenu-base-garanti)). The 2025 update maintains the $0.50 phase-out rate consistent with the Ontario pilot parameters.

#### United States: CTC Expansion as a Quasi-UBI Natural Experiment

The 2021 expansion of the Child Tax Credit (CTC) under the American Rescue Plan Act (ARA) created the largest near-universal cash-transfer program in U.S. history and functions as a quasi-UBI natural experiment. The expansion increased the credit from $2,000 to **$3,000 per child (ages 6–17) and $3,600 per child (ages 0–5)**, made it fully refundable, and distributed half as monthly payments from July through December 2021, reaching approximately **37 million families** ([Tax Policy Center](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/how-did-2021-american-rescue-plan-act-change-child-tax-credit); [Columbia CPSPM](https://povertycenter.columbia.edu/sites/povertycenter.columbia.edu/files/content/Publications/Expanded-CTC-and-Child-Poverty-in-2021-CPSP.pdf)).

The CTC expansion **cut the child poverty rate by 43%** — from 9.2% to 5.2% on the Supplemental Poverty Measure — moving 2.9 million children out of poverty and producing the lowest child poverty rate on record. Strikingly, about three-quarters of this reduction came from the *expansion* itself rather than the pre-existing credit structure, indicating that making the credit fully refundable and delivering it monthly was transformative for families previously "left behind" by partial refundability requirements ([Columbia CPSPM](https://povertycenter.columbia.edu/sites/povertycenter.columbia.edu/files/content/Publications/Expanded-CTC-and-Child-Poverty-in-2021-CPSP.pdf)).

On employment effects—the central anxiety surrounding cash-transfer programs—the evidence from CTC expansion is strongly reassuring. Ananat et al. (2022), using difference-in-differences with Current Population Survey and Census Pulse data from April to December 2021, found **"very small, inconsistently signed, and statistically insignificant impacts"** of the expanded CTC on both employment and labour-force participation among adults in households with children. Critically, labour-supply responses did not even differ for households for whom the CTC expansion had eliminated a previous work incentive (the pre-ARP phase-out structure), suggesting that concerns that near-universal cash transfers would discourage work may be overstated in practice ([Ananat et al. 2022, NBER w29823](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29823)).

On spending and multiplier effects, Schild et al. (2023) found that families spent approximately **$75 of each $100** in imputed CTC payments, directed primarily toward food ($28), housing ($31), and child-related goods and services ($15). Spending responses were larger for low-income, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic Black households—consistent with higher marginal propensities to consume among liquidity-constrained families and implying a meaningful demand-side multiplier from transferring resources to lower-income households ([Schild et al. 2023, NBER w31412](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31412)).

Whether these effects scale to a permanent, universal UBI remains an open question. The CTC expansion was temporary (six months of monthly payments), targeted to families with children (not truly universal), and layered atop an existing tax-credit structure rather than replacing a welfare system. The demand-side multiplier logic—transferring income to households with high marginal propensities to consume to stimulate aggregate demand—is portable to a UBI design, but the CTC's near-universal coverage among families with children means its employment effect estimates are not directly generalizable to a program covering all adults regardless of parental status.

#### U.S. Macroeconomic Modeling: Financing Scenarios and Divergent Projections

The Penn Wharton Budget Model (PWBM) provides the most granular dynamic-scoring analysis of a UBI in the U.S. context. PWBM models a $6,000-per-year adult UBI (costing approximately **$1.5 trillion annually**) under three financing scenarios ([Penn Wharton Budget Model 2018](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/legacy-briefs/2018-03-29-options-for-universal-basic-income-dynamic-modeling.pdf)):

- **Deficit-financed**: Federal debt rises **63.5% by 2027 and 81.1% by 2032**; GDP falls 6.1% by 2027 and 9.3% by 2032; hours worked decline 5.6–6.7%. The debt-driven crowding-out of private capital services is the central mechanism: government borrowing reduces capital formation, depressing output and payroll tax revenues (which fall 7.1–10.4%), further compounding the fiscal deterioration.
- **Payroll-tax-financed**: An **11.25 percentage-point payroll tax increase** finances the UBI, directly distorting labour-supply decisions through higher marginal tax rates.
- **Externally financed** (modeled as an Alaska-style resource dividend): Even without domestic tax distortions, GDP still declines because labour-supply reductions reduce output.

These projections stand in sharp contrast to the Roosevelt Institute's Keynesian macro-econometric model, which projected that a deficit-financed $6,000/year UBI would **increase GDP by up to 6.8% within eight years** through consumption stimulus. The divergence is not merely a normal scientific disagreement—it reflects fundamentally different model architectures. The Roosevelt model does not include a production function relating capital services and labour supply to GDP, does not model borrowing constraints at the household level, and assumes UBI transfers do not reduce labour supply. PWBM explicitly incorporates forward-looking household optimization, capital formation, and labour-supply responses, producing qualitatively opposite results ([Penn Wharton Budget Model 2018](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/legacy-briefs/2018-03-29-options-for-universal-basic-income-dynamic-modeling.pdf)).

The gap between Keynesian (demand-stimulated, multiplier-positive) and neoclassical/neoclassical-synthesis (supply-constrained, capital-crowding-out) projections illustrates the fundamental model-diversity problem that Chrisp (2023) identifies (discussed below).

#### Chrisp (2023): The Macroeconomic Modeling Review [HP1]

Chrisp's (2023) review in *Basic Income Studies* (vol. 18, no. 2, pp. 215–237) provides the most systematic assessment of the UBI macroeconomic-modeling literature to date. Its central finding is that **RCTs and microsimulation studies "tell us very little" about the general-equilibrium or community-level effects** that would arise under a national UBI, because they cannot capture how individuals interact when everyone receives a transfer—employers potentially raising wages if workers are less inclined to work, neighbours influencing each other's labour-market behaviour, or higher incomes for all low-income households leading to higher prices and rents ([Chrisp 2023, Bath repository](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/303250952/The_macroeconomic_effects_of_a_UBI_FINAL.pdf); [DOI: 10.1515/bis-2022-0027](https://doi.org/10.1515/bis-2022-0027)).

Chrisp identifies two sources of model diversity and uncertainty:

1. **Productivist vs. post-productivist theoretical priors**: Productivist models assume UBI reduces marginal effective tax rates, incentivizes employment, and stimulates consumption. Post-productivist models assume UBI provides an "exit option" from labour markets, increases bargaining power, and may reduce consumption through degrowth dynamics. These lead to fundamentally different predictions that cannot be resolved by pilot evidence because pilots do not test a true UBI at scale.

2. **Context-dependence**: A "tiger-sized" UBI funded through redistributive income taxation produces different effects than a "tabby-sized" UBI funded through sovereign money creation. Providing a UBI in a country with no social security system (or with a residual-welfare regime like the U.S.) yields different results than in a Nordic universal-welfare state like Finland.

Chrisp's review concludes with a call for greater diversity of models, mechanisms, and contexts in UBI macroeconomic research—noting that the absence of real-life national UBI examples heightens reliance on assumptions and makes any cost or distributional calculation "incomplete" without general-equilibrium analysis ([Chrisp 2023](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/303250952/The_macroeconomic_effects_of_a_UBI_FINAL.pdf)).

#### Finland: Fiscal Context

Finland's 2017–2018 basic income experiment was a partial, unemployment-targeted intervention (€560/month for 2,000 unemployed recipients), not a universal program. A national UBI at that benefit level applied to all Finnish adults (~4.4 million) would cost roughly €29–30 billion annually before behavioural responses and offsets—approximately 13–14% of Finland's GDP. However, no parliamentary budget-office equivalent has published a formal distributional and macroeconomic analysis of a full Finnish national UBI comparable to Canada's PBO reports. The Chrisp (2023) review notes that a UBI in a Nordic social-democratic context with an existing universal welfare state would have very different fiscal and labour-market effects than in a residual-welfare regime, because much of the UBI would replace existing benefit payments rather than constitute new spending. The net cost would be substantially lower than the gross cost, but the marginal-tax-rate requirements for the residual amount would depend on Finland's already-high tax-to-GDP ratio (approximately 43%) and the structure of existing social transfers ([Chrisp 2023](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/303250952/The_macroeconomic_effects_of_a_UBI_FINAL.pdf)).

#### Inflation Risk: Deficit-Financed vs. Tax-Financed UBI

The inflationary consequences of UBI depend critically on the financing mechanism. Deficit-financed UBI schemes inject new purchasing power without corresponding supply-side increases, creating classic demand-pull inflation risk. The Penn Wharton Budget Model's projection that deficit-financed UBI would crowd out private capital and reduce GDP through the debt channel implicitly assumes that the Federal Reserve would accommodatively monetize some of the additional debt, generating inflationary pressure ([Penn Wharton 2018](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/legacy-briefs/2018-03-29-options-for-universal-basic-income-dynamic-modeling.pdf)).

Tax-financed schemes, by contrast, are less inflationary in the short run because they redistribute existing purchasing power rather than creating new net demand. However, the PBO's Canadian analysis shows that even with full elimination of existing tax credits and reduction of basic personal amounts, the **behavioural cost** (from reduced labour supply lowering tax revenues while raising GBI expenditures) creates a permanent net fiscal drain. The PBO's 2025 estimate of $5.0 billion in behavioural costs under the nuclear-family definition means that even a fully tax-offset GBI is not fiscally-neutral: it generates a structural revenue loss proportional to the labour-supply response ([PBO 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)).

VAT-based financing scenarios, often proposed in Australian and U.S. discussions, would be moderately inflationary in the short run (as consumption taxes raise prices) but less so than pure deficit financing, since VAT revenues are collected contemporaneously. However, VAT is regressive, partially offsetting the progressive distributional impact of the UBI transfer itself—a tradeoff that no pilot has tested empirically.

#### Aging Populations and Intergenerational Fiscal Sustainability

All three countries face demographic headwinds that compound UBI's long-term fiscal challenge. Across the OECD, the share of population aged 65 and over has more than doubled between 1960 and 2022, reaching approximately 18% on average ([OECD 2025](https://oecdecoscope.blog/2025/11/07/the-fiscal-impact-of-population-ageing-how-can-we-afford-getting-older/)). Finland is among the most rapidly aging: Kotämäki (2025) finds that Finland's rapid aging has **reduced GDP per capita and productivity substantially** over the past decade while **general government debt has increased significantly**, constraining fiscal space for new permanent transfers ([Kotämäki 2025, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0313592625004345)). Canada faces similar demographic pressures through its public pension and healthcare systems, while the U.S. confronts the combined strain of Medicare and Social Security solvency challenges. The Penn Wharton model's finding that UBI reduces Social Security payroll tax revenues by 7.1–10.4% by 2027–2032 (by shrinking the labour tax base) illustrates how UBI could **exacerbate** rather than alleviate the fiscal strain on age-entitlement programs ([Penn Wharton 2018](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/legacy-briefs/2018-03-29-options-for-universal-basic-income-dynamic-modeling.pdf)).

### Political sustainability and comparative long-term consequences synthesis

The post-2015 pilot wave has generated rigorous evidence on short-run effectiveness, but the political durability of UBI — whether it can survive electoral cycles, fiscal scrutiny, and ideological contestation — remains contested across all three countries. This subsection synthesizes the political-durability evidence as a first-class long-term consequence, drawing on cancellation events, referendum outcomes, coalition-building dynamics, public-opinion shifts, and historical analogues to assess whether UBI faces structural headwinds that pilot evidence alone cannot resolve.

#### Ontario's cancellation and the political vulnerability of GBI programs

The Ontario Basic Income Pilot, launched in April 2017 by the Liberal government of Premier Kathleen Wynne, was terminated in July 2018 after the Progressive Conservative (PC) government led by Doug Ford took power following a change in government. The Ford administration announced it would "focus resources on more proven approaches" instead of continuing the experiment, which was estimated to cost approximately $115 million over its full three-year duration ([PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/economy/making-sense/ontario-is-canceling-its-basic-income-experiment)). The cancellation came mid-run — only about one year into the planned three-year pilot — and effectively eliminated the possibility of a final evaluation. Dr. Kwame McKenzie, a special adviser to the pilot, observed that "even when you think you have cross-party support, such as in the Ontario Basic Income Pilot, political realities can change" ([PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/economy/making-sense/ontario-is-canceling-its-basic-income-experiment)). Notably, a PC spokesperson had previously indicated that the new government would continue the program and look forward to seeing the results, underscoring how a political reversal can rapidly dismantle a pilot regardless of prior bipartisan rhetoric.

The cancellation revealed structural vulnerabilities inherent to GBI pilots operating within short electoral cycles. First, a pilot launched by one government lacks institutional protection against a successor government opposed to the policy. Second, the cancellation eliminated the final evaluation data — researchers at Carleton's Centre for Social and Cultural Preservation (CSPSC) launched emergency efforts to preserve participant-recorded data, but the systematic quantitative evaluation was lost ([Carleton CSPSC](https://carleton.ca/cspsc/ontario-basic-income-pilot/)). Third, the cancellation demonstrated that GBI programs can become politically framed as extensions of a specific party's agenda rather than as policy experiments serving the public interest. The CCPA characterized the cancellation as inflicting "structural violence" on participants who had come to rely on the income floor ([CCPA](https://www.policyalternatives.ca/news-research/basic-income-cancellation-impacts-on-ontario/)). The Bristol University Press *Journal of Public Safety and Justice* article on "refusal power and policy credibility" further frames the cancellation as an exercise of state power that undermines policy credibility — citizens who participated in good faith were left without recourse when the political sponsor changed ([Bristol UP, JPSJ](https://bristoluniversitypressdigital.com/abstract/journals/jpsj/34/2/article-p109.xml)).

#### The 2016 Swiss referendum: a landmark case of political feasibility failure

On June 5, 2016, Switzerland held the world's first national referendum on a universal basic income. The initiative, which gathered over 100,000 citizen signatures to qualify for the ballot under Switzerland's direct-democratic popular initiative system, proposed an unconditional basic income of 2,500 Swiss francs per month for adults (approximately $2,555 at the time). The proposal was overwhelmingly rejected: **76.9% of voters opposed** the measure, with only 23.1% in favor ([BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-36454060)). Not a single Swiss parliamentary party endorsed the initiative. Critics argued that disconnecting the link between work and money would be detrimental to society, and right-wing parliamentarian Luzi Stamm warned that with open borders, a Swiss-scale UBI would attract immigration from around the world ([BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-36454060)).

The Swiss result is instructive for political feasibility analysis. It demonstrated that even in a country renowned for direct democracy and high institutional trust, a national UBI — absent any pilot evidence of its own — could not overcome deep-seated work-norm ideology, fiscal conservatism, and concerns about immigration. The referendum occurred before any of the post-2015 pilots (Finland, Stockton, Ontario) had reported results, meaning the Swiss vote was cast in an evidentiary vacuum. Yet the result also reveals that **positive small-scale evidence may be insufficient** to shift national opinion: Finland, which had launched its own experiment earlier in 2017, still could not produce a pro-UBI majority in Switzerland. This suggests that structural political headwinds — fiscal cost, work-norm expectations, and the absence of organized political champions — operate independently of pilot outcomes.

#### U.S. municipal coalition-building without a federal mandate

The United States has pursued UBI primarily through municipal-level pilots rather than national experimentation. Mayor Michael Tubbs of Stockton founded **Mayors for a Guaranteed Income (MGI)** in June 2020, initially with approximately 30 mayors from cities across the political spectrum, including Keisha Lance Bottoms (Atlanta), Chokwe Antar Lumumba (Jackson), and Melvin Carter (St. Paul) ([Cities Today](https://cities-today.com/us-mayors-form-coalition-for-guaranteed-income/); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/01/19/the-mayors-piloting-guaranteed-income-programs-across-the-us.html)). By mid-2024, MGI had grown to approximately 160 mayors across almost every state ([BIEN](https://basicincome.org/news/2024/06/four-years-of-u-s-mayors-for-a-guaranteed-income/)). MGI, in partnership with the University of Pennsylvania School of Social Policy & Practice, established the Center for Guaranteed Income Research to consolidate pilot learnings and build toward a federal policy ([MGI](https://mayorsforagi.org/)).

Despite this impressive coalition growth, the U.S. landscape remains constrained by several structural factors. First, there is **no federal mandate** for a UBI, and the polarized federal landscape — particularly Republican opposition — makes legislation unlikely in the near term. Second, the coalition is predominantly composed of progressive-Democratic mayors, which limits UBI's perceived bipartisan appeal. Third, most U.S. pilots are small-N, short-duration, and designed to build political coalitions rather than produce rigorous national-scale evidence. The Stanford Basic Income Lab's umbrella review (Hasdell 2020) confirms that "a fully universal and unconditional basic income has never been implemented at scale" and that most pilots are not universal by design; see S1.1 for the full cross-pilot synthesis.

#### Finland's political legacy: cross-party support for the experiment, silence after

Finland's basic income experiment enjoyed cross-party support at its inception in 2017, launched under Prime Minister Juha Sipilä's centre-right government as a key project of the governmental program ([Intereconomics](https://www.intereconomics.eu/contents/year/2017/number/2/article/basic-income-in-the-finnish-context.html)). The experiment was understood as a signal that Finland was exploring basic income as a policy option for social security reform ([Hiilamo 2022](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/files/232276909/13882627221104501.pdf)). Crucially, unlike Ontario, the Finnish experiment was allowed to complete its planned two-year duration — it was not cancelled mid-run ([Finland Toolbox](https://toolbox.finland.fi/life-society/finlands-basic-income-experiment-2017-2018/); [Government of Finland](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/suomen-perustulokokeilu-jatkuu-suunnitelmien-mukaisesti)).

However, after the experiment concluded in December 2018, neither Sipilä's government nor the succeeding Social Democratic governments of Prime Ministers Antti Rinne and Sanna Marin pursued permanent adoption of a national basic income. The Rinne/Marin government scrapped the controversial 2018 activation model that had contaminated the experiment's control group but did not act on broader social security reform along basic income lines ([Hiilamo 2022](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/files/232276909/13882627221104501.pdf)). This silence suggests that even in a Nordic social-democratic context with high institutional trust and welfare-state capacity, the political incentive to convert a pilot into permanent legislation was absent. The cross-party support that sustained the experiment did not extend to permanent policy adoption, indicating that pilot evidence — even positive well-being and small employment effects — does not automatically generate legislative momentum.

Three patterns are politically significant. First, **support is conditional**: Canadians express strong abstract support for UBI (75% in Gallup's survey) but that support erodes sharply when fiscal trade-offs are introduced — only 49% are willing to pay higher taxes, and a majority (54%) doubt Canada can afford it ([Angus Reid 2020](https://angusreid.org/universal-basic-income-covid19/); [PMC 7249249](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7249249/)). Second, in the U.S., **UBI opinion is intensely polarized by partisanship**, with 78% of Republicans opposed and 66% of Democrats in favor ([Pew 2020](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/08/19/more-americans-oppose-than-favor-the-government-providing-a-universal-basic-income-for-all-adult-citizens/)). This partisanship makes UBI a wedge issue rather than a consensus policy. Third, **pilot evidence has not yet moved opinion in a pro-UBI direction**: the 2020 U.S. polling was conducted after the Stockton SEED preliminary results were publicized, yet opposition still exceeds support among the general population. The age gradient (67% of under-30s favor, 72% of 65+ oppose) suggests that UBI's political future may depend on generational replacement of the electorate rather than on evidence from existing pilots.

#### Long-run constituency-building analogues

The 19th-century Mothers' Pension program (Aizer et al. 2016) provides a historical analogue for how cash-transfer programs can create durable political constituencies and generate positive intergenerational effects — including improved longevity, education, and adult income for children of recipients. This evidence is discussed in detail in S3.1 as a long-run labor-market and intergenerational-effects reference point; the U.S CTC expansion of 2021 also illustrates the tension between demonstrable poverty reduction and political fragility (discussed in S3.2).

#### Structural political headwinds across all three countries

Several structural headwinds constrain UBI's political sustainability regardless of pilot evidence quality:

1. **Short electoral cycles**: Ontario's cancellation after a change in government (June 2018) is the paradigmatic case. Four-year electoral cycles mean that pilots launched by one government can be terminated by a successor, eliminating both the evaluation and the policy itself. As discussed in S3.1, Hoynes and Rothstein (2019) argue that pilots cannot capture the general-equilibrium and political-economy dynamics that determine whether a policy would survive and function at scale ([NBER w25538](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25538)).

2. **Fiscal conservatism**: A UBI large enough to increase transfers to low-income families would, in Hoynes and Rothstein's assessment, be "enormously expensive" — requiring either substantial tax increases or the replacement of existing safety-net programs, each politically contentious ([NBER w25538](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25538)). The PBO's repeated analyses (2021, 2025) signal sustained Canadian institutional interest, but the 2025 update shows that poverty reduction declined from 49% to 34% (nuclear family) / 40% (economic family) as economic conditions changed — demonstrating that fiscal feasibility is parameter-sensitive and contested ([PBO 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)).

3. **Work-norm ideology**: The Swiss referendum's decisive defeat, driven partly by the perceived threat to the work-money nexus, illustrates that cultural and ideological commitment to conditional welfare — that benefits should be contingent on work or need — remains a powerful political force. In the U.S., this manifests as the persistent political framing of means-tested programs versus universal transfers, with the latter stigmatized as "government handouts" ([Pew 2020](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/08/19/more-americans-oppose-than-favor-the-government-providing-a-universal-basic-income-for-all-adult-citizens/)).

4. **Absence of a durable pro-UBI coalition**: In none of the three countries has a durable, cross-party coalition emerged with sufficient legislative force to advance a permanent UBI. Finland's experiment enjoyed initial cross-party support but produced no follow-through legislation; Canada's pilot was cancelled by an opposing government; the U.S. coalition is anchored in progressive municipal politics with no federal mandate. On the need for macroeconomic modeling to better incorporate political-economy constraints — the institutional mechanisms and interest-group dynamics that determine whether a pilot can transition to permanent policy — see S3.2 (Chrisp 2023, *Basic Income Studies*).

#### Political durability across welfare regimes

The three countries' welfare-regime contexts shape political-durability prospects through different mechanisms. **Finland** (social-democratic/Nordic) offers the most favorable backdrop: high institutional trust, a universal welfare state, cross-party support for the experiment, and completion of the full two-year design. Yet even here, the absence of a permanent-adoption push after 2018 suggests that the Nordic welfare state's existing architecture reduces rather than increases the urgency of UBI — the existing safety net may be perceived as sufficient. **Canada** (liberal welfare regime) presents a mixed picture: the Ontario cancellation demonstrates vulnerability to partisan reversal, but the PBO's repeated analyses (2021→2025) and the introduction of House Bill C-223 and Senate Bill S-233 — which propose a national framework for a guaranteed livable basic income — signal sustained institutional interest at the federal level despite provincial-level political rejection ([PBO 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)). **The United States** (residual welfare regime) faces the steepest political barriers: a weak safety net, intense partisanship, no federal mandate, and a polarized public (54% oppose, 45% favor). The municipal coalition-building strategy through MGI represents a plausible long-game approach, but it has not yet produced legislative momentum beyond the local level ([MGI](https://mayorsforagi.org/)).

The synthesis reveals a cross-national pattern: **financially, UBI is most feasible in Finland and Canada** (existing welfare-state infrastructure, lower fiscal gap, and institutional capacity for tax-benefit integration), while **politically, all three countries face structural headwinds** — short electoral cycles, fiscal conservatism, work-norm ideology, and the absence of durable cross-party pro-UBI coalitions — that pilot evidence alone has not overcome. The PBO's repeated analysis suggests that institutional memory and technocratic capacity can sustain a policy idea even after political rejection, but converting that capacity into legislation requires a political window that has not yet opened in any of the three countries. The 2021 U.S. CTC expansion — which provided $3,000/$3,600 per child to 37 million families and was allowed to expire after one year — illustrates both the promise (rapid poverty reduction) and the fragility (political expiration without legislative codification) of cash-transfer expansion in a polarized environment ([CBPP](https://www.cbpp.org/research/federal-tax/expanding-child-tax-benefit-would-substantially-reduce-child-poverty)).

The long-term political trajectory of UBI will depend less on whether pilots demonstrate well-being and health gains — evidence that is now robust and convergent — than on whether a durable political coalition can be constructed to translate pilot evidence into permanent legislation, and whether fiscal conditions constrain the opportunity. The Ontario cancellation, the Swiss referendum failure, and Finland's post-experiment legislative silence collectively suggest that **pilot evidence is necessary but not sufficient** for political sustainability. What remains absent in all three countries is the kind of sustained, cross-party, institutionally embedded advocacy that characterized the historical expansion of Social Security or the Nordic welfare state — a political force capable of converting evidence into durable policy.
