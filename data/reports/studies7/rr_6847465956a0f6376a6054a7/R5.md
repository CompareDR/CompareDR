# Universal Basic Income in Developed Countries: Effectiveness and Long-Term Consequences of the Finland, Canada, and United States Experiments

## Executive Summary

Between 2017 and 2023, three developed countries ran the most closely watched cash-transfer experiments of the modern era: **Finland's national randomized controlled trial** (2017–2018), **Canada's Ontario Basic Income Pilot** (2017–2019, cancelled mid-stream), and a wave of **U.S. experiments** ranging from the Stockton Economic Empowerment Demonstration (2019–2021) to the large OpenResearch Unconditional Cash Transfer study (2020–2023), alongside the long-running quasi-experimental evidence of the Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend. Read together, the evidence supports a consistent, if nuanced, verdict: **unconditional cash meaningfully improves mental health, perceived economic security, and life satisfaction, while its effects on employment are small in either direction** — slightly positive in small targeted programs such as Stockton, essentially zero in Finland's first year and in Alaska, and modestly negative (roughly **−2 percentage points** of labour-force participation) in the largest and most rigorous U.S. experiment to date ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20200143), [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719)).

The "effectiveness" of UBI therefore depends heavily on which outcome is weighted. As an **income-floor and wellbeing instrument**, the pilots performed well across all three countries. As an **employment-activation tool**, results are mixed and context-dependent. As a **fiscally sustainable national policy**, none of the pilots can answer the decisive questions: microsimulation and macroeconomic modelling suggest a full UBI would cost on the order of **$3 trillion per year in the United States** (about 75% of 2017 federal expenditures) and **C$85–107 billion gross per year in Canada**, with distributional outcomes that depend entirely on financing design ([Conversable Economist](https://conversableeconomist.com/2019/09/10/universal-basic-income-combined-with-what-else/), [PBO](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)). The long-term consequences — labour-market structure, inflation, political economy, and intergenerational effects — remain genuinely uncertain, because pilots are by construction temporary, partial, and unfunded by the distortionary taxes a real UBI would require ([Ralph Bunche Institute](https://ralphbuncheinstitute.org/where-is-the-ubi-movement-at-right-now-with-karl-widerquist/)).

## 1. Scope, Definitions, and What Experiments Can and Cannot Show

"Universal Basic Income" is used loosely in public debate. The experiments reviewed here are almost all **guaranteed-income or negative-income-tax-style interventions** — cash paid with no work requirement, but targeted at unemployed or low-income people rather than paid universally. A strict UBI has five properties: it is **periodic, cash, individual, universal, and unconditional**. Finland's experiment paid **€560 per month** to 2,000 randomly selected unemployed persons — unconditional but not universal ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20200143)). Ontario paid up to **C$16,989 per year** to low-income adults aged 18–64 with a 50% benefit-reduction rate — closer to a negative income tax ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30965/w30965.pdf)). Only Alaska's Permanent Fund Dividend is genuinely universal, and it is small (typically **$1,000–2,000 per person per year**) ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190299)). This gap between the label and the design matters for every inference drawn below.

This report restricts itself to **peer-reviewed economic studies and post-2015 expert policy analysis** concerning developed countries, with primary attention to Finland, Canada, and the United States. The evidence hierarchy runs from randomized controlled trials (Finland; Stockton; OpenResearch's Unconditional Cash Transfer study, hereafter ORUS) through synthetic-control quasi-experiments (Alaska), saturation-site retrospective analysis (Manitoba's Mincome experiment in Dauphin, Canada), post-hoc surveys of a cancelled RCT (Ontario), to microsimulation costings (Canada's Parliamentary Budget Officer; the OECD's TaxBen model) and structural macroeconomic modelling ([Campbell](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/), [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304393224000680)). A 2024 Campbell Collaboration systematic review screened the global literature and retained only **27 studies of 10 experiments** meeting minimum quality standards — 25 of them still carrying a high risk of bias — which should temper any strong claim made about "what UBI does" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)).

The timeline below situates the key interventions. Two features stand out: the modern wave is strikingly **short-duration** (two to three years), and the Canadian experience spans both the oldest saturation experiment (Mincome, 1974–1979) and the most abrupt political cancellation (Ontario, 2018) — a pairing that previews the political-economy risks discussed in Section 6.

Timeline of major basic income experiments in developed countries

A methodological caveat from economist Karl Widerquist frames the entire report: pilots test **temporary, partial, non-universal transfers paid for by nobody**, while a real UBI is **permanent, universal, and financed by distortionary taxation**, and no experiment on the first can identify the effects of the second — particularly general-equilibrium effects on wages, prices, and labour-market structure ([Ralph Bunche Institute](https://ralphbuncheinstitute.org/where-is-the-ubi-movement-at-right-now-with-karl-widerquist/)). Similarly, Hoynes and Rothstein's review of the U.S. evidence concludes that no existing cash-transfer programme or experiment approximates a full UBI closely enough to measure its long-run consequences, and that simulations based on current behavioural elasticities are the only available tool ([Conversable Economist](https://conversableeconomist.com/2019/09/10/universal-basic-income-combined-with-what-else/)). With those limits stated, the experiments still deliver the best evidence available on the household-level margins — health, wellbeing, short-run labour supply — and it is to those results we now turn.

## 2. Finland (2017–2018): The National Randomized Experiment

### 2.1 Design and institutional context

Finland's experiment remains the only **nation-state-run RCT** of an unconditional basic income. From a sampling frame of persons receiving unemployment benefits in November 2016, the social insurance institution Kela randomly assigned **2,000 people to receive €560 per month, tax-free, unconditionally**, retaining the payment even if they took a job; a control group of roughly 173,000 unemployed persons remained in the standard unemployment-insurance system ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20200143)). Because the payment replaced basic unemployment allowances euro-for-euro, its behavioural bite came not from extra money on day one but from **removing the benefit-withdrawal penalty on earnings**: the participation tax rate facing the treatment group fell by 23 percentage points, and the average effective marginal tax rate on accepting work dropped from roughly **66% to 43%** ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20200143)).

The experiment was embedded in a shifting political environment that complicated its interpretation. It was launched under a centre-right coalition as a work-incentive test rather than a redistributive programme, and — critically — in **2018 the government simultaneously introduced an "activation model"** that cut unemployment benefits for both treatment and control groups who failed to meet new activity requirements, muddying the second year's comparison ([STM](https://stm.fi/en/-/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)). Political-science analysis of the episode notes that the pilot was designed more to resolve bureaucratic interest in incentive reform than to test UBI as a social paradigm, and that expectations management failed when the public read it as a verdict on basic income itself ([Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13882627221104501)).

### 2.2 Employment effects: near zero on average, positive for some groups

The register-based evaluation by Verho, Hämäläinen, and Kanninen, published in *AEJ: Economic Policy* (2022), is the definitive result. In the first experimental year, treated individuals worked **49.6 days versus 49.3 days** for controls — a statistically insignificant difference — indicating that removing the work disincentive did not measurably raise employment on average ([EC ESPN](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)). Over the longer window from November 2017 to October 2018, the treatment group averaged about **78 employment days, roughly six days more** than controls, but the authors themselves caution that the 2018 activation model confounds this estimate and the causal attribution is uncertain ([STM](https://stm.fi/en/-/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi)).

Subgroup analysis revealed more texture beneath the flat average. Employment responses were **positive for foreign-language speakers and for families with children** — the latter gaining an estimated 13.7 days, though not statistically significant — which the evaluators interpret as evidence that the unconditional payment eased specific barriers (childcare costs, benefit bureaucracy) for groups facing the steepest effective tax rates on work ([EC ESPN](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)). The policy lesson cuts both ways: a basic income layered on top of an existing benefit system produces only marginal incentive changes for the median recipient, so the observed null is a statement about **Finland's replacement-level design**, not about cash per se.

### 2.3 Wellbeing effects: large, robust, and policy-relevant

Where Finland's experiment moved the needle decisively was subjective wellbeing. In the end-of-experiment survey, the treatment group reported **life satisfaction of 7.3 versus 6.8** on an eleven-point scale; **16.6% versus 25.0%** experienced mental strain (depression, sadness, nervousness); **38.6% versus 48.6%** reported economic stress; and **58.2% versus 46.2%** expressed confidence in their future ([EC ESPN](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en), [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/social-sector/our-insights/an-experiment-to-inform-universal-basic-income)). A peer-reviewed analysis in the medical literature estimated the odds of experiencing mental strain in the treatment group at **0.61 times** those of controls (95% CI 0.45–0.83) ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12555240/)).

Two caveats qualify — but do not erase — these gains. First, the wellbeing results rest on **survey response rates of only 31% (treatment) and 20% (control)**, with no baseline survey, so differential non-response cannot be ruled out ([Elgar](https://www.elgaronline.com/downloadpdf/monochap-oa/book/9781800880801/book-part-9781800880801-12.pdf)). Second, some of the gain plausibly reflects the **administrative relief of escaping conditionality bureaucracy** rather than the money itself, which is arguably a feature rather than a bias for policy design. Even with these qualifications, the Finnish wellbeing effect sizes are the strongest in the developed-country experimental record and are directionally consistent with Stockton, Ontario, and the systematic-review evidence (Section 5).

### 2.4 How Finland changed the policy conversation

Finland's greatest long-term consequence may be **demonstrative rather than behavioural**. The experiment proved a modern welfare state can randomize its core safety net, link tax and population registers for near-perfect outcome measurement, and survive the politics of doing so — while also showing how quickly political winds shift: the centre-right government declined to extend the experiment beyond its legislated window, and subsequent Finnish policy debate moved toward activation rather than universality ([Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13882627221104501)). Modelling by the OECD underscores why: replacing Finland's existing benefits with a budget-neutral basic income would produce **drastic redistribution away from current beneficiaries and would likely increase poverty**, because Finland's system is already comparatively generous at the bottom ([Banca d'Italia/OECD](https://www.bancaditalia.it/pubblicazioni/altri-atti-convegni/2018-workshop-public-finance/2-Paralliussen-paper.pdf?language_id=1)). The pilot's null employment result and positive wellbeing result thus landed in a fiscal context where scaling up was never arithmetic-neutral, and the experiment ended as an evidence contribution rather than a policy bridge.

The international spillovers were nonetheless substantial. Finland's two-year window became the reference design for the global policy conversation — cited in the Ontario design documents, echoed in the U.S. mayoral pilots' unconditional-cash framing, and used by both advocates (the wellbeing gains) and skeptics (the employment null) as their preferred Exhibit A ([Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13882627221104501)). This selective appropriation previews a recurring pattern across all three countries: because the experiments bundle income, incentives, and deconditionality into one treatment, nearly any policy position can find a Finland-compatible data point, which raises the premium on reading the full evaluation rather than its headlines ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20200143), [EC ESPN](https://ec.europa.eu/social/BlobServlet?docId=20846&langId=en)).

## 3. Canada: A Cancelled Trial and a Rediscovered One

### 3.1 The Ontario Basic Income Pilot (2017–2019): ambitious design, abrupt termination

Ontario's pilot was, on paper, the most generous and best-powered North American experiment. Launched in 2017 across Hamilton, Lindsay, and Thunder Bay, it enrolled roughly **4,000 treatment participants and 2,000 comparison participants**, offering low-income adults aged 18–64 up to **C$16,989 per year (single) or C$24,027 (couple)**, plus a C$500 monthly disability supplement, taxed back at **50% of earned income** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30965/w30965.pdf), [Lakehead University](https://www.lakeheadu.ca/about/news-and-events/news/archive/2021/node/62714)). The design team explicitly built in randomization and a comparison community architecture intended to support rigorous evaluation over three years.

In July 2018, a newly elected provincial government announced the pilot's cancellation; payments stopped in March 2019, after roughly C$150 million of a planned three-year budget had been committed, and no official evaluation was ever produced ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30965/w30965.pdf), [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/bis-2021-0021/html)). Guy Standing's analysis of the cancellation frames it as a cautionary tale in the **political fragility of pilots**: an experiment whose results would arrive after the electoral cycle that created it carries no constituency to defend it, and its termination wastes both money and the trust of participants who had reorganized their lives around three years of promised income ([De Gruyter](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/bis-2021-0021/html)).

### 3.2 What Ontario showed anyway: post-hoc evidence

Because the randomized design was destroyed, Ontario's evidence comes from researcher-led surveys and interviews of former participants — methodologically weaker, but substantively consistent with the RCT record elsewhere. The McMaster/Hamilton Roundtable study ("Southern Ontario's Basic Income Experience") surveyed **217 former participants and interviewed 40**: about **80% reported improved health**, roughly **three-quarters of those employed before the pilot kept working**, and among the continuously employed, **37% reported higher pay** while in the pilot; of those who stopped working, **40.6% used the income to return to school or training** ([McMaster](https://labourstudies.mcmaster.ca/app/uploads/2024/06/southern-ontarios-basic-income-experience.pdf)). Around **70% reported skipping fewer meals**, and **79% said the income made them more motivated to find better work** ([McMaster](https://labourstudies.mcmaster.ca/app/uploads/2024/06/southern-ontarios-basic-income-experience.pdf)).

A second peer-reviewed analysis of the pilot's Hamilton site reported a **small decline in labour-force participation alongside large quality-of-life gains**, and qualitative work with participants documented a recurring theme captured in one interviewee's phrase "I was trusted for once": the removal of surveillance and conditionality itself generated dignity and planning capacity ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/bistud/v16y2021i2p209-256n2.html), [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/social-policy-and-society/article/i-was-trusted-for-once-imagining-more-humane-income-supports-through-the-ontario-basic-income-pilot/C92C2564BF8BF871A8BBF337CB574AA4)). The Ontario case thus answers a narrow question — what do low-income people do with an unconditional C$17,000 floor? — with "mostly keep working, eat more regularly, and invest in health and education," while leaving the RCT-level causal questions permanently unanswered.

### 3.3 Mincome (Dauphin, Manitoba, 1974–1979): the saturation-site benchmark

Canada's older experiment supplies the only **whole-town saturation** evidence in the developed-world record. In Dauphin, Manitoba, any resident could receive a guaranteed annual income, allowing researchers to observe community-level rather than merely individual effects. Evelyn Forget's reanalysis of administrative health data found hospitalization rates fell by about **8.5%** relative to controls — driven by accidents, injuries, and mental-health admissions — and high-school continuation rates improved markedly, on the order of **15% higher grade 11/12 enrollment** ([Progress.org](https://www.progress.org/wiki/forget-mincome-town-with-no-poverty/), [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)).

Labour-supply reanalyses found modest reductions: Hum and Simpson estimated hours-worked declines of about **1% for men, 3% for married women, and 5% for unmarried women**, concentrated among new mothers and adolescents staying in school; Calnitsky and Latner's later structural estimate found a larger **11.3 percentage-point participation response**, attributing roughly 30% of it to social-multiplier effects visible only because Dauphin was saturated ([Progress.org](https://www.progress.org/wiki/forget-mincome-town-with-no-poverty/), [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). A 2022 reanalysis in *Canadian Public Policy* has, however, disputed the hospitalization finding on econometric grounds, and the Campbell review rates the Mincome evidence base as high risk of bias — a reminder that even the canonical historical result rests on contested ground ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). Calnitsky's later work also documented lower crime in Dauphin during the saturation period, suggesting community-level spillovers that dispersed pilots cannot detect.

### 3.4 Canada's fiscal reckoning: PBO costings and the BC Expert Panel

Canada has gone further than any other country in costing a national programme. The Parliamentary Budget Officer estimated in 2021 that an Ontario-style guaranteed basic income would cost **C$85 billion gross in 2021–22, rising to C$93 billion by 2025–26**, cut **poverty by roughly half** on the Market Basket Measure, raise the disposable income of the lowest quintile by **17.5%**, and reduce hours worked by about **1.3%** — a behavioural cost of only **C$3.0–3.3 billion per year** ([PBO](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2122-001-S--distributional-fiscal-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income--analyse-financiere-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale)). The 2025 update, reflecting the expanded Canada Workers Benefit, put the gross cost at **C$107 billion (nuclear-family basis)** with poverty reduced **34–40%** and behavioural costs of C$3.6–5 billion ([PBO](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)).

The British Columbia Expert Panel (Green, Kesselman, and Tedds, 2021) reached a strikingly different policy conclusion from similar arithmetic: a poverty-line basic income for BC alone would cost about **C$52 billion per year** — roughly doubling the provincial budget — and the panel **recommended against both a UBI and further pilots**, arguing instead for 65 targeted reforms to the existing system, on the grounds that universality is an inefficient way to reach the minority of households in deep poverty ([Government of BC](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/governments/about-the-bc-government/strategies/poverty-reduction-strategy/basic-income-report)). Basic-income scholars responded that the panel applied a static lens and understated dynamic benefits (health savings, human capital, reduced crisis-service use), and the exchange remains the sharpest expert disagreement in the Canadian literature ([Bristol University Press](https://bristoluniversitypressdigital.com/view/journals/jpsj/32/3/article-p443.xml)).

## 4. United States: Small Pilots, Big Experiments, and a 40-Year Natural Experiment

### 4.1 Stockton SEED (2019–2021): the pilot that launched a movement

The Stockton Economic Empowerment Demonstration gave **$500 per month for 24 months to 131 residents** of low-income neighbourhoods, with 200 controls, and became the template for the mayoral guaranteed-income movement. The peer-reviewed results (West and Castro, *Journal of Urban Health*, 2023) found treated participants experienced **less income volatility (19% versus 26% experiencing month-to-month swings in year one)** and lower psychological distress on the Kessler K10 scale before the pandemic period; notably, **most treatment effects faded after the payments ended**, suggesting the mechanism is the ongoing income floor rather than a permanent change in circumstances ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160253/)).

SEED's headline labour-market result — full-time employment rising from **28% to 40%** among recipients versus a five-percentage-point rise among controls — is directionally pro-employment and was widely cited as rebutting the "cash makes people idle" hypothesis ([SSRC](https://ssrc-static.s3.amazonaws.com/moa/APortraitofCalifornia2021-2022.pdf)). The caveat is scale: 131 recipients in one city, with pandemic-era conditions contaminating the second year, cannot identify population effects. Its deeper legacy is institutional: SEED spawned **Mayors for a Guaranteed Income**, which grew to roughly 150 member cities with about 60 MGI-affiliated pilots; spending data across these pilots show recipients directing about **35% of funds to retail purchases and 32% to food** — overwhelmingly basic consumption rather than temptation goods ([Yahoo Finance/PRNewswire](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/over-one-dozen-guaranteed-income-170300695.html)).

### 4.2 The Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend: universal, permanent, and small

Alaska is the only jurisdiction in the developed world operating something structurally close to a true UBI: since 1982, every resident has received an annual dividend from oil-revenue investment returns, typically **$1,000–2,000 per person**. Jones and Marinescu's synthetic-control study in *AEJ: Economic Policy* (2022) found the dividend had **no effect on overall employment** but **increased part-time work by 1.8 percentage points (about 17%)**, with the demand stimulus to non-tradable sectors offsetting the modest labour-supply reduction in general equilibrium ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190299)).

The Alaskan case carries two distinct long-term lessons. First, at small scale, universality appears compatible with a stable labour market — forty years of dividends produced no visible work exodus, consistent with Marinescu's earlier synthesis that a 10% income increase via unconditional cash reduces labour supply only about 1% ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190299)). Second, the dividend's political economy is double-edged: it is the most popular programme in the state and effectively untouchable, but its financing is resource rents, not broad taxation — so it demonstrates political sustainability without demonstrating fiscal transferability to tax-financed systems.

### 4.3 OpenResearch's Unconditional Cash Transfer study (2020–2023): the largest and most rigorous

The ORUS study — now published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* — is the most consequential U.S. experiment: **1,000 low-income adults (aged 21–40, household income at or below 300% of the federal poverty line) in Illinois and Texas received $1,000 per month for three years**, with 2,000 controls receiving $50 ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719), [QJE](https://academic.oup.com/qje/advance-article/doi/10.1093/qje/qjag042/8761769)). Its labour-market findings are unambiguous and modestly negative: **labour-force participation fell 2.0 percentage points** by survey measure (−4.1 points pooling unemployment-insurance records), hours worked fell by **1.3–1.4 per week**, non-transfer income fell by **$1,500–1,900 per year**, and unemployment spells lengthened by **1.1 months** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719)).

Equally important is what did *not* improve: the study found **no significant gains in job quality, no meaningful human-capital investment, and subjective-wellbeing improvements that appeared in year one and then reverted toward baseline** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719)). A companion health paper (Miller et al., 2024) found **no improvements in physical health** and mental-health effects that were null after the first year ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719)). Recipients' largest time-use gain was **leisure** — a result supporters read as valuable freedom and critics as confirmation of work disincentives at scale. Either way, ORUS supplies the best-identified estimate of what a $12,000-per-year unconditional floor does to prime-age low-income workers, and it is a small, real, negative labour-supply effect paired with transient wellbeing gains.

### 4.4 The wider U.S. pilot wave and the first comprehensive meta-analysis

Beyond the flagship studies, the post-2019 U.S. landscape includes over a hundred municipally sponsored pilots and targeted RCTs such as Baby's First Years, which randomized mothers to **$333 versus $20 per month** and found higher mid/high-frequency brain activity (EEG) in infants at age one — among the first causal evidence that cash can affect early childhood neurodevelopment — though cognitive-outcome differences had faded to null by age four ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2115649119)). Corinth and Mayhew's 2026 meta-review for the American Enterprise Institute catalogued **122 pilots across 33 states and DC, spending $481.4 million on 40,921 recipients**; across the **30 RCTs reporting employment outcomes, the mean effect was +0.8 percentage points**, but the **four largest and best-powered pilots averaged −3.2 points**, implying an income elasticity of labour supply around −0.18 ([AEI](https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/what-can-we-learn-from-guaranteed-basic-income-pilots-in-the-united-states-evidence-on-employment-effects/)).

That divergence — small pilots mildly positive, large pilots negative — is one of the most important patterns in this report. Small samples underpower the detection of small negative effects and are vulnerable to selection and site effects; as statistical power rises toward ORUS scale, the estimated employment effect converges on **a small negative number**, squarely consistent with the neoclassical income effect predicted by Hoynes and Rothstein and with Marinescu's −1%-per-10% synthesis ([AEI](https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/what-can-we-learn-from-guaranteed-basic-income-pilots-in-the-united-states-evidence-on-employment-effects/), [Conversable Economist](https://conversableeconomist.com/2019/09/10/universal-basic-income-combined-with-what-else/)). The U.S. evidence, taken as a whole, says cash buys security and modestly reduces work effort — and that the trade-off is real rather than rhetorical.

## 5. Cross-Country Comparison

### 5.1 Design parameters

The three national experiences differ so profoundly in design that treating them as replicates of one experiment would be a category error. Finland tested the **incentive margin** (removing benefit withdrawal) at replacement-level generosity on the unemployed; Ontario tested the **income margin** at near-poverty-line generosity on the working-age poor; the U.S. studies tested everything from $500/month (Stockton) to $1,000/month (ORUS), while Alaska tests a small universal dividend in perpetuity. The table below aggregates the core parameters.

| Experiment | Jurisdiction / period | Transfer | Target population | Design | Key evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basic Income Experiment | Finland, 2017–2018 | **€560/month**, tax-free, unconditional | 2,000 unemployed, aged 25–58 | National RCT, ~173,000-person control pool | Verho et al. 2022, *AEJ: Policy* ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20200143)) |
| Ontario Basic Income Pilot | Canada, 2017–2019 (cancelled) | **C$16,989/yr** single; 50% clawback | ~4,000 low-income adults 18–64 | RCT intended; destroyed by cancellation | Post-hoc surveys ([McMaster](https://labourstudies.mcmaster.ca/app/uploads/2024/06/southern-ontarios-basic-income-experience.pdf)) |
| Mincome (Dauphin) | Canada, 1974–1979 | NIT-style guarantee | Whole town (saturation) | Saturation site vs. matched controls | Forget 2011; Calnitsky & Latner 2017 ([Progress.org](https://www.progress.org/wiki/forget-mincome-town-with-no-poverty/)) |
| Stockton SEED | U.S., 2019–2021 | **$500/month** × 24 months | 131 low-income residents | RCT, 200 controls | West & Castro 2023 ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160253/)) |
| Alaska PFD | U.S., 1982–present | **~$1,000–2,000/yr**, universal | All residents | Synthetic control | Jones & Marinescu 2022 ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190299)) |
| Unconditional Cash Transfer (ORUS) | U.S., 2020–2023 | **$1,000/month** × 36 months | 1,000 low-income adults 21–40 | RCT, 2,000 controls | Vivalt et al., *QJE* ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719)) |

Two design observations carry analytical weight. First, **generosity relative to existing benefits** — not the headline dollar figure — determines the pure income effect: Finland's €560 mostly replaced benefits recipients already had (small income effect, clean incentive effect), while Ontario and ORUS added genuinely new money on top (larger income effect, visible labour-supply response) ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20200143), [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719)). Second, only Dauphin tested **saturation**, and only Alaska tested **permanence** — meaning the two margins that matter most for long-run national consequences, community general equilibrium and intertemporal behaviour, each rest on a single study.

### 5.2 Outcome comparison

Across every experiment, the outcome pattern is remarkably stable: **wellbeing and security improve; employment moves little; health gains concentrate on mental rather than physical outcomes**. ### 5.3 Synthesis: what "effectiveness" means across the three countries

Judged as an **anti-poverty and wellbeing instrument**, the interventions were effective in all three countries by remarkably consistent margins. The Campbell systematic review — the highest rung of the evidence ladder — pooled the global experimental literature and found unconditional cash **reduced food insecurity (SMD −0.41 to −0.57) and mental distress (about −0.25 SD)**, improved life satisfaction in two of three studies reporting it, and produced **no general physical-health effect** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). The three national experiences sit squarely inside that envelope.

Judged as a **labour-market intervention**, effectiveness is genuinely contested. The defensible synthesis is: unconditional cash produces a **small negative pure income effect on labour supply** (ORUS: −2 pp participation; Marinescu's synthesis: ~−1% per 10% income rise; AEI meta-analysis of the largest pilots: −3.2 pp) that can be **offset or reversed at small scale or when conditionality removal dominates** (Stockton; Finland's subgroups; Alaska's part-time shift) ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719), [AEI](https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/what-can-we-learn-from-guaranteed-basic-income-pilots-in-the-united-states-evidence-on-employment-effects/)). Neither the advocates' "cash unleashes work" nor the critics' "cash destroys work" survives contact with the full evidence base; what survives is a small, real trade-off whose social cost depends on the fiscal question examined next.

## 6. Long-Term Consequences and the Expert Debate

### 6.1 Fiscal sustainability: the arithmetic that pilots cannot escape

The single most consequential long-term fact about UBI is its scale. The chart below places the pilot budgets beside credible national costings; the **four-to-five-order-of-magnitude gap** between what has been tested and what would be built is the central measurement problem of the entire field.

Fiscal scale: pilot budgets versus national UBI costings

Hoynes and Rothstein calculate that paying **$12,000 per adult per year in the United States would cost roughly $3 trillion annually — about 75% of total 2017 federal expenditures** — making clear that any realistic UBI must either be far smaller, be phased out with income (converting it into a negative income tax with its own marginal tax rates), or displace large parts of the existing welfare state ([Conversable Economist](https://conversableeconomist.com/2019/09/10/universal-basic-income-combined-with-what-else/)). Kearney and Mogstad similarly note that even a $10,000 dividend would exceed half the federal budget, and argue the fiscal space required would crowd out investments (early childhood, infrastructure) with stronger evidence bases ([Economic Strategy Group](https://www.economicstrategygroup.org/publication/universal-basic-income-ubi-as-a-policy-response-to-current-challenges/)). Canada's PBO arithmetic is more tractable precisely because the modelled programme is income-tested: gross costs of **C$85–107 billion** fall to a fraction of that net of taxes recovered and programmes replaced, with behavioural costs of only **C$3–5 billion per year** ([PBO](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2122-001-S--distributional-fiscal-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income--analyse-financiere-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale), [PBO](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)).

### 6.2 Distribution: who gains depends entirely on the financing

The expert critique that has aged best is Robert Greenstein's: **a UBI financed by cutting existing means-tested programmes redistributes upward**, because universality sends most of the money to non-poor households while the cuts fall on the poor ([Basic Income Earth Network](https://basicincome.org/news/2016/08/us-poverty-expert-robert-greensteins-case-bi/)). The OECD's microsimulation of Finland quantified the mechanism: a budget-neutral basic income replacing existing benefits **raises poverty and inequality in an already-generous welfare state**, since the flat payment cannot match targeted benefits at the bottom ([Banca d'Italia/OECD](https://www.bancaditalia.it/pubblicazioni/altri-atti-convegni/2018-workshop-public-finance/2-Paralliussen-paper.pdf?language_id=1)). The IMF's parallel analysis reached the same structural conclusion: whether a UBI reduces poverty is a financing question, not a design slogan ([Banca d'Italia/OECD](https://www.bancaditalia.it/pubblicazioni/altri-atti-convegni/2018-workshop-public-finance/2-Paralliussen-paper.pdf?language_id=1)).

The Canadian evidence shows the optimistic version of the same theorem: the PBO finds the **bottom quintile's disposable income rises 17.5%** and poverty falls by a third to a half even after offsetting programme cuts — but those gains are financed by eliminating credits and benefits whose loss is concentrated in the second and third deciles, producing a programme that helps the poorest while squeezing the near-poor ([PBO](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2122-001-S--distributional-fiscal-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income--analyse-financiere-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale)). The long-run distributional consequence of UBI is therefore not a fact to be discovered but a **choice to be legislated** — a point on which the OECD, IMF, CBPP, and PBO analyses, despite their ideological spread, substantively agree.

### 6.3 Macroeconomic structure: what the models say about permanent UBI

Because no pilot can test permanence and financing simultaneously, the long-run labour-market question falls to structural models. Luduvice's 2024 *Journal of Monetary Economics* analysis, using an overlapping-generations model calibrated to the U.S., delivers the cleanest statement of the financing trade-off: an **expenditure-neutral UBI** (funded by consolidating existing transfers) modestly **raises output and employment** and yields a welfare gain equivalent to **2.8% of consumption**; a **$1,000-per-month UBI funded by consumption taxes requires a 22–24 percentage-point tax increase**, contracts output and employment, and delivers its welfare gain (+3.9% CEV) only by redistributing toward low-wealth households at the cost of aggregate activity ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304393224000680)).

Two model-independent long-term risks deserve emphasis. First, **wage and price feedback**: Alaska is the only evidence on general equilibrium, and it shows labour-demand stimulus offsetting supply reductions in non-tradables — but at $1,500 a year, not $12,000 ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190299)). Second, **indexation and erosion**: a UBI set in nominal politics can be quietly shrunk by inflation, as Alaska's dividend debates illustrate, converting an income floor into a symbolic cheque. The ORUS finding that wellbeing gains reverted after year one warns of a parallel *psychological* adaptation risk: transfers normalize, and permanent effects may require permanent escalation ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719)).

### 6.4 Political economy: pilots as policy instruments

The Canadian and Finnish experiences converge on a sober institutional lesson: **pilots are politically cheap to start and equally cheap to kill**. Ontario's cancellation mid-experiment — after participants had enrolled in courses, leased apartments, and left shelter situations on the strength of three promised years — imposed real harms and generated a research ethics debate that will shadow any future government proposing experimentation on vulnerable populations ([De Gruyter](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/bis-2021-0021/html)). Finland's government, having legislated a fixed two-year window, simply let the experiment lapse, and the policy debate moved on to activation requirements — the opposite direction ([Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13882627221104501)).

The long-run political consequences also include a demonstration effect operating in the other direction: the U.S. mayoral pilots normalized unconditional cash in municipal politics — 122 pilots, 40,921 recipients, $481.4 million spent — creating a durable policy constituency and a research infrastructure that did not exist before 2019 ([AEI](https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/what-can-we-learn-from-guaranteed-basic-income-pilots-in-the-united-states-evidence-on-employment-effects/)). Widerquist's structural warning completes the picture: the political success of pilots may be purchased at the price of **evidential irrelevance to the permanent universal programme they are taken to vindicate**, and the gap between what pilots show and what advocates claim remains the field's defining tension ([Ralph Bunche Institute](https://ralphbuncheinstitute.org/where-is-the-ubi-movement-at-right-now-with-karl-widerquist/)).

## 7. Methodological Caveats and the Limits of the Evidence

The evidence base is thinner than its public profile suggests. The Campbell review's verdict — **25 of 27 included studies at high risk of bias** — reflects structural problems: self-reported outcomes with low response rates (Finland's wellbeing survey: 31% vs 20%), attrition, short horizons, and the near-impossibility of blinding cash transfers ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11180702/)). Ontario's evidence is post-hoc and self-selected; Mincome's headline health finding has been directly challenged on econometric grounds; and even ORUS, the gold standard, studies adults under 40 in two states at one point in the business cycle ([Progress.org](https://www.progress.org/wiki/forget-mincome-town-with-no-poverty/), [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719)).

Three identification problems bound every conclusion in this report. **Temporary versus permanent**: recipients who know payments end in two or three years will not reorganize careers, fertility, or location around them, so pilots almost certainly *understate* long-run behavioural response in both directions. **Partial versus universal**: dispersed pilots cannot capture community effects (Dauphin's social multipliers hint these are large) or employer-side wage adjustments. **Unfinanced versus financed**: pilot money is manna; real UBI money arrives with the taxes that fund it, and the sign of the net effect flips with the financing instrument, as Luduvice's neutral-versus-consumption-tax contrast shows ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304393224000680), [Progress.org](https://www.progress.org/wiki/forget-mincome-town-with-no-poverty/)).

Publication and salience biases cut in both directions. Small, positive, movement-affiliated pilots (Stockton) receive press coverage vastly disproportionate to their statistical weight, while the best-powered result in the literature (ORUS's −2 pp) arrived years after the narrative had congealed; conversely, single null or negative results are sometimes elevated into general verdicts by critics, although the AEI meta-analysis shows the apparent contradiction between "positive pilots" and "negative big pilots" is exactly what statistical power predicts ([AEI](https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/what-can-we-learn-from-guaranteed-basic-income-pilots-in-the-united-states-evidence-on-employment-effects/)). The honest epistemic posture, a decade into the modern experimental wave, is **confidence about household-level wellbeing effects, modest confidence about small labour-supply effects, and humility about everything at national scale**.

## 8. Conclusion

Across Finland, Canada, and the United States, the post-2015 experimental record supports three conclusions with reasonable confidence. **First, unconditional cash works as a wellbeing and security instrument**: mental strain, economic stress, food insecurity, and income volatility improved in essentially every design that measured them, with effect sizes large enough to matter for health-policy accounting. **Second, the employment effects are small and sign-variable**: near zero in Finland and Alaska, positive in small targeted pilots, and modestly negative (−2 to −3 pp participation) in the largest, best-powered studies — a pattern consistent with standard income effects plus incentive-margin gains, not with either utopian or dystopian predictions. **Third, the decisive long-term questions — fiscal sustainability, financing incidence, general-equilibrium wage effects, and political durability — are exactly the ones pilots cannot answer**, and on those questions the expert literature delivers its own clear verdict: at UBI scale, financing choices dominate design choices, budget-neutral versions of full UBI tend to raise poverty in generous welfare states, and targeted guaranteed-income designs deliver most of the poverty reduction at a small fraction of the fiscal cost ([Banca d'Italia/OECD](https://www.bancaditalia.it/pubblicazioni/altri-atti-convegni/2018-workshop-public-finance/2-Paralliussen-paper.pdf?language_id=1), [PBO](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)).

The most defensible reading of the decade's evidence is therefore neither that "UBI works" nor that "UBI fails," but that the experiments have progressively narrowed the disagreement to its irreducible core: **societies can buy a measurable amount of security, dignity, and mental health with unconditional cash, at the price of a small reduction in labour supply and a very large fiscal commitment**, and whether that trade is worth making is a political choice about values and financing — not a question any further two-year pilot is likely to settle. The Finland, Ontario, Stockton, and ORUS results will endure as the empirical foundation of that choice; the cancelled Ontario pilot will endure as its standing warning.
